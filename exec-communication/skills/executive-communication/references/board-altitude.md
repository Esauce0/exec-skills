# Board altitude

Distilled mainly from the ram-charan dossier (Boards That Deliver, Boards That Lead, Boards at Work), with Rumelt and the AI sources where noted.

## What a board governs

A board governs the few things only a board can: the company's central idea and strategy, the CEO and succession, the talent pool at the top, performance and health, and material risk. Anything outside those is operating detail unless a director asks.

## What boards need from a management presenter

- **Altitude.** What the item means for the company, translated from what the team did (see the table below).
- **Clear choices.** The options considered, the recommendation, and the assumptions that would reverse it.
- **Named risks.** The downside that could materially hurt the company, the mitigations, and the risk appetite implied.
- **The talent behind the plan.** Who leads it and whether the bench is deep enough.
- **A few numbers, as trends.** Pick the handful that show which way the business is moving.
- **A short pre-read,** so the meeting becomes dialogue.
- **No surprises.** Bad news reaches directors between meetings, through the CEO or sponsor, before anyone raises it in the room.

A board appearance is also a succession read on the presenter. Directors are forming a view of whether this person could run more.

## The presenter is there on behalf of the CEO

For anyone below the executive team, the board slot belongs to the CEO or the sponsoring executive. Pre-wire the material with them. Align on what the board has already heard, what the CEO wants from the discussion, and what not to raise. Never contradict the CEO's framing in the room; raise disagreements beforehand.

## Translating operating detail to board altitude

| Operating detail | Board altitude |
|---|---|
| "We shipped 14 features in the assistant this quarter." | "Enterprise adoption of the assistant rose from 18% to 31% of seats, which de-risks the January renewal cohort." |
| "Latency improved 40%." | (Leave out, unless it unblocked a material deal or risk.) |
| "We're evaluating three model vendors." | "We're moving the largest AI workload to a cheaper provider in Q4, which lifts AI gross margin by about 9 points. Quality holds on our own evaluation set." |
| "Hallucination reports increased." | "We had a quality regression in two new AI skills, caught before most customers saw it. We've added a launch gate. Residual risk: medium, owned by <name>." |
| "The team is hiring ML engineers." | "The plan depends on three senior ML hires; two are closed, and the third is the main execution risk." |

## Briefing structure (after Charan, with Rumelt's kernel as the spine)

1. **Purpose line:** decision, input, or information? Which board area: strategy, risk, talent, performance?
2. **What changed:** the market, technology, or performance shift that makes this item live.
3. **Diagnosis:** the challenge in two sentences.
4. **Choices:** two or three options, the recommendation, what would reverse it.
5. **Risk:** material downside, mitigations, implied risk appetite. For AI: model behavior, regulatory, data rights, reputational, cost.
6. **Talent:** who leads, bench depth, gaps.
7. **Numbers:** the few health metrics, as trends.
8. **The ask:** the specific question for the board.

## AI at board altitude

Boards increasingly ask about AI. What they need is different from what executives need:
- How AI changes the company's competitive position and central idea.
- The economics: margin effect, investment required, the range of outcomes.
- Risk governance: tiers, launch gates, who owns quality, incidents and near-misses.
- Hype discipline: separate the industry-level debate from this company's unit economics.

## Q&A at board level

- Answer the question asked, directly, then stop.
- If you don't know, say so and commit to a date for the answer. Never guess at a board.
- If a director asks for operating detail, give it concisely; don't volunteer more.
- Watch for the question behind the question (a director's worry about risk, the CEO, or the plan).
