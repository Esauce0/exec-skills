# Meeting Presence

## Purpose

Executives form much of their view of someone in live settings: how they open, how they handle being challenged, whether they make a call, whether they listen, whether the meeting ends better because they were there. Senior leaders weight gravitas (composure, decisiveness, candor, judgment) far above polish or appearance.

This skill prepares and debriefs those moments at the level of behavior, because a presence built on sounding impressive collapses the first time someone pushes on the substance.

## Use when

- Preparing for any consequential meeting with executives.
- Debriefing one.
- Feedback mentions presence, confidence, gravitas, "reads junior in the room," or "talks too much."
- The user will present at staff, a leadership offsite, a QBR, or a board slot.

## Don't use when

- The task is written material (use `executive-communication`), though the two are usually prepared together.

## Standards

Everything this skill produces follows the three exec-core standards. If exec-core isn't loaded, apply them as stated here:
- **Evidence:** separate what happened from inference; label any claim about another person's view or motive as a hypothesis with confidence and a falsifier; never state it as fact.
- **Posture:** when the user's behavior is part of the problem, fix the behavior before the wording.
- **Prose:** answer first, recommendation over options, owners and dates, no filler.

## Core doctrine

1. **Gravitas outweighs communication and appearance** [Hewlett]. In her research, senior leaders weighted gravitas far above communication, and appearance least. Gravitas shows up as composure under challenge, making a call, speaking truth upward with evidence, reading the room, and articulating where things are going.
2. **Blunders are asymmetric** [Hewlett]. One visible lapse (losing composure, flip-flopping, overclaiming, not knowing your own numbers) can outweigh months of good presence. Pre-mortem high-visibility moments.
3. **Open with the claim** [Minto; Pfeffer; Larson]. The first 30 seconds carry the answer or the recommendation, without an apology for taking time or a context-first preamble.
4. **Present to learn their view** [Larson]. Expect interruption. When an executive pushes back with a specific suggestion, find the concern underneath and address that.
5. **Know the meeting's purpose and the decider** [Grove; Scott]. Is this meeting for listening, clarifying, debating, deciding, or persuading? Behaving as if it's a decision meeting when it's a debate (or the reverse) is a common failure.
6. **Stop adding value** [Goldsmith]. Adding a small improvement to someone else's idea, winning every point, starting with "no," "but," or "however," and showing how smart you are all cost more with each level. Silence after a good answer is a skill.
7. **Put the biggest problem on the table** [Campbell]. Name the unspoken issue early, framed as a shared problem. Executives trust the person who makes a meeting more honest without making it more heated.
8. **Voice, then commit** [Campbell; Grove; Bezos]. Dissent once, clearly, with evidence. After the decision, support it, including with your own team.
9. **Your words get amplified as you rise** [Watkins]. Offhand comments in leadership forums become directives. Speak with that in mind.
10. **Trust sequence for hard conversations** [Maister]. Engage, listen, frame, envision, commit. With a new or skeptical executive, don't jump to the recommendation before they feel heard.
11. **Collect views before the room; speak last when you decide** [Duke; Campbell]. When the user runs the meeting, independent input first prevents the loudest voice from anchoring.
12. **Close with decisions, owners, dates** [Charan]. When the user drove the meeting or owns the outcome, they send the written follow-up within 24 hours. Being the default recap-sender for other people's meetings reads as coordination.
13. **Presence standards aren't neutral** [Hewlett; Ibarra]. Presence feedback can mean a real capability gap (composure, decisiveness, clarity) or pressure to conform to the incumbent style. Triage it; the user chooses what to adapt.

## Evidence to gather

- People files for every attendee, and their `models/reputation.md` rows.
- Prior `meetings/` files with them, especially the debriefs (the user's recurring patterns live there).
- Product Brain stakeholder files and recent decisions on the topic.
- The invite and any pre-read.

## Reasoning procedure (preparation)

1. **Purpose and decider:** what is this meeting for, and who decides? What stage of listen/clarify/debate/decide/persuade is it at?
2. **Attendees:** from their `people/` files: what each cares about (with evidence), their pressures, how they react to challenge, their current trust tier with the user.
3. **The user's objective,** stated so it can be observed by the end of the meeting.
4. **The opening:** the answer or recommendation in two sentences.
5. **What the user will own in the room,** and what they'll decide vs ask.
6. **Likely challenges** and the short answers. Include "I don't know; I'll have it by Thursday" where true.
7. **The one question** the user will ask that shows next-level thinking (a cross-functional implication, the risk nobody named, the company-level tradeoff).
8. **What to avoid,** specific to this user's patterns (from past debriefs: over-explaining, defending, adding value, going technical).
9. **Pre-wiring:** who needs to hear it first.
10. **Blunder pre-mortem:** what could read as losing composure, flip-flopping, overclaiming, or not knowing the numbers?

## Reasoning procedure (debrief)

1. Observations first: what happened, who said what, what was decided.
2. Objective met? Evidence.
3. Signals by attendee, classified with `evidence-discipline` (tone alone is not evidence).
4. Posture shown (`executive-posture`).
5. Habits observed (Goldsmith list; the user's own recurring patterns), including offhand comments that others could take as directives.
6. What to do differently, one or two items.
7. Follow-up sent?

## Diagnostic questions

- What is this meeting for: listening, clarifying, debating, deciding, or persuading?
- Who decides?
- What will you say in the first 30 seconds?
- What's the hardest question you could get, and what's your short answer?
- Which habit showed up in your last three debriefs?
- What unspoken issue would make this meeting more honest if named?

## Failure modes

- Context-first openings that lose the room before the point arrives.
- Defending instead of inquiring when challenged.
- Answering the question asked, then three more nobody asked.
- Relitigating a decision in the meeting after it's made, or afterward in the hallway.
- Silence in cross-functional forums: never offering a view outside one's own area.
- Performing confidence without a recommendation behind it.
- Treating conformity feedback as capability feedback, or the reverse.

## Tensions in the doctrine

- **Conform vs be yourself.** Hewlett's research describes what executives currently reward; Ibarra warns that rigid authenticity is a trap, while also noting presence standards are stricter for some groups. The user decides what to adapt, with the tradeoff stated.
- **Project power vs stay curious.** Pfeffer: project confidence and power. Larson and Maister: listen and learn the other person's view. The user can be confident about the recommendation and still curious about the objection.
- **Speak up vs add too much value.** Pfeffer and Doshi push under-assertive people to take up more space; Goldsmith warns successful people talk too much. Which cure applies is an evidence question from the debriefs.
