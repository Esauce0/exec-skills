# Executive Posture

## Purpose

Senior people infer your level from behavior long before they read a ladder document. What you escalate, what you decide without asking, what you talk about, which problems you treat as yours: each of these signals a posture. This skill reads the signal, finds the behavior producing it, and changes the behavior.

It exists to prevent the most common failure of career coaching: making someone *sound* more senior. Executive theater (senior vocabulary over junior behavior) is detected quickly by real executives and costs more trust than plain junior writing.

## Use when

- Reviewing any written communication to senior people.
- Planning or debriefing a meeting with executives.
- The user describes how they handled a situation.
- Diagnosing "they still treat me like a PM" or "I'm not being taken seriously."
- Weekly and monthly reviews (posture across many interactions is one of the best leading indicators of readiness).

## Don't use when

- The user needs the substance fixed first (a wrong recommendation delivered in perfect posture is still wrong).

## The seven postures

A posture is how you are showing up in a given moment, and anyone can occupy any posture whatever their title. Full markers for each: `references/posture-ladder.md`.

| Posture | Owns | Characteristic question | Default horizon |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coordinator** | The process: meetings, follow-ups, status | "Is everyone informed?" | This week |
| **Project manager** | Delivery of a defined thing | "Will it ship on time?" | This quarter's plan |
| **Subject-matter expert** | The right answer in a domain | "What's technically correct?" | The question in front of them |
| **Product manager** | A product's outcomes | "Are we solving the right problem for users, and is it working?" | 1-2 quarters |
| **Product leader** | Product capability in an area: strategy, topology, the quality of others' decisions | "Are my teams making good decisions, and do they know why?" | 2-4 quarters |
| **Organizational leader** | An organization's outcomes and its operating system, and a seat on a peer leadership team | "Is the organization set up to win, and are we making the right cross-functional tradeoffs?" | 1-3 years |
| **Executive** | A share of the company's fate: capital and people allocated across competing priorities, direction under ambiguity, risk | "What should the company do, and what are we not doing?" | Multi-year |

Evidence-entry tags: `posture:coordinator`, `posture:project-manager`, `posture:expert`, `posture:product-manager`, `posture:product-leader`, `posture:org-leader`, `posture:executive`.

Postures are not a strict ranking. Subject-matter expertise is a different axis: executives go deep deliberately, and an AI PM's technical depth is an asset. The failure is being *only* the expert: consulted for answers, never trusted with ownership.

## What produces the signal

The words are symptoms of these behaviors, which are what senior readers actually react to:

1. **Unit of ownership.** Task, project, domain, product outcome, product area, organization, enterprise outcome. What do you treat as yours?
2. **Decision posture.** Asks permission; presents options; recommends; decides and informs; sets the frameworks others decide within.
3. **Escalation pattern.** What gets sent upward relative to your actual authority. Escalating in-scope decisions is the single most common posture leak.
4. **Altitude of content.** Activity, then deliverables, then product outcomes, then business results, then enterprise tradeoffs.
5. **Relationship to ambiguity.** Waits for clarity; asks for clarity; creates clarity for others.
6. **Relationship to problems outside your lane.** Ignores them; reports them; proposes fixes; takes ownership through legitimate channels.
7. **Accountability language.** "We're blocked by" vs "I will" vs "The miss is mine; here's what changes."
8. **Where attention goes.** The details of execution vs the system that produces execution.
9. **Treatment of peers.** Rivals for resources vs a first team solving the company's problems together (Lencioni).
10. **Time horizon** of what you raise unprompted.

## Doctrine basis

The seven postures and the ten behaviors that produce the signal are this system's synthesis. They draw on:

- Each level changes skills, time allocation, and work values, and people fail by keeping the old values [Charan].
- The seven shifts from functional to enterprise leader, including analyst to integrator and warrior to diplomat [Watkins].
- Levels of product work, and the identity lag of new leaders who keep proving they can still do the old job [Doshi].
- Deciding who decides and keeping problem-solving local [Hughes Johnson].
- Latitude is information about how someone's judgment is perceived [McCord].
- The peer leadership team as the first team [Lencioni].

## Reasoning procedure

1. **Establish the situation's demand.** Who is the audience, what are the stakes, and what is the user's actual scope and authority? Read `self/current-role.md` (decision rights) from the Career Brain if available.
2. **Identify the posture exhibited,** citing specific markers from the artifact or account (quote the phrase, name the decision, point to the escalation).
3. **Identify the target posture.** Usually that means thinking one level above current title, acting within current authority, and being explicit about ownership. The right target is sometimes a lower posture: a VP in a live incident may rightly act as a project manager for an hour.
4. **Find the behavior producing the gap.** Map each marker to one of the ten behaviors above. Look for the root: usually an avoided decision, an unowned problem, a missing recommendation, or content at the wrong altitude.
5. **Prescribe the behavior change first.** "Make the call on rollout percentage yourself" before "rephrase the ask."
6. **Then fix the expression** using `executive-prose`.
7. **Check for overreach.** Does the target behavior claim authority the user doesn't have, step on a peer's or the boss's ownership, or perform seniority to an audience that knows the user's scope? If so, pull it back: recommend at the next level, decide at your own.
8. **Name the pattern if it's recurring.** One escalation leak is a note; the same leak three weeks running is a development area to record in `models/readiness.md`.

## Interventions: weak vs strong

In each row below, the weak version edits words and the strong version names the behavior. More in `references/interventions.md`.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Rewrite this update to sound more executive." | "You're escalating three decisions that fall within your scope. Make the calls on the first two and ask Maria only for the one that needs her authority." |
| "Be more concise." | "Half this update reports meetings. Report state: what's decided, what's at risk, what you need." |
| "Sound more confident." | "You present three options and no recommendation. Pick one, say why, and say what it costs. That's what reads as confidence." |
| "Show more strategic thinking." | "You describe the feature's progress but not what it changes for the renewal risk the VP is worried about. Lead with that link." |
| "Speak up more in staff meetings." | "You raise only issues inside your product. Bring one cross-team tradeoff per month with a proposed resolution; that's the organizational-leader signal." |

## Failure modes

**The user's:**
- Escalating in-scope decisions to look collaborative or to avoid blame.
- Reporting activity (syncs, meetings, alignment) as progress.
- Staying in expert mode in executive rooms: answering the technical question perfectly and never offering a view on what the company should do.
- Coordinating a cross-functional problem instead of owning its resolution.
- Treating the boss as the audience for problems rather than for decisions and results.
- Performing seniority: strategic vocabulary, borrowed urgency, criticism of other teams, without the decisions to back it.

**The coach's:**
- Upgrading vocabulary instead of diagnosing behavior.
- Pushing the user to always occupy the highest posture, regardless of scope. That produces overreach.
- Missing that the user's scope genuinely doesn't include the decision (sometimes escalation is correct, and good judgment is recognizing it).

## Output

For a reviewed artifact or account:

```
Posture shown: <posture>, because <2-3 specific markers>.
Target: <posture>, given <audience, stakes, the user's actual authority>.
Underlying behavior: <the behavior producing the gap>.
Change: <the behavior change, concrete>.
Then the words: <rewrite or edits, per executive-prose>.
Overreach check: <none, or what to pull back>.
Pattern: <first occurrence, or recurring with links to prior evidence>.
```

Keep it short when the fix is small.

## Tensions in the doctrine

- **Operate at the next level vs stay in your lane.** Ibarra, Watkins, and Charan argue you must start doing the next job's work before the title arrives. Pfeffer puts the perceptions of those above you at the center, so next-level moves that make the boss look bad cost more than they earn; Campbell's team-first ethic cautions against self-promotion. The procedure's resolution: expand the scope of your *thinking* and *recommendations* freely; expand the scope of your *decisions* only through legitimate grants of authority.
- **Perception is part of the job.** Doshi treats "optics" as a legitimate layer of product work; Pfeffer goes further and treats perception management as central. Campbell and Slootman emphasize substance. The skill treats signal as real (senior people do infer level from posture) while refusing signal without substance.
