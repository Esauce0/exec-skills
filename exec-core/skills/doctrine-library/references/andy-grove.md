# Andrew S. Grove

## Why this source matters here
Grove was a founding-era Intel executive who became president (1979) and CEO (1987-1998). He ran the company through the 1985-86 exit from memory chips and the 1994 Pentium floating-point crisis. High Output Management (1983, revised 1995) was written for middle managers. Grove counts individual contributors who influence others through expertise as middle managers ("know-how managers"), which describes a senior PM almost exactly. Only the Paranoid Survive (1996) moves up a level, to how companies and careers get caught by fundamental shifts. The doctrine comes from a large, engineering-led, manufacturing-heavy company with a strong culture of data and open debate. Its production metaphors carry over to software and AI product work only partly: outputs there are harder to count and cycle times are shorter.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. You are measured by the output of your organization plus the neighboring organizations you influence
- **Claim:** A manager's output is not their own work. It is the output of the unit they run plus the output of adjacent units they influence. Activity, effort and artifacts only count through their effect on that combined output.
- **Source:** High Output Management, part 1, on managerial leverage.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user reports what they did (a PRD, a deck, a launch), the coach asks what changed in the output of their product area and of the adjacent teams (platform, sales, support, data science) because of it. For an IC PM, the "neighboring organizations" term is the whole job and the main evidence for Director readiness (scope-expansion, next-level-readiness). The coach reframes weekly updates and promotion cases from "I shipped" to "these teams produced X, and I changed Y about how they work."
- **Boundary conditions:** Output across teams is hard to attribute. The frame can tip into claiming credit for others' work, which damages executive trust. Use it to choose work, and state contribution modestly in anything written.

### D2. Choose activities by leverage, and change the mix before you try to work harder
- **Claim:** Output rises in three ways: do activities faster, raise the leverage of each activity, or shift time toward higher-leverage activities. An activity is high-leverage when it affects many people, when a brief well-aimed action changes someone's work or behavior for a long time, or when one person supplies a unique piece of knowledge to a large group at the moment it matters.
- **Source:** High Output Management, part 1.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach runs a leverage audit on the user's calendar (see Frameworks) and asks which hours changed what other people did for weeks afterward. Typical PM-to-Director shifts: from writing specs to setting the decision frame others write within; from attending every review to designing the review; from answering questions to publishing the answer once. (managerial-leverage)
- **Boundary conditions:** Leverage assumes other people exist to multiply through. A PM on a thin team may still need to do the work directly, and doing it well is itself a unique-knowledge activity. Leverage is not delegation for its own sake.

### D3. Negative leverage is as real as positive leverage, and it grows with seniority
- **Claim:** A senior person's small actions can reduce output across many people. Meddling teaches subordinates to stop taking initiative. Indecision or waffling freezes everyone waiting on the call. Arriving unprepared wastes the time of a whole room.
- **Source:** High Output Management, discussion of leverage and meddling.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach looks for the user's negative-leverage habits, not just missing positive ones: re-opening decisions, late reviews that block teams, rewriting other people's work, vague asks that send people off to guess. At Director level each of these costs more because more people are waiting. (executive-posture, managerial-leverage)
- **Boundary conditions:** Stepping in is sometimes correct (low task-relevant maturity, crisis, quality slipping). What makes it negative is doing it without a reason you can state or without handing control back afterward.

### D4. Know-how managers are managers: influential ICs are judged on managerial output
- **Claim:** Middle management includes people who supervise no one but whose knowledge shapes others' work: specialists, internal consultants, planners. Their job is to gather and disseminate know-how and to nudge decisions, the same activities as supervisors. Grove suggests a know-how manager should serve roughly as many internal "customers" as a supervisor would have direct reports, or risk becoming a meddler.
- **Source:** High Output Management, sections on middle managers and know-how managers.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach does not treat "no direct reports" as "not yet managerial." It asks whom the user's knowledge serves, how many teams depend on it, and whether it reaches them at decision time. This is the bridge that lets an IC PM build Director evidence before the title. (next-level-readiness, scope-expansion)
- **Boundary conditions:** Some companies' ladders require people management for Director. Know-how leverage can prove readiness for scope but does not replace experience managing people when the next role needs it (see Tensions: Charan).

### D5. Information gathering and nudging are the base of the job; formal decisions are a small share
- **Claim:** Most managerial work is gathering information, passing it on, and nudging people and decisions in a direction. The most useful information comes from quick, often casual, verbal exchanges and from visiting where the work happens. Written reports are worth more as a discipline for the writer than as a channel for the reader.
- **Source:** High Output Management, chapter on managerial activities (information gathering, "visits", nudging).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks where the user's information comes from and how fresh it is: customer calls, sales floor, support queues, eval dashboards, model-failure reviews. For executive posture, the coach separates nudging (a well-timed suggestion to the right person) from announcing. Senior leaders trust people who know things early (enterprise-thinking, executive-posture). Writing a weekly update is reframed as the user's own thinking discipline first.
- **Boundary conditions:** In remote or distributed organizations, casual exchanges have to be arranged on purpose. Heavy reliance on hallway information can also feed gossip and in-group politics.

### D6. Run decisions as a process: free discussion, clear decision, full support, and settle the frame first
- **Claim:** The ideal model has three stages: free discussion where status does not limit who speaks; a clear decision stated as a decision; full support from everyone, including those who argued against it. Support does not require agreement. Before starting, settle six questions: what decision is needed, by when, who decides, who is consulted, who ratifies or can veto, who must be informed. If the decision proves wrong, go back to discussion. Peers left alone tend to avoid taking positions (the "peer-group syndrome"). Intel added a slightly more senior person ("peer-plus-one") to force a call.
- **Source:** High Output Management, decision-making chapter.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user brings a stuck decision, the coach first asks the six questions, because most stalls come from an unsettled frame (unclear decider or veto holder), not from lack of analysis. The coach tests whether the user gives full support after losing an argument, or keeps litigating it in 1:1s and hallways. That behavior is watched closely in candidates for leadership teams. (decision-making, executive-posture)
- **Boundary conditions:** Assumes a culture where dissent early is safe and a decider is legitimate. Full support does not cover ethical, legal or safety objections, which should be escalated rather than swallowed. Speed and reversibility are not built into this model (see Tensions: Bezos/Amazon).

### D7. Match supervision to task-relevant maturity; delegation without follow-through is abdication
- **Claim:** How closely to manage a person depends on their task-relevant maturity (TRM): their experience, training and drive on this specific task, not in general. Low TRM calls for structure (what, when, how). Medium TRM calls for two-way discussion and support. High TRM calls for agreeing objectives and monitoring lightly. A senior person has low TRM on a new kind of task. Delegating without monitoring is abdication, and the delegator remains responsible.
- **Source:** High Output Management, TRM chapters (Grove adapts situational leadership).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks whether the user manages everyone the same way, or micromanages experts and under-supports people new to a task. It also turns TRM inward: the user moving into AI product leadership, P&L or people management has low TRM there, and should ask their own manager for closer support rather than hide it. (managerial-leverage, talent, managing-up)
- **Boundary conditions:** TRM is a practitioner heuristic. The situational-leadership research it draws on has weak empirical support. Use it as a prompt to adjust supervision task by task, not as a scoring system for people.

### D8. The one-on-one belongs to the subordinate
- **Claim:** The 1:1 is the subordinate's meeting. They bring the agenda and an outline, and the supervisor's role is to learn and to coach. Frequency depends on the subordinate's TRM and how fast the work is changing. Grove treated it as one of the highest-leverage hours a manager spends.
- **Source:** High Output Management, meetings chapter.
- **Changes coaching how:** Applied upward, this is a managing-up rule: the user owns their 1:1 with their manager. The coach asks for the agenda the user sent in advance: indicators, problems, decisions needed, early warnings. A user who arrives without an agenda is handing their best channel to senior attention back to the manager. (managing-up, meeting-presence)
- **Boundary conditions:** Some managers run 1:1s their own way. Adapt to them, but still bring a written agenda. Grove's longer, less frequent format assumes stable work, and fast-moving AI teams may need shorter, more frequent syncs.

### D9. Meetings are the medium of managerial work, and each type has its own discipline
- **Claim:** Managerial work happens mostly in meetings, so the question is how to run them, not how to avoid them. Process-oriented meetings (1:1s, staff meetings, operations reviews) are recurring and exchange information. Mission-oriented meetings are called to produce a decision or output. They need a stated objective, a person accountable for the outcome, a small attendance, and written minutes with decisions and actions. Grove treats a manager spending a large share of time (he cites about a quarter) in ad hoc mission meetings as a sign the organization is poorly designed.
- **Source:** High Output Management, meetings chapter.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user to classify their recurring meetings and check that each mission meeting ends with a written decision and owner. Meeting presence is judged by output: did the meeting the user called produce a decision, and did the user's contribution in others' meetings change the result? A calendar full of ad hoc "syncs" is treated as a sign of unclear decision rights to fix (organizational-design), not as a time-management problem. (meeting-presence)
- **Boundary conditions:** Written-first cultures (Amazon, Stripe) move some of this work into documents, and the meeting becomes the discussion of the document.

### D10. Planning is today's action for tomorrow's output; measure output with paired indicators
- **Claim:** Planning has three steps: estimate what the environment will demand, assess what current operations will deliver, and decide what to do to close the gap. The output of planning is the set of actions taken today. Management by objectives answers two questions: where am I going (objective), and how will I pace myself to know I am getting there (key results). Indicators should count output, not activity, and should come in pairs so that an effect and its counter-effect are both measured (for example speed and quality, or inventory and shortages). Leading indicators give earlier warning than lagging ones.
- **Source:** High Output Management, planning, MBO and indicators sections.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks what the user is doing this week because of next year's plan. A plan that changes nothing today is not a plan. For AI products, the coach insists on paired indicators: adoption paired with quality or harm rates, latency with cost, automation rate with escalation rate. It flags any single metric the user reports without its counterweight. (enterprise-thinking, evidence-discipline)
- **Boundary conditions:** Objectives and key results drift into activity lists when outcomes are hard to measure. Too many indicators produce noise. Grove's manufacturing context had countable outputs that research-heavy or early-discovery work lacks.

### D11. Strategic inflection points are seen first in the middle; find your Cassandras
- **Claim:** A strategic inflection point is a time when a business's fundamentals are about to change, driven by a "10X" change in one of the forces acting on it (competitors, suppliers, customers, potential entrants, substitutes, complementors). People close to customers and competitors, usually middle managers, sense it before the executive floor does. Telling signal from noise takes broad and intense debate, not analysis by one person. Warning signs include "strategic dissonance" (what leaders say and what the organization actually does diverge), no clear answer when asked which single competitor the company would eliminate if it could (his "silver bullet" test), and a key complementor losing importance. Intel's middle managers had already moved most fab capacity to microprocessors before top management formally left memories.
- **Source:** Only the Paranoid Survive, preface and chs. 2, 5-6 (preview consulted); Commoncog case.
- **Changes coaching how:** This is the strongest enterprise-thinking license for someone in the middle. The coach asks what the user sees from customers, model capability shifts, pricing and competitors that leadership has not yet absorbed, and how to raise it as a well-argued Cassandra warning rather than as noise or complaint. It also asks where the org's resources are already moving regardless of the official strategy. For AI product leaders, model-capability jumps and changes in the cost curve are candidate 10X forces. (enterprise-thinking, executive-communication, ai-product-leadership)
- **Boundary conditions:** Most alarms are noise. A Cassandra who cries wolf often loses credibility. Grove's advice presumes leaders who invite debate, and the user should gauge how receptive the room is before escalating.

### D12. Let chaos reign, then rein in chaos, and be clear about what you will not pursue
- **Claim:** Getting through an inflection point needs two phases. First, loosen control and allow experiments and debate. Then commit to a clear direction and redeploy resources. At the late stage, the direction must name what the company will not pursue as well as what it will. Emotional attachment to the old business keeps leaders stuck even after the analysis is clear. Grove's device was to ask what a newly hired CEO would do, then do it themselves.
- **Source:** Only the Paranoid Survive, chs. 5, 7-8 (chapter framing verified in preview TOC); the Moore-Grove "why not do it ourselves" exchange.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user is stuck defending a legacy roadmap item, the coach asks the outsider question: if a new VP took over your area tomorrow, what would they stop? For executive communication, the coach checks that strategy memos state the explicit "not doing" list. (decision-making, executive-posture, executive-communication)
- **Boundary conditions:** Needs authority to reallocate resources. An IC can run experiments and propose the rein-in, but the explicit "not doing" call usually belongs to someone more senior. The user should not announce it before that person has made it.

### D13. Being technically right does not settle it when the rules have changed
- **Claim:** In the 1994 Pentium flaw, Intel handled the problem on statistics (an average spreadsheet user would hit it once in many thousands of years) and treated replacement as something to judge case by case. The public and a key customer rejected Intel's right to decide what was good enough. Intel reversed to replace every chip on request and took a $475 million write-off. Grove reads it as a 10X change in how customers saw the company, and he admits he was slow to see it.
- **Source:** Only the Paranoid Survive, ch. 1 (preview consulted); Fortune profile on his pride in the episode.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user defends a decision on data alone (an eval score, a low incident rate), the coach asks who now believes they have the right to judge it: customers, regulators, the press, an executive sponsor. This matters in AI products, where a statistically rare failure can become a trust event. It also separates a capability problem from a perception problem (attribution-analysis). (executive-posture, enterprise-thinking)
- **Boundary conditions:** Not an argument for giving in to every loud complaint. The lesson is to notice when the audience that judges quality has changed.

### D14. Your career is your business, and it has its own inflection points
- **Claim:** No employer owes anyone a career. Each person runs their career as a sole proprietor competing with others worldwide, and owns their skills and the timing of their moves. The same inflection-point logic applies to careers as to companies.
- **Source:** Only the Paranoid Survive, preface (consulted); paperback-edition career chapter (by reference).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach treats shifts in the AI market (new model capabilities, roles merging, PM work being automated) as possible career inflection points for the user. It asks what the user is doing now to be positioned on the far side, rather than waiting for sponsorship to carry them. (trajectory-model, next-level-readiness)
- **Boundary conditions:** Taken too far, this becomes permanent job-hunting that erodes current-role performance and trust. Sponsorship still matters. The frame is about ownership, not about being disloyal.

### D15. Performance review is among the highest-leverage things a manager does
- **Claim:** A review is task-relevant feedback whose purpose is to improve the person's performance. Assess output and also internal measures (the work that affects future output), and guard against rewarding visible activity over output. Deliver it plainly, listen, and keep your own ego out of it. Grove puts the most development effort into top performers, because improving them moves the group most.
- **Source:** High Output Management, performance appraisal chapter.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach uses the same lens on the user's own self-assessment and promotion packet: output first, internal measures second, activity last. For someone taking on reports, the coach asks how they will review others without rewarding busyness. (talent, next-level-readiness)
- **Boundary conditions:** Ratings and calibration systems vary widely. Grove's view that output can be measured holds better in operations than in early-stage product discovery.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Leverage audit (run on the last two weeks of calendar)
| Activity | People whose work it changed | How long the effect lasts | Unique knowledge supplied at decision time? | Leverage (high / medium / low / negative) | Action: keep, redesign, delegate, drop |
- Mark negative leverage explicitly: reviews that blocked a team, reopened decisions, rewrites of others' work.
- Target: move at least one recurring low-leverage block per month into a high-leverage form (a written decision frame, a reusable review template, a teaching session).
- Output check: name one number in a neighboring team's output that moved because of the user this quarter.

### Decision frame (before any contested decision)
1. What exactly is being decided (one sentence, options listed)?
2. By when, and what happens if it slips?
3. Who decides (one name)?
4. Who must be consulted before the decision?
5. Who can ratify or veto it?
6. Who must be told afterward, and by whom?
- Then run it: free discussion (collect the dissent before deciding), clear decision (in writing), full support (no relitigating in side channels), revisit only on new information.
- If peers are stuck, name the peer-plus-one who will force the call.

### Task-relevant maturity to supervision style
| TRM on this task | Style | What it looks like for a PM or Director |
| Low | Structured: what, when, how | Clear spec of outcome and steps, frequent check-ins, fast feedback |
| Medium | Two-way: discuss reasoning, support | Agree approach together, review key decisions, coach on trade-offs |
| High | Objectives and light monitoring | Agree outcome and indicators, spot-check, stay out of the way |
- Re-rate TRM whenever the task changes: a strong PM new to pricing or model evaluation is low-TRM on that task.

### Meeting classifier
- Process meeting? Confirm cadence, standing agenda, indicators reviewed, and that it would be missed if cancelled.
- Mission meeting? Confirm stated objective, one accountable owner, small attendance, pre-read, written decision and action list sent within 24 hours.
- More than about a quarter of the week in ad hoc mission meetings: find the unclear decision right or interface causing them.

### Planning and indicators
- Demand: what will customers, the market and the company require of this area in 12 months?
- Status: what will current work deliver if nothing changes?
- Gap: what must change, and what is the first action this week?
- Each objective answers "where am I going?"; each key result answers "how will I know I am on pace?"
- For every indicator, name its counter-indicator. Prefer at least one leading indicator per objective.

### Inflection-point watch (quarterly)
- Has our key competitor changed, or has the silver-bullet answer become unclear?
- Has our key complementor (platform, model provider, channel) changed or lost importance?
- Do capable people around us seem to be "losing it", with old playbooks failing without an obvious reason?
- Is there strategic dissonance: official strategy says one thing, resource allocation and front-line behavior say another?
- Who are our Cassandras (sales engineers, support leads, researchers), and when did the user last hear them out?
- Output: a one-page signal-or-noise note to the manager, with evidence, the claim, and what would disconfirm it.

## Diagnostic questions
- What did your product area and the neighboring teams produce this quarter that they would not have without you?
- Which three hours last week had the longest-lasting effect on other people's work? Which had negative leverage?
- For the decision you are stuck on: who decides, who can veto, and have you asked them?
- After the last decision that went against you, what did you say about it in rooms where the decider was absent?
- Who supervises your work on the tasks where your maturity is low, and have you asked for that support?
- What agenda did you bring to your last 1:1 with your manager?
- Which of your recurring meetings produce a written decision? Which would nobody miss?
- Which of your metrics has no counter-metric?
- What are customers, competitors or model providers doing that your leadership has not yet discussed? What evidence would make it signal rather than noise?
- If a new leader replaced you tomorrow, what would they stop doing in your area?
- Where is technically defensible judgment being overruled by who now gets to decide what "good" means?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating at the next level, per Grove):
- Status updates report output and indicators across the user's area and adjacent teams, not a list of activities.
- The user publishes decision frames (the six questions answered) before contested decisions and circulates written outcomes afterward.
- After losing a debate, the user visibly executes and defends the decision, and raises new information through the proper channel.
- The user's 1:1 agendas arrive in advance with indicators, risks and asks.
- Meetings the user calls end with a named decision, an owner and a date.
- The user writes early-warning notes on market or technology shifts backed by front-line evidence, and some are later adopted.
- Supervision visibly varies by person and task.
- Plans connect to what the team is doing this week.

Negative (current-level or below):
- Updates describe effort and artifacts.
- The user reopens decisions or lobbies after the fact.
- The user rewrites others' work or requires sign-off on low-stakes items (negative leverage).
- A calendar dominated by ad hoc syncs with no written outcomes.
- Metrics reported without counter-metrics or baselines.
- The user arrives at 1:1s without an agenda, or treats them as status recitals.
- The user defends a decision on data while stakeholders' trust is eroding.
- Warnings are raised as complaints without evidence, or not raised at all.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Treating "leverage" as permission to stop doing hard work personally. Grove counts supplying unique knowledge as leverage. Delegating it to someone less able is not.
- Claiming the output of neighboring teams as one's own in promotion materials. The formula is for choosing work, not for claiming credit.
- Using "full support" to shut down dissent early, or skipping free discussion because the leader has already decided. That turns Grove's model into theater.
- "Paranoia" as a management style producing anxiety and constant alarms. Grove's paranoia is about the business and the competition, not suspicion of colleagues.
- Playing Cassandra to gain visibility: many alarms, little evidence, no proposed test.
- Applying TRM as a fixed label ("she's low-maturity") instead of per task.
- Copying Grove's manufacturing metrics into discovery work, forcing counts where judgment is needed.
- Holding long 1:1s by default because Grove did, whether or not the work needs them.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Slootman (process discipline vs. urgency):** Grove builds output through process: indicators, meeting types, planning cycles, calibrated supervision. Slootman raises output by raising standards and tempo from the top, and treats most process as drag. Grove is more right in steady operations and large interdependent organizations where coordination failures are expensive. Slootman is more right in turnarounds or when an organization has settled into comfort. Both share intolerance for indicators that hide reality.
- **Bezos/Amazon (full support vs. disagree and commit):** They sound alike. Grove's model is a full cycle for all significant decisions: discussion, decision, support, revisit on evidence. Amazon's "disagree and commit" sits inside a system that also sorts decisions by reversibility (one-way vs. two-way doors) so that most are made quickly by small groups. Grove fits high-stakes, hard-to-reverse calls. Amazon's version fits a high volume of reversible decisions where speed matters more. Stripe also listed "disagree and commit" among its classic slogans (see Hughes Johnson).
- **Hughes Johnson (shared lineage, different emphasis):** Stripe's OKR guidance uses Grove's two questions almost word for word, and her operating system extends his planning and meeting disciplines. She puts more weight on documenting decision rights and ways of working in advance. Grove relies more on the live meeting and the manager's judgment.
- **Charan (Leadership Pipeline):** Charan's passages say each move up requires dropping individual-contributor work and taking on managing others. Grove's know-how manager lets an influential IC produce managerial output without reports. For the user, Grove supports building Director evidence as an IC. Charan warns that the passage into managing people still has to be crossed if the target role requires it.
- **Cagan (empowered teams):** Cagan wants teams given problems, not features, and trusted to solve them. Grove's low-TRM guidance endorses directive supervision. They agree where TRM is high. Cagan would say most product teams deserve high-TRM treatment. Grove would ask for evidence of maturity on the specific task.
- **Duke (decision quality):** Grove says to revisit a decision when it turns out to be wrong. Duke warns against judging decisions by outcomes ("resulting"). Combine them: revisit on new information about the world, not on a single bad outcome.
- **Campbell:** Grove's management is data- and process-first and his 1:1s were feared. Campbell's coaching starts with relationships and care. Grove's approach works when trust is already high. Campbell's matters more when a team is fragile or a leader is new.
- **Larson:** Larson's staff-plus archetypes are a modern version of Grove's know-how managers, and they agree that senior ICs lead through knowledge and influence.

## Limits and biases
- Context: an engineering-led semiconductor company from 1968 to the 1990s, with factory metaphors (the "breakfast factory") and countable outputs. Software and AI product work has fuzzier outputs and faster cycles.
- Authority: Grove wrote HOM as a senior executive and OTPS as CEO. Advice like "let chaos reign, then rein in chaos" assumes power to reallocate resources that a PM lacks.
- Survivorship and hindsight: Intel survived the memory exit and the Pentium crisis, and both are told afterward by the person who led them. The middle managers' pre-emptive shift is also a story of luck and strategic dissonance he tolerated.
- Style: Grove was known for confrontational meetings and feared 1:1s. Intel's "constructive confrontation" culture does not transplant to cultures where directness is read differently, and it has been harder on people with less positional or social power.
- Evidence: HOM is practitioner doctrine. TRM rests on situational-leadership models with weak research support. The Cassandra idea depends on psychological safety Grove does not examine.
- Era and gender: the 1983 text defaults to male pronouns and a mostly male management context. The 1995 revision updated examples but not the underlying model.
- Grove's own misses (the Pentium response, the RISC near-miss recounted in Fortune) show the method does not remove leadership bias. It gives others a way to challenge it.
