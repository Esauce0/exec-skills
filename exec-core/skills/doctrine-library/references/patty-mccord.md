# Patty McCord

## Why this source matters here
McCord was Netflix's chief talent officer from 1998 to 2012, through the 2001 layoff of a third of the company, the IPO, and the shift from DVDs by mail to streaming, and co-wrote the 2009 culture deck with Reed Hastings. She writes from the chair of the person advising the CEO on who stays, who goes, what they are paid, and what the team must look like next year, a vantage point an ambitious PM rarely sees. Her doctrine transfers well as a lens (how will the business decide whether I am needed for what comes next?) and as manager doctrine for the user's own team. It transfers poorly as culture design in a company that does not pay top of market or offer generous severance: without those conditions, the keeper test is mostly fear.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Context, not control
- **Claim:** The deck holds that the best managers get great outcomes by setting context rather than controlling people. Context means the strategy, the metrics, the assumptions, the objectives, clear roles, the stakes, and transparency about how decisions are made (slide list by reference). McCord frames management's job as building great teams that do great work on time, not policing attendance or process.
- **Source:** Netflix culture deck (2009); TED talk; Powerful.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user complains of being micromanaged, the coach reframes: what context is the executive missing, or assuming the user lacks, that makes control feel necessary? Executive trust grows when the executive sees the user make the call they would have made, which requires the user to have absorbed their context. When the user micromanages down, the coach asks which piece of context the user has not given.
- **Boundary conditions:** Needs experienced people and aligned incentives. With a new team, a regulatory constraint, or a safety-critical AI launch, some control is appropriate (see Grove on task-relevant maturity).

### D2. Every employee should understand how the business makes money
- **Claim:** Even well-intentioned people do dumb things when uninformed. McCord's HBR example: Netflix staff fixated on subscriber growth without seeing that spending on DVDs, distribution centers, and content ran ahead of revenue. Her test for clarity: ask a random employee what they should be doing right now to increase their bonus; if they cannot answer, the company has not been clear. She pushed P&L literacy for everyone; in the Wharton interview she argues that teaching people to read a P&L builds a capability that serves them for their whole career.
- **Source:** HBR 2014 (leaders own culture); Powerful ch. 2; Wharton interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach tests the user's enterprise understanding directly: can the user explain the company's P&L, the unit economics of their product (for an AI product: inference cost per task, gross margin effect, pricing), and which of the company's few big levers their roadmap moves? A PM whose case is framed only in user and feature terms is operating below the next level. The coach adapts the bonus test: can the user say what they should be doing this week to move the number the CEO watches?
- **Boundary conditions:** Some companies restrict financial information; the user must seek it (a finance partner, the CFO's staff) rather than wait for it.

### D3. Hire, reward, and tolerate only fully formed adults; latitude tracks judgment
- **Claim:** If you hire people who put the company first, most of them (her heuristic is 97%) will do the right thing without policies; don't write rules for the rest, remove them. Netflix replaced vacation tracking with an honor system and reduced the expense policy to acting in the company's best interests. Adult behavior includes talking openly about issues with your boss, colleagues, and reports. She cared more about fairness than consistency, noting that the most valuable employees get more leeway everywhere.
- **Source:** HBR 2014 (first tenet).
- **Changes coaching how:** Latitude is information. If the user gets less discretion than peers (more check-ins, more approvals), the coach treats that as evidence about how the user's judgment is perceived, not just the boss's style, and asks which past decision created the doubt. The coach also treats avoidance of direct conversations with the boss as non-adult behavior in McCord's terms.
- **Boundary conditions:** The 97% figure is a heuristic, not data. "Adults" framing can obscure real power asymmetries between a boss and a report.

### D4. Tell the truth about performance, continuously, and skip the rituals
- **Claim:** Netflix dropped formal reviews as too ritualistic and infrequent and asked for performance conversations as part of the work. It used simple informal 360s (what should a colleague stop, start, continue), moving from anonymous to signed and often face-to-face. McCord calls performance improvement plans fundamentally dishonest: when skills no longer fit, a PIP is a months-long paper trail toward a known outcome. Her Maria story: a strong manual QA engineer whose work was automated; rather than a PIP, tell her the truth and give generous severance.
- **Source:** HBR 2014 (second tenet).
- **Changes coaching how:** For attribution analysis, the coach has the user run a signed stop/start/continue with the boss and key peers and treats any surprise as evidence. For the user as manager, no ceremonial PIPs when fit is gone; say what changed and why. For the user's own position: if the user is on a PIP or its informal equivalent, McCord's read is that the decision may already be made, so the coach asks whether to invest in turning it around or to plan a transition.
- **Boundary conditions:** Employment law, works councils, and companies without severance budgets change the calculus; honesty without a cushion is harsh, and some jurisdictions require documented process.

### D5. Build the team the future needs, not the one you have or the one that got you here
- **Claim:** McCord asks managers to imagine a documentary of their team six months out: what results are on screen, how the work differs from today, and what skills produce it. Only then compare with the current team; in a fast-changing business, expect mismatches, and handle them with honest conversations and recruiting. Past contribution does not entitle anyone to the future role: the early bookkeeper who built royalty tracking but lacked CPA-level skills for a public company was told so and given a generous package rather than a jury-rigged job.
- **Source:** HBR 2014 (third tenet); Powerful ch. 5 ("Build the company now that you want to be then").
- **Changes coaching how:** Two uses. As manager, the user runs the documentary exercise before any headcount or reorg discussion. As a lens on the user, the coach asks the user to write the documentary their boss would write for the next six months and ask whether the user's skills are in it. For an AI PM the future film may feature evaluation, cost, platform, or governance work; the coach steers development toward what is on screen, because scope follows future need, not accumulated credit.
- **Boundary conditions:** Constantly optimizing for the next six months can destroy institutional knowledge and loyalty; slower-moving or regulated businesses reward continuity more.

### D6. The keeper test, and its inverse as a lens on how the boss sees the user
- **Claim:** The deck's test for managers: which of my people, if they said they were leaving for a similar job at a peer company, would I fight hard to keep? Everyone else should get a generous severance now, so the slot can go to a star. Hastings and Meyer later added an employee-side prompt (No Rules Rules, 2020, after McCord's tenure): employees can ask their manager how hard the manager would work to change their mind if they were thinking of leaving. Netflix's 2024 memo kept the test and, as reported, paired it with a question of whether the manager would hire the person again knowing what they know today.
- **Source:** Netflix culture deck (2009); No Rules Rules (2020); Entrepreneur on the 2024 memo.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach uses the inverse as a diagnostic, not an anxiety exercise: would the user's manager fight to keep them, and what is the evidence? Look for behavior, not words: does the boss put the user's name forward for high-stakes work, fight for their compensation, protect their time, discuss their next role? Lukewarm or generic praise and exclusion from planning are the opposite signals. Pair it with "would they hire me for the role I want next?" In a solid relationship the user can ask a calibration version directly.
- **Boundary conditions:** Outside a keeper-test culture, asking the literal question can read as a flight-risk signal or a compensation play; phrase it as calibration ("what would make me someone you'd fight to keep for the next stage?"). Use only where trust is established.

### D7. The business is a team, not a family, and careers belong to the employee
- **Claim:** The deck compares Netflix to a pro sports team, not a family: coaches hire, develop, and cut to have stars in every position. McCord calls the promise of lifetime employment a lie and wants companies to be a great place to be from. She changed jobs every four or five years herself and tells employees to take recruiters' calls and learn their market rate. In the Wharton interview she argues employees expect too much from employers to take care of them.
- **Source:** Netflix culture deck (2009); Rebel Playbook interview; Wharton interview; HBR 2014 (compensation).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach does not let the user wait to be developed or promoted; the user owns the plan. The trajectory model includes external options as a live variable: the user should know their market value, keep a portable record of results, and treat a move to another company as a legitimate path to the next level, not a failure.
- **Boundary conditions:** The team framing can rationalize treating people as disposable. The company that tells you it isn't a family still expects commitment; know which one you are dealing with (see Campbell tension).

### D8. Radical honesty in both directions, and bad news early
- **Claim:** People hate being lied to and spun. McCord criticizes leaders who hide bad news from employees who already know it, and says trust breaks when leaders say one thing and do another. Values are behaviors you can watch; her example is a startup CEO who named efficiency as the top value yet told staff to finish a pool game rather than be on time for a meeting.
- **Source:** Powerful ch. 3; Rebel Playbook interview; HBR 2014 (fourth tenet).
- **Changes coaching how:** For executive trust, the user brings bad news early and unspun; executives usually know part of it already, and spin costs more credibility than the news. For the user as leader, the coach checks for gaps between the values the user states and the behavior the user models in their own meetings and prep.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some information is restricted (pending reorganizations, material non-public information); honesty then means saying what you cannot share.

### D9. Debate vigorously, on facts, for the company
- **Claim:** McCord wants people to hold strong opinions and argue them on data, with facts informing rather than dictating the conclusion, and to build a reputation for putting the company's interest over their own in decisions.
- **Source:** Powerful ch. 4 ("Debate vigorously," per reader notes).
- **Changes coaching how:** For decision-making, the coach checks that the user argues from customer and business evidence rather than seniority or preference, and asks when the user last lost an argument gracefully because the data went the other way. Executives trust people whose positions move with evidence.
- **Boundary conditions:** Fact-based debate assumes shared access to data; in organizations where information is hoarded, the argument is partly about who gets to see what.

### D10. When it isn't working with your manager: frame it as team effectiveness, bring fixes, time-box, then move
- **Claim:** Ask for a 1:1 about how the two of you could be a more effective team, speak only for yourself, and bring ideas with each problem: be the problem fixer, not the problem finder. Document what is and isn't working and reassess after about a month. If nothing changes, start interviewing; there is no shame in moving on, and you take everything you did with you.
- **Source:** Slate "How To!" (2021).
- **Changes coaching how:** For managing up, this is a decision rule the coach can run: one direct conversation framed around shared effectiveness, a written record, a dated checkpoint, and a pre-agreed exit trigger. It keeps the user from both endless grievance and impulsive exits.
- **Boundary conditions:** A month is short for a large company with slow cycles; adjust the window to the rhythm of the work. Visa, family, or market constraints can remove the exit option.

### D11. People are motivated by contributing to success alongside excellent colleagues, not by perks
- **Claim:** McCord's founding lesson came from an engineer, left alone after the 2001 layoffs, who said he'd rather work by himself than with mediocre colleagues. The best perk is excellent colleagues; morale programs don't fix a failing product. In her era Netflix paid top of market, skipped performance bonuses on the view that the right people don't need them, and let employees choose their salary-to-equity mix.
- **Source:** HBR 2014 (opening, third tenet, fifth tenet); Powerful ch. 1.
- **Changes coaching how:** For talent and managerial leverage, the user's retention plan for their team is clear, important problems and strong colleagues, not engagement gestures. For the user's own career choices, the coach weighs the quality of colleagues and closeness to the company's critical path above title.
- **Boundary conditions:** Top-of-market pay is a precondition, not an afterthought; a PM in a cost-constrained company cannot replicate the model and should not promise it.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Documentary exercise (for the user's team, and in reverse for the user)
1. Imagine a documentary of the team six months from now. Write the scenes: what results, what customers see, what the team does differently.
2. List the skills and judgment those scenes require.
3. Only now compare with the current team: where are the gaps and surpluses?
4. For each mismatch, decide: develop (with a date), redeploy, recruit, or part ways honestly.
5. Reverse version: write the documentary your boss would film of their org. Are you in it? In what role? What skill of yours is on screen?

### Keeper-test inverse self-assessment
| Question | Evidence to collect | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Would my boss fight to keep me? | Stretch assignments, advocacy in calibration, compensation actions | Generic praise; left out of planning |
| Would they hire me again today for this role? | Recent decisions they delegated fully | More approvals than peers |
| Would they hire me for the role I want next? | They discuss it unprompted; give me its problems | They change the subject |
| Would a peer company want me? | Recruiter interest; market-rate data | No external signal in 12+ months |
Treat any warning sign as an attribution question: capability, visibility, perception, or context?

### Business-literacy drill for a PM
- Draw the company's P&L from memory; mark where your product appears.
- State your product's unit economics (for AI: cost per inference or task, margin contribution, pricing logic).
- Name the three company-level levers the CEO talks about most and which one your roadmap moves.
- Answer the adapted bonus test: what should you be doing this week to move the number leadership watches?
Gaps become assignments (finance partner 1:1, earnings call review, board deck read).

### Signed stop/start/continue
Ask the boss and three peers, in writing or face to face and attributed: one thing to stop, one to start, one to continue. Log them; act on one stop and one start within 30 days; tell each giver what changed.

### Context checklist before delegating or escalating
Before handing work down or taking a problem up, confirm the recipient has: the strategy, the metric, the assumptions, the objective, who owns what, what is at stake, and how the decision will be made. Missing items are the user's to supply.

### Difficult-manager time box
1. Request a 1:1 framed around being a more effective team.
2. Speak for yourself; bring one proposed fix per problem.
3. Write down what is working and not; set a date about a month out (adjust to cycle time).
4. At the checkpoint: improved, continue; unchanged, begin an external search without drama.

## Diagnostic questions
- Can the user explain how the company makes money and where their product sits in the P&L?
- Which number does the CEO watch, and how does the user's work move it this quarter?
- If the user's boss filmed a documentary of the org six months out, would the user be in it, and doing what?
- Would the user's boss fight to keep them? What behavior, not words, shows it?
- Does the user have more, the same, or less latitude than peers? What decision set that level?
- When did the user last bring bad news before it was asked for?
- Is the user being rewarded for past contribution or positioned for future need?
- What is the user's market value, and when did they last check it?
- Which of the user's reports would the user fight to keep, and what is the user doing about the others?
- Is the user running a de facto PIP on someone instead of telling them the truth?
- Are there gaps between the values the user states to their team and the behavior they model?
- If the relationship with the boss is not working, what is the dated checkpoint and exit trigger?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating at the next level, per this source):
- Frames product proposals in company economics (margin, cost to serve, revenue levers), not only user value.
- Brings bad news early, with the numbers, and a proposed fix.
- Runs the future-team exercise before asking for headcount.
- Tells reports the truth about fit, promptly, and treats exits generously.
- Uses latitude well: makes calls within context, and executives rarely reverse them.
- Knows their market value and speaks about their career as something they are managing.
- Argues from data and changes position when the data changes.

Negative (current-level or below):
- Cannot say how their product affects the P&L.
- Waits for the boss or HR to define their growth path.
- Keeps an underperformer in role for months to avoid a hard conversation, or runs a ceremonial PIP.
- Asks for headcount based on current workload rather than future needs.
- Complains about control from above without asking what context the executive is missing.
- Treats past contributions as entitlement to the next role.
- States values to the team that their own preparation and meetings contradict.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Weaponizing the keeper test: managers using it as a pretext to remove people they dislike. The WSJ's 2018 reporting quoted Netflix employees who saw it as cover for ordinary workplace politics.
- Importing the rhetoric without the preconditions (top-of-market pay, generous severance, strong hiring), which produces fear without talent density.
- A user reading "careers are yours" as permission to disengage from the team or to treat every conflict as a reason to leave.
- Obsessive self-application of the keeper test that turns into insecurity and people-pleasing; the inverse is a quarterly diagnostic, not a daily worry.
- "Adults don't need process" used to skip onboarding, documentation, or fair calibration, which disadvantages people without insider networks.
- Radical honesty without care, delivered as bluntness about people rather than about fit with future needs.
- Coaches applying Netflix doctrine uncritically in regulated, unionized, or slow-cycle environments where it fails.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Campbell (loyalty):** Campbell's loyalty attached to people and outlasted their roles; McCord's keeper test attaches to the business's future need. Both insist on honest, dignified exits (her generous severance, his "heads held high"). McCord is more predictive of how a fast-changing company will treat the user; Campbell describes what earns an individual executive's trust. The user needs both: trusted by the person, indispensable to the plan.
- **Scott (care and growth):** Scott values rock stars on gradual trajectories as stable excellence; McCord's Maria story is a rock star whose craft stopped fitting the plan. Scott grounds candor in caring about the individual, McCord in the business. Where roles are being automated (common for AI PMs' teams), McCord decides whether the role exists; Scott governs how the conversation is held.
- **Pfeffer (power):** Both reject the idea that the organization will look after you. Pfeffer's answer is to build power and manage impressions; McCord's is to build skills the business needs, understand the business, and keep external options. McCord assumes honesty is rewarded; Pfeffer warns it often isn't. McCord is more right in high-performance cultures with legible results; Pfeffer in political ones.
- **Charan and Hughes Johnson (process):** Charan's pipeline and Hughes Johnson's operating systems invest in developing people through passages, ladders, and review processes. McCord distrusts formal reviews and development programs and prefers hiring the skills needed. Process-heavy approaches are more right at scale and for fairness; McCord's for speed in a fast-changing business.
- **Horowitz:** Horowitz argues companies must train their people and that hiring for strength is not enough; McCord prefers hiring fully formed adults. Horowitz is more right where skills are company-specific; McCord where the market can supply them.
- **Grove:** Grove's task-relevant maturity says control should vary with the person's experience on the task; that is the boundary condition on "context, not control."
- **Watkins:** Watkins treats onboarding and early wins as something to plan deliberately; McCord's "adults" model assumes people self-onboard, which leaves new leaders to figure out context alone.

## Limits and biases
- Context: a consumer subscription business in hypergrowth, able to pay top of market, in California law, with Hastings's personal commitment. Hastings himself distinguishes creative firms, which thrive on variation, from industrial ones, which reduce it; the doctrine is built for the former.
- Survivorship: Netflix's success is taken as evidence for the practices; many companies copied the deck without the results.
- Critique: WSJ (2018) reporting based on more than 70 current and former employees described a "culture of fear," callous firings, and the keeper test being used as cover for ordinary politics. Netflix's 2024 memo revision kept the test, reportedly acknowledging that people will have setbacks and pairing it with a rehire question.
- Her own exit: she left Netflix in 2012 after being asked to move on; press framed it as being fired by her own system, while she has described it as amicable and as fitting her wish to work less. Accounts differ; either way it is a live example of the doctrine applied to its author.
- Evidence: the 97% figure and her statement that in 30 years she never saw an HR initiative improve morale are her experience, not data.
- Incentives: after Netflix she built a consulting and speaking practice on this doctrine.
- Equity: "fully formed adults" and unstructured feedback can favor people who already know the unwritten rules; the model has little to say about bias in who gets judged a keeper.
