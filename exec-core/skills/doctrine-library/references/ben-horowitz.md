# Ben Horowitz

## Why this source matters here
Horowitz was a product manager and product leader at Netscape, then co-founded Loudcloud and, as CEO, took it through the dot-com crash, a near-death period and a pivot into Opsware, which HP acquired in 2007. He then co-founded Andreessen Horowitz, where he has spent years evaluating and advising CEOs and executive teams. His blog essays (2010 to 2012, largely collected in The Hard Thing About Hard Things) and What You Do Is Who You Are are written for founder-CEOs of venture-backed companies. The transfer to this user runs in two directions. First, they show how the people above the user evaluate executives, so the user can hold themselves to that standard before they hold the title. Second, they give the user a precise definition of politics, which helps separate the politics they should refuse to practice from the political systems they must operate inside. The context (hypergrowth, existential risk, a CEO with total authority) differs from a PM's position in a mid-size company, and the coach should translate CEO-level advice to the user's actual span of control.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. Politics is advancement by means other than merit, and leaders create it by rewarding lobbying
- **Claim:** Horowitz defines politics as people advancing their careers or agendas by means other than merit and contribution. He argues that even apolitical CEOs create it when they reward behavior outside a defined process: an off-cycle raise for the person who pushed hardest, a casual hint to an executive about expanded scope that becomes lobbying material, promotions with no visible, defensible criteria. The hot spots are compensation, organizational design and territory, and promotions. The remedies: hire people with the right kind of ambition (D2); run strict, consistent processes for pay, org design and promotion and do not make exceptions; and handle complaints about colleagues carefully. Behavioral complaints are addressed with both parties present; complaints about a colleague's competence require the leader to form a view immediately, before the doubt spreads.
- **Source:** "How to Minimize Politics in Your Company" (2010).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach uses the definition in three directions. For the user's own conduct: asks for promotion, scope or pay are made through the process with a merit case, not by side-channel lobbying or threats. For navigating others: when the user describes a political peer, the coach first asks what the system rewards; if leaders grant exceptions to whoever pushes, expect more pushing, and read it as incentive, not character. For the user as a future leader: the coach checks that the user's own team processes (who gets the interesting project, who gets promoted) are explicit and consistent.
- **Boundary conditions:** Horowitz writes as the CEO who owns the system. A PM cannot redesign company compensation; in a highly political company, refusing all non-process moves may just leave the user invisible. Pfeffer's view is the counterweight.

### D2. Be ambitious for the company; your own success should be a by-product
- **Claim:** Drawing on Andy Grove, Horowitz separates ambition for the company's success, with personal success following from it, from ambition for oneself regardless of the company's outcome. He screens for it through language: people with the wrong kind talk about "my" initiatives and their résumé and claim sole credit; people with the right kind credit their teams, rarely say "I," and focus intensely on how to beat competitors. Hiring the wrong kind is, in his account, the surest way to make a company political, and it demotivates everyone who resents working on someone else's career.
- **Source:** "The Right Kind of Ambition" (2010); "How to Minimize Politics in Your Company" (2010).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach reads the user's self-review, promotion packet and executive updates for "me prism" language and rewrites claims into company outcomes with credit given to named contributors. In career conversations, the coach asks the user to state the company problem their next role would solve before the title they want.
- **Boundary conditions:** Language screening can penalize people who have been coached to self-advocate (a common piece of advice to women and underrepresented groups) or who come from cultures with different norms. Evaluate the substance behind the pronouns, not the pronoun count.

### D3. Know whether your company is at peace or at war, because the right behaviors reverse
- **Claim:** In peacetime a company has a large advantage in a growing market and can focus on expanding it; in wartime it faces an existential threat and survival depends on strict alignment to the mission. Horowitz contrasts the behaviors: the peacetime leader follows protocol, delegates details, seeks broad buy-in and minimizes conflict; the wartime leader breaks protocol to win, obsesses over details that matter to the mission, neither seeks nor tolerates dissent, and may heighten conflict deliberately. He argues a leader can learn both but must know the rules well enough to know when to break them. His examples include Andy Grove leaving the memory business.
- **Source:** "Peacetime CEO/Wartime CEO" (2011).
- **Changes coaching how:** Before advising on style, the coach asks which mode the user's company, and the user's product area, is in. A user running consensus-building rituals while their product line faces an existential competitive threat is misreading the moment; a user importing wartime intensity into a stable, growing business will be seen as abrasive and political. The coach also asks the user to name who decides that the mode has changed.
- **Boundary conditions:** Wartime is easy to claim and can license bad behavior. The user rarely sets the mode; acting wartime without the executive team's framing reads as going rogue.

### D4. Executives are judged on whether they know what to do and can get the organization to do it, not only on results
- **Claim:** a16z evaluates CEOs on three questions. Does the CEO know what to do: a strategy that is also the company's story, fast and good decisions made on incomplete information, and an information architecture that keeps them learning? Can the CEO get the company to do it: build a strong team and run an organization where people can work instead of dealing with politics? And did the CEO achieve results against objectives sized to the real opportunity? Results are a lagging, black-box measure; the first two are the white-box measures that predict the future. In The Hard Thing, Horowitz also relays Bill Campbell's four areas for evaluating an executive: results against objectives, how well they manage and build their team, innovation (not hitting this quarter's number by damaging the future), and working with peers.
- **Source:** "How Andreessen Horowitz Evaluates CEOs" (2010); The Hard Thing About Hard Things (Campbell framework, by reference).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach assembles the user's promotion case in white-box terms: examples of the user deciding correctly with partial information; the strategy-as-story the user can tell; the team the user built; how the user's area works with peers. A case built only on metrics moved is treated as incomplete. The coach also checks objective sizing: sandbagged goals hit easily do not count as evidence.
- **Boundary conditions:** In companies that evaluate almost entirely on numbers (some sales-led organizations), white-box evidence helps with sponsors but will not overcome missed results.

### D5. Senior people are hired and kept for strengths, not for lack of weakness
- **Claim:** When hiring executives, first figure out what you actually need (Horowitz recommends acting in the role yourself, talking to excellent people who have held it, and writing the criteria down), then hire for world-class strength in what matters most. Optimizing for lack of weakness yields pleasant, unremarkable executives. The right hire is right for this company at this point in time. The final decision should be made by the hiring leader alone, because consensus pulls toward lack of weakness.
- **Source:** "Hiring Executives: If You've Never Done the Job..." (2010).
- **Changes coaching how:** Two applications. For the user's candidacy, the coach asks what the user is world-class at that the next role needs, and discourages a "well-rounded, no gaps" narrative. For the user as a hiring manager, the coach pushes for written criteria before interviews and for owning the decision rather than tallying interviewer votes.
- **Boundary conditions:** At Director level, glaring weaknesses in collaboration or integrity still disqualify; "strength over lack of weakness" is about competence dimensions, not character.

### D6. Scale changes the executive job, and the person who fit the last stage may not fit the next
- **Claim:** Big-company executives tend to be interrupt-driven optimizers: they manage incoming demands, refine process and make complex tradeoffs. Startup executives must originate work, launching many new initiatives, hiring well and building process from nothing. Moving between the two creates a rhythm mismatch and a skill mismatch. Horowitz screens with questions like what the candidate will do in the first month (look for more initiatives than seem possible) and how the new job differs from the current one. Conversely, when a company grows, early loyal employees may not be the right people for the larger role; he advises deciding in the company's interest, stating the decision as made rather than hedging, acknowledging your own part in the mismatch, and considering pairing a demotion with a raise.
- **Source:** "Why Is It Hard to Bring Big Company Execs into Little Companies?" (2010); "Demoting a Loyal Friend" (2012).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks whether the user's target role needs an originator or an optimizer, and which the user has actually demonstrated. For an AI PM in a mid-size company, new AI product lines often need originator rhythm inside an optimizer company; the user should show both. The coach also raises the uncomfortable question early: if the user's area grows quickly, would leadership bring in someone more experienced above them, and what evidence would prevent that?
- **Boundary conditions:** Horowitz writes from the CEO's side of a demotion. The user facing one should treat it as information about fit at a stage, not a verdict on capability, and decide whether to stay.

### D7. Every org design is a tradeoff; design around communication and decisions, not egos
- **Claim:** Growth degrades communication, shared knowledge and decision-making, and the leader's job is to give ground grudgingly. The tools are specialization, organizational design and process. All organizational designs are bad in the sense that each optimizes some communication paths at the expense of others, so the designer should decide which knowledge and decisions matter most, design around them rather than around executives' ambitions, name the paths left unoptimized, and add processes to cover them. The purpose of process is communication.
- **Source:** "Taking the Mystery Out of Scaling a Company" (2010).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user proposes a reorg or new team structure, the coach asks which communication paths it optimizes, which it breaks, and what process will cover the broken ones. If the proposal's main effect is to give the user more scope, the coach flags it as the ego-driven design Horowitz warns against and as likely to be read that way by peers.
- **Boundary conditions:** Timing matters: Horowitz notes process is easier to put in place before people are entrenched. In a large company, a Director's org design proposals need to fit the surrounding structure.

### D8. Management debt compounds; expedient people decisions come due later
- **Claim:** Management debt is an expedient, short-term management decision with an expensive long-term consequence. His three examples: putting two people in one job to keep both, which blurs accountability and confuses everyone who reports to them; matching an outside offer for a key employee, which leaks and teaches everyone that threatening to quit is the way to get a raise; and skipping performance management and feedback to protect the culture, which leaves people unaware of weaknesses they could have fixed.
- **Source:** "Management Debt" (2012).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user, as a manager, is about to create co-owners, counter an outside offer, or delay difficult feedback, the coach names the debt and its likely cost. On the user's own career, the coach cautions against using an outside offer as leverage in a company that operates the way Horowitz describes: it may work once and reduce trust.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some companies (Netflix under McCord's approach) deliberately benchmark pay to the outside market, which reduces the political damage of outside offers. Know which system the user is in.

### D9. A good product manager acts as the CEO of the product and takes written positions
- **Claim:** Horowitz's Netscape-era document contrasts good and bad PMs. Good PMs know the market, product and competition deeply and take full responsibility; bad PMs have excuses (not enough funding, engineering is weak, too much work). Good PMs define the what clearly, in writing, and leave the how to engineering; they act as engineering's peer rather than a gopher or project manager. They produce collateral that scales their knowledge (FAQs, positioning, briefs) rather than firefighting all day. They take written positions on important issues; bad PMs voice opinions verbally and complain afterward that leadership made them do it. Good PMs focus on revenue and customers; bad PMs obsess over competitors' features.
- **Source:** "Good Product Manager/Bad Product Manager" (mid-1990s; a16z 2012).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks the user's record of written positions on contested decisions. A user who disagreed only verbally, or only after the fact, has no evidence of judgment and will be seen as a complainer. The coach also counts excuses in the user's accounts of setbacks, and looks for leverage artifacts the user has produced.
- **Boundary conditions:** The "CEO of the product" framing predates modern empowered teams with strong design and engineering leads. Taken literally in a peer-based trio, it reads as overreach (see Cagan).

### D10. One-on-ones are the employee's meeting and the leader's main information channel
- **Claim:** Horowitz treats 1:1s as part of the company's communication architecture: the employee sets the agenda, the manager listens about 90% of the time, and the meeting carries the ideas, problems and frustrations that do not fit formal channels. Suggested questions include what the top problem in the organization is, what is not fun about working here, who is doing great work, and what the employee would change in the manager's shoes.
- **Source:** "One on One" (2012); information architecture in "How Andreessen Horowitz Evaluates CEOs" (2010).
- **Changes coaching how:** For a user managing people, the coach checks who sets the 1:1 agenda and who talks. For the user's own 1:1 with their manager, the coach helps the user bring the agenda, including the problems the manager needs to hear. It also asks what the user learned last week that they could not have learned from dashboards.
- **Boundary conditions:** In very large orgs, 1:1s with directs are insufficient as an information system; skip-levels and written channels are needed too.

### D11. Executive skills are made, and giving honest feedback is the first unnatural one
- **Claim:** Horowitz argues CEOs are made, not born, and that much of the job consists of unnatural skills that feel wrong until practiced. The foundational one is giving honest feedback. Formulaic methods like the compliment-criticism-compliment sandwich become transparent to experienced people. Feedback works when it is authentic, comes from wanting the person to succeed, is delivered privately, is adapted to the person, is direct without being mean, and is a dialogue. Frequent feedback makes hard conversations normal.
- **Source:** "Making Yourself a CEO" (2012).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach treats discomfort with delivering critical feedback as a normal developmental gap, not a personality verdict, and prescribes repetitions. It checks whether the user's feedback to reports and peers is specific and timely, and whether the user invites feedback on their own decisions.
- **Boundary conditions:** Directness norms vary across cultures and companies; the tone should fit the setting, even when the content should not soften.

### D12. The hardest leadership skill is managing your own psychology
- **Claim:** Horowitz calls managing his own psychology the hardest skill he learned. Leaders fail by taking every problem too personally (becoming frantic or sick, or terrorizing people) or not personally enough (denial). The aim is urgency without panic. Tactics: build relationships with peers who have been through similar crises; write out the reasoning on hard decisions to get distance from emotion; focus on where you are going rather than on what you fear hitting. In The Struggle, he adds: share the burden with as many capable people as you can, look for moves, and do not waste energy on self-recrimination.
- **Source:** "What's the Most Difficult CEO Skill?" (2011); "The Struggle" (2012).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user is carrying a failing launch, a layoff, or an executive's loss of confidence, the coach separates emotion from decision: it asks the user to write the decision memo, names who else could share the problem, and checks for both failure modes (catastrophizing, denial). It treats visible composure under pressure as part of executive posture.
- **Boundary conditions:** This is not a substitute for real mental-health support. The isolation Horowitz describes is a CEO's; a Director usually has a manager and peers to lean on and should use them.

### D13. When a better product is beating you, there is no silver bullet
- **Claim:** Facing a competitor with a clearly better product (Microsoft's faster web server at Netscape; BladeLogic at Opsware), Horowitz's instinct was a strategic workaround: partnerships, acquisitions, repositioning. The correct answer both times was the hard one: fix the product. If the company is not good enough to win, repositioning will not save it.
- **Source:** "Lead Bullets" (2011).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user's response to losing deals is a new segment, a reframing, a partnership or a pricing trick, the coach asks the lead-bullets question first: are customers buying this category, and just not from us? If so, the user's credibility with executives depends on naming the product gap and a plan to close it.
- **Boundary conditions:** Sometimes repositioning is the right strategy (the competitor's strength does not matter to a segment you can own). The test is whether the move avoids the real weakness or exploits a real difference.

### D14. Culture is what leaders do, not what they say
- **Claim:** Culture is how people in a company make decisions when the leader is not present, and it is set by leaders' behavior. When leaders do not follow their own rules, compliance collapses. Horowitz recommends "shocking rules" that make a priority impossible to miss (a16z fines partners for being late to entrepreneur meetings) and object lessons after serious failures. Leaders who violate their own stated values have to correct themselves publicly and forcefully.
- **Source:** What You Do Is Who You Are (2019), via the TechCrunch interview; book by reference.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user leads a team, the coach asks which behavior the user most wants and whether the user visibly does it (e.g., writing their own reviews on time, showing up to customer calls). For AI product teams, the coach can use the idea to set a shocking rule around, for example, evaluation discipline before launch.
- **Boundary conditions:** A Director's shocking rule operates inside the company's culture; one that conflicts with company norms reads as eccentric or insubordinate.

### D15. Brilliant people can be bad employees; watch for the pattern in yourself
- **Claim:** Horowitz describes three kinds of smart but harmful employees: the heretic, who builds a persuasive case that the company is hopeless and run by fools rather than working on fixable problems; the flake, whose reliability degrades; and the jerk, whose harshness shuts down discussion. The heretic is especially damaging because intelligence makes the narrative persuasive and a public position is hard to walk back. Exceptions can be made for extraordinary talent only rarely.
- **Source:** "When Smart People Are Bad Employees" (2011).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach watches for the user drifting into the heretic pattern after frustration with leadership: building the case that leadership is incompetent in 1:1s with peers. That pattern ends trust and sponsorship. The coach redirects to specific, fixable problems raised through channels.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some organizations really are badly run. The distinction is between naming specific problems to people who can fix them and building a general case against leadership to an audience.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Politics hot-spot audit (for the user's current or future team)
| Hot spot | Question | Political signal | Remedy per Horowitz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compensation | Is pay set on a cycle with criteria? | Off-cycle raises for whoever pushes | Strict cycle, no exceptions |
| Org design and scope | How are scope changes decided? | Leaders hinting at future scope; lobbying | Decide and announce through one process |
| Promotions | Are criteria written and visible? | Surprise promotions; campaigning | Defensible, consistent process |
| Complaints | How are complaints about peers handled? | Doubts about a colleague's competence circulating | Behavioral: both parties in the room. Competence: form a view now |

### Executive self-evaluation (white box plus Campbell)
For the last two quarters, the user writes evidence under each heading:
1. Knows what to do: strategy they can tell as a story; decisions made on partial information and how they turned out; how they stay informed.
2. Gets the org to do it: people hired or developed; how easily peers and teams can work with the user's area.
3. Results against well-sized objectives: were targets ambitious relative to the opportunity?
4. Innovation: did anything that hit this quarter's number mortgage the next one?
5. Peers: what did the user do to help peers succeed, and what did they get from peers?
Gaps in 1, 2 and 5 are more predictive than a miss in 3.

### Peace or war check
Ask: is there an existential threat to this company or product line? Who has declared the mode? Given the mode, which of the user's current behaviors are mismatched (consensus-seeking in war, or intensity and protocol-breaking in peace)?

### Originator or optimizer check
List the user's last ten significant actions. Which were originated (new initiatives, hires, processes created) and which were responses to incoming demands? Compare to what the target role needs.

### Management-debt check before a people decision
Does this decision create two owners for one outcome? Reward an outside offer or a threat? Delay feedback someone needs? If yes, write down the long-term cost and who will pay it.

### Ambition language pass
Before submitting a promotion packet or self-review: count "I" and "my" claims; for each, name the company outcome and the contributors; cut claims that cannot be tied to a company result.

## Diagnostic questions
- In the user's company, what gets rewarded outside formal process? Has the user benefited from, or been hurt by, such exceptions?
- When the user last asked for scope, title or pay, did they go through the process or around it?
- Would the user's peers describe their ambition as for the company or for themselves? What would they cite?
- Is the user's company or product area in peacetime or wartime, and who says so?
- What is the user world-class at that the next role requires? What would a hiring leader write as the role's criteria?
- Can the user tell their area's strategy as a story that explains why it matters to the company?
- What decisions did the user make with incomplete information in the last quarter, and how fast?
- Does the user's evidence show origination, optimization, or both?
- On which contested decisions did the user take a written position before the outcome was known?
- What management debt is the user carrying or about to create?
- When the user's product loses to a competitor, is their proposed response a lead bullet or a silver bullet?
- Is the user building a general case against leadership in conversations with peers?
- Under pressure, does the user tend to over-personalize or to deny? Who do they talk to?

## Observable signals
Positive (executive-level behavior, per Horowitz):
- Makes asks through formal processes with a merit case; does not use outside offers or threats as leverage.
- Credits named contributors and frames accomplishments as company outcomes.
- Takes written positions on contested decisions before the outcome is known.
- Matches style to the company's mode and says explicitly when they think the mode has changed.
- Decides quickly on partial information, documents the reasoning and revisits it with evidence.
- Runs team processes (assignments, promotions) that are explicit enough that nobody lobbies.
- Faces product gaps directly in executive forums rather than reframing around them.
- Stays composed and decisive under pressure; brings others into hard problems.

Negative:
- Lobbies executives informally for scope or title; gets hints and repeats them.
- Uses "my team, my product, my win" language; claims sole credit.
- Disagrees verbally or after the fact, then says leadership made the call.
- Imports wartime intensity into a stable business, or runs consensus processes during a crisis.
- Presents a well-rounded, no-gaps narrative with no distinctive strength.
- Proposes reorganizations whose main effect is more scope for the user.
- Counters every competitive loss with a new positioning idea.
- Builds a case with peers that leadership is incompetent.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Claiming wartime to justify abrasiveness, skipping process, or overriding peers when leadership has not declared a crisis.
- Using the politics definition to label every colleague who communicates well as political. Horowitz's test is means other than merit; making merit visible is not politics.
- Refusing all informal influence on principle in a company whose system runs on it. Horowitz tells CEOs to fix the system; the user usually cannot.
- Reading "hire for strength" as permission to ignore the user's weaknesses that block the next role.
- Treating the Good PM/Bad PM document as a current job description. It is a 1990s Netscape artifact; its "CEO of the product" framing clashes with modern peer-based product trios.
- Coach misuse: applying CEO-scale advice (demote a loyal friend, fire the whole chain of command as an object lesson) to a user who lacks that authority.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Cagan (empowered teams).** Horowitz's wartime leader obsesses over details and does not tolerate dissent; Cagan's leader gives context and lets teams find solutions, with disagreements settled by evidence. Horowitz's good PM is the product's CEO; Cagan's PM is nobody's boss. Horowitz fits existential crises and small founder-led teams; Cagan fits companies with time to learn and strong cross-functional leads.
- **Pfeffer (power).** Horowitz treats politics as a pathology the CEO designs away. Pfeffer treats power-seeking as inevitable and argues individuals must build power deliberately; merit alone rarely wins. Horowitz is more right for a leader designing their own org; Pfeffer is more realistic for an individual in someone else's system. The user needs both: refuse non-merit tactics personally, and map power anyway.
- **Doshi (optics).** Doshi says optics is legitimate work and describes perception-driven evaluation; Horowitz defines politics as advancement by means other than merit. They agree when optics makes real merit visible and diverge when a culture rewards optics itself.
- **McCord (Netflix).** Horowitz calls matching outside offers management debt that teaches people to threaten to quit. McCord's Netflix encouraged people to know their market value and paid to market, removing the need for counteroffer drama. Horowitz is right in companies with fixed pay cycles; McCord's model works where pay is openly benchmarked to market.
- **Scott (Radical Candor).** Both reject formulaic feedback and want directness from care. Horowitz's wartime raised voice and purposeful profanity sits uneasily with Scott's warning against obnoxious aggression.
- **Lencioni.** Lencioni wants productive conflict in healthy teams as a matter of course; Horowitz's peacetime leader minimizes conflict and his wartime leader heightens contradictions. Lencioni's norm is closer to what most product leadership teams need.
- **Charan (Leadership Pipeline).** Charan emphasizes developing people through each leadership passage. Horowitz is more willing to replace or demote executives when the company outgrows them. Build-first fits stable, large companies; replace-when-needed fits hypergrowth.
- **Watkins.** Watkins's STARS situations (start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success) split peace and war into finer, more usable diagnoses for a leader entering a new role.
- **Bezos/Amazon.** Amazon's "disagree and commit" keeps dissent in the room before commitment; Horowitz's wartime leader does not tolerate dissent. Amazon's norm is the safer default for a non-CEO.
- **Grove and Campbell.** Mostly aligned: Horowitz borrows Grove's right-kind-of-ambition idea and Campbell's executive evaluation framework, and cites Grove as the model wartime CEO.

## Limits and biases
- Written by a founder-CEO and then a VC for founder-CEOs of venture-backed technology startups, mostly 2010 to 2012. The context is hypergrowth and existential risk with near-total CEO authority. A PM in a mid-size company has far less control over process, pay and org design.
- Heavy survivorship: Opsware's outcome makes the stories persuasive; companies that followed the same advice and failed are not in the sample.
- War metaphors and wartime norms (raised voices, profanity, intolerance of dissent) can license abusive behavior. Research on emotional expression and status (for example Brescoll and Uhlmann, 2008) suggests anger raises men's perceived status and lowers women's, so wartime displays carry different risks depending on who performs them.
- The right-kind-of-ambition screen relies on language cues that vary by culture and gender socialization; applied mechanically, it can penalize people who were taught to claim credit.
- Evidence is anecdotal and aimed at teaching, not research.
