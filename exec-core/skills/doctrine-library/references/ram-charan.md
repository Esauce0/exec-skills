# Ram Charan

## Why this source matters here
Charan is a long-time adviser to CEOs and boards (GE, DuPont, Verizon, Novartis, and Colgate are among the clients named in his books). The Leadership Pipeline grew out of Walter Mahler's 1970s "critical career crossroads" work at GE, which Charan and Stephen Drotter extended and, by 2001, had tested in more than 80 companies. What he offers this system that few others do is a precise, level-by-level statement of what has to change at each promotion. That turns "are you ready for Director?" into something the coach can test against evidence. His business-acumen and execution books supply the enterprise-thinking vocabulary, and his governance books describe what the audience at the top of the pipeline needs. Transfer caution: the model was built in large, hierarchical, multi-business corporations where general managers own P&Ls. Tech product orgs are flatter and matrixed with Engineering and Design, titles inflate, and product leaders often own outcomes without a P&L. Use the passages to read the work, not the title.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Every promotion is a passage with three shifts: skills, time application, and work values. Values is where people fail.
- **Claim:** Each level needs new skills, a different allocation of time, and a change in what the person believes is important work. Skills and time can be observed and measured. Values are harder to see: someone can look as if they have made the turn while still holding individual-contributor values. If the values shift, time and skills usually follow.
- **Source:** Drotter & Charan 2001, Passage One section; Pipeline pp. 135-139 (Kraft notes).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach assesses readiness on the three dimensions separately and gives values the most weight. "Could you do the next job?" becomes "What work do you find satisfying now, and is it what the next level has to value?" Evidence comes from calendars and from what the user chooses to highlight when describing a good quarter.
- **Boundary conditions:** The environment reinforces values. If the org rewards individual heroics at Director level, keeping IC values may be rational. Diagnose the incentive system before blaming the person.

### D2. The characteristic failure at every passage is continuing to do the work that earned the promotion.
- **Claim:** The highest performers are the most reluctant to change. The pressure to move time from doing to managing grows at every passage, and people who don't start reallocating early become liabilities who clog the pipeline. A warning sign: behavior that almost exactly repeats what brought the earlier success.
- **Source:** Drotter & Charan 2001 (Passage One); Pipeline pp. 33, 47-48, 189, 220.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach names the user's favorite old work and tracks its share of the calendar over time. For an AI PM that is typically writing the spec, tuning prompts, running evals personally, or being the sharpest voice in the model review.
- **Boundary conditions:** In small startups and player-coach roles some hands-on work is part of the job. The failure is valuing that work above the level's work and letting it crowd the level's work out, not doing it at all.

### D3. Readiness is not the same as performance at the current level.
- **Claim:** Results at one level should not be the main reason to promote someone to the next. Talent inventories wrongly treat potential as performance. The book distinguishes three kinds of potential. Turn potential means the person can do next-level work within three to five years or sooner. Growth potential means bigger jobs at the same level. Mastery potential means the same work, done better.
- **Source:** Pipeline pp. 167-172, 186; Drotter & Charan 2001 (benefits list).
- **Changes coaching how:** A promotion case has to contain evidence of target-level work already done, meaning the skills, time use, and values of the next passage, not just current-level wins. The coach asks which kind of potential the org currently attributes to the user and whether that matches the user's goal.
- **Boundary conditions:** Many tech companies promote people who are "already operating at the next level", which fits this model. Others promote on current-level impact plus open headcount. Model how this org actually decides (attribution-analysis).

### D4. Skipped passages leave gaps that surface later, under pressure.
- **Claim:** Fast-tracked high-potentials, and people who move from project leadership straight to business leadership (consultants are the book's example), miss the learning in between: setting objectives, coaching, selection, handling conflict. They treat reports like "worker bees" and hold their own managers accountable for technical rather than managerial work.
- **Source:** Drotter & Charan 2001 (Passages One and Two); Pipeline pp. 132, 140-141.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user may jump from IC to Director, which is common in AI orgs and in lateral hires, the coach names the passage skills that have never been practiced and designs compensating experience. An example is formally managing a PM or a cross-functional pod before or alongside the title.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a flat product org, skipping may be the only route up. The remedy is to name the gap and fill it deliberately, not to turn down the role.

### D5. Managing managers is where the leadership foundation is built, and most people underrate it.
- **Claim:** At Passage Two the leader must only manage. The key skills are choosing who should make the first turn, assigning managerial work, measuring people as managers, and coaching them. They must also move back to IC roles those who get no satisfaction from managing, and look past their own job to business-wide strategic issues.
- **Source:** Drotter & Charan 2001, Passage Two.
- **Changes coaching how:** For a Director candidate the coach asks what evidence exists of developing managers, not just ICs. Has the user ever moved a strong IC back out of management? Can they tell the people who can do the work from the people who can lead?
- **Boundary conditions:** In product orgs many Directors manage ICs, so the manager-of-managers test arrives at VP. Map by what the reports do, not by title.

### D6. Functional leadership means valuing work you don't understand and competing for resources on business grounds.
- **Claim:** At Passage Three the functional head communicates through at least two layers and manages unfamiliar areas, which they must learn to value. They play as a team with the other function heads, fit functional strategy into business strategy, build a long-horizon functional strategy aimed at lasting advantage, and delegate much of the functional oversight.
- **Source:** Drotter & Charan 2001, Passage Three; Pipeline pp. 72-79.
- **Changes coaching how:** The VP-of-Product test has several parts. Does the user value design research, data science, product marketing, and operations as much as PM craft? Do they argue for headcount in business terms rather than to protect their function? Do they have a two-to-three-year strategy for the product function (capabilities, talent, operating model), or only a roadmap?
- **Boundary conditions:** In product orgs, "functional" peers such as Engineering and Design often co-own the same outcomes, so the team-play test is more intense than in Charan's model.

### D7. The business-manager turn swaps a feasibility lens for a profit lens across short and long horizons, and it needs reserved thinking time.
- **Claim:** Passage Four (P&L ownership) is a sharp turn. The leader judges activities on short- and long-term profit, manages unfamiliar functions, balances quarterly targets against three-to-five-year goals, and reserves time to think. Failure signs include uninspired communication with no strategic frame, a contentious team of functional heads, solving problems personally, and neglecting culture.
- **Source:** Drotter & Charan 2001, Passage Four; Pipeline pp. 90-92.
- **Changes coaching how:** For VP or GM tracks, the coach checks three things. Does the user frame product bets in P&L terms? Is there protected thinking time on the calendar? Are the user's peers or reports fighting? Charan treats that last one as a signal about the leader.
- **Boundary conditions:** Most product VPs don't own a P&L. Use a "shadow P&L" exercise (F3) until a CPO-with-P&L or GM role is in view.

### D8. Business acumen is a small universal core: cash, margin, velocity, return, growth, and customers, plus how they interact.
- **Claim:** Every business, from a street vendor to a multinational, runs on the same few variables. It must generate cash, earn a return on capital (return = margin x velocity), grow profitably, and satisfy customers better than competitors do. A real businessperson understands each variable and how they trade against each other. Every job connects to them.
- **Source:** What the CEO Wants You to Know (Commoncog summary).
- **Changes coaching how:** Every recommendation the user takes to executives gets translated into its effect on these variables. For AI products the mapping looks like this: inference and serving cost is margin, iteration and eval cycle time is velocity, growth quality means retained and profitable usage rather than subsidized usage, and cash means compute commitments and prepayments. The coach asks which variable the initiative moves, by how much, and on what assumption.
- **Boundary conditions:** Research bets and platform investments don't map cleanly. The discipline is still to state the path and the assumptions. The frame is thin on network effects and option value, so pair it with Rumelt and Doshi for strategy.

### D9. Execution is the leader's job and runs through three linked processes: people, strategy, and operations.
- **Claim:** Execution is a discipline, not a set of tactics, and the leader has to be personally engaged in it. The people, strategy, and operations processes must be tied together. Putting the right people in the right jobs is the one task a leader cannot delegate. The seven leader behaviors are: know your people and your business, insist on realism, set clear goals and priorities, follow through, reward the doers, expand people's capabilities, and know yourself.
- **Source:** Execution (Bossidy & Charan).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user presents a strategy, the coach asks two more questions. The people question: who specifically executes it, and are they capable? The operations question: what are the milestones, quarterly targets, and contingencies? Strong promotion evidence is a strategy that shipped because the user staffed it and followed through.
- **Boundary conditions:** The book is written from a large-industrial CEO's seat (Bossidy at AlliedSignal). At Director level, follow-through has to respect peers' decision rights.

### D10. Decisions die in meetings that lack candor and follow-through.
- **Claim:** Indecision is a cultural product. It comes from reviews and meetings where people don't say what they think and don't commit to what was decided. The fix is intellectual honesty, "decisive dialogue" (open, candid, informal, ending in a course of action), and consistent follow-through and feedback.
- **Source:** "Conquering a Culture of Indecision" (abstract); Execution on robust dialogue.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach audits the user's recurring forums. Do they end with a decision, an owner, and a date? Does the user surface uncomfortable facts in reviews? At senior levels the user is expected to design the operating cadence, not just attend it.
- **Boundary conditions:** Candor in an unsafe or highly political culture can cost a mid-level leader. Read the room with Watkins and Pfeffer first.

### D11. Judging people is the scarcest executive skill. Bosses grow leaders with stretch jobs plus specific, frequent feedback.
- **Claim:** Know-How lists judging people among the eight core know-hows, alongside positioning the business, connecting the dots of external change, leading the social system, molding a team, setting goals, setting priorities, and dealing with societal pressures. Leaders at All Levels adds the Apprenticeship Model. Spot leaders early, give them "leaps" (jobs that stretch them), coach continuously rather than through annual reviews, assess distinctive strengths before weaknesses, promote on evidence, and don't hoard talent.
- **Source:** Know-How (publisher list); Leaders at All Levels (secondary summary); Execution ("the job no leader should delegate").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks whether the user can state, for each report or key collaborator, their natural talents, their next stretch, and what is blocking them. It also checks whether the user has ever exported talent to other teams. For the user's own career, the coach pushes toward leap assignments rather than increments of scope.
- **Boundary conditions:** The model assumes a boss willing to push you. In a do-it-yourself environment (Ibarra) the user has to create their own leaps.

### D12. At each new level, write your own job definition, including how you'll be measured and how the role differs from the ones above and below.
- **Claim:** A newly appointed manager should define the skills, time applications, values, and performance standards for the level, set out how the role differs from the boss's and the reports', and ask for feedback on fit. Standards should span operating results, customer results, leadership results, management results, relationships, social responsibility, and the individual technical work that only this person should do.
- **Source:** Pipeline pp. 148, 191-192.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach has the user write a one-page level charter for the current and target roles, including the small slice of IC work that only they should do. The charter becomes promotion evidence and the agenda for the expectations conversation with the boss (see Watkins).
- **Boundary conditions:** In an ambiguous org a written charter can look like a land grab. Frame it as alignment with the boss, not a claim.

### D13. Group and enterprise passages are about valuing other people's success and running the whole. Early signs show up far lower.
- **Claim:** A group manager's job is the success of other people's businesses. The work is judging which business strategy deserves capital, developing business managers, setting portfolio strategy, and assessing core capabilities honestly. At enterprise level the leader lives with three or four high-impact decisions a year, lets go of the individual pieces to manage the whole, handles external constituencies, and picks direct reports who may want the job.
- **Source:** Drotter & Charan 2001, Passages Five and Six; Pipeline pp. 107-114.
- **Changes coaching how:** These are long-horizon trajectory markers, not current bars. Early tests: does the user take satisfaction when a peer's product wins? Can they recommend defunding their own project? At Director level, portfolio thinking means judging peers' bets fairly.
- **Boundary conditions:** Don't use these as the bar for a Director case. That demoralizes and mis-targets.

### D14. Boards need management's help to govern the few things only a board can govern. They don't need operating detail.
- **Claim:** In Boards That Deliver, progressive boards add value through three building blocks: group dynamics, an information architecture, and a focus on substantive issues. They contribute in five areas: the right CEO and succession, CEO compensation, strategy, the leadership talent pool, and the company's health, performance, and risk. In Boards That Lead, boards take charge of the central idea, CEO selection, board composition, ethics, and compensation architecture. They partner with management on strategy, capital allocation, risk appetite, and talent, and they stay out of operations. The book's opening anecdote shows the failure mode: a strategy session buried under consultant slides ends with directors picking at advertising and discounting details while the strategy itself goes undiscussed.
- **Source:** Boards That Deliver (publisher excerpt; Larsson review); Boards That Lead (summary); Owning Up (question list, e.g., risks that could send the company over the cliff, readiness to name the next CEO, whether the board really owns strategy, how it gets the information it needs).
- **Changes coaching how:** This drives the board-altitude mode of executive-communication (F5). Lead with the central idea and the choice at stake. Name the material risks. Show the talent behind the plan. Keep the pre-read short so the meeting becomes dialogue. Make sure nothing lands in the boardroom as a surprise. The coach also tells the user that any board or ELT appearance doubles as a succession read: directors are judging the presenter's judgment and altitude, not just the content.
- **Boundary conditions:** This is a US public-company governance model. Venture-backed startup boards are often more operational and investor-driven. Below the CEO, managers present to the board by invitation, and the CEO owns the board relationship. Never build that channel around them.

## Frameworks in operational form

### F1. Passage map for a product org (approximate)
Map by the work, not the title. Three tests: what the person's direct reports do (ICs, managers, whole functions); whether they own a P&L; and whether their peers are in the same function or other functions.

| Passage | Typical product titles (approximate) | Defining work | Time shift | Values shift | Signature failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Managing self | PM, Senior PM; Staff/Principal PM (specialist track) | Own an area; deliver on time and at quality | Plan own work; reliability | Company standards, collaboration | n/a |
| 1. Managing others | Group PM, PM Manager, some "Directors" of small teams | Plan work, fill jobs, assign, coach, measure | From doing to enabling: 1:1s, reviews, hiring | Value making others productive, not producing | Still writes the specs and prompts; team used as extra hands |
| 2. Managing managers | Director, Senior Director (manages GPMs) | Select and develop managers; hold them to managerial work; think across the business | Divest individual tasks; time on selection and coaching | Value developing managers; interest in strategy and culture | Judges managers on product output; can't tell "can do" from "can lead" |
| 3. Functional manager | VP Product or Head of Product (whole function or a large line) | Long-horizon functional strategy; team play with Eng, Design, and GTM heads | Less pure-function time; more business-team time | Value every sub-function; business need over turf | Protects turf; mistakes the roadmap for strategy |
| 4. Business manager | CPO with P&L, GM of a product line or BU | Profit lens over short and long term; integrate functions; custodian of culture | Reserve time to think | Value the business over any single function | Solves problems personally; functional team at war |
| 5. Group manager | EVP or President over several businesses | Capital allocation across strategies; develop GMs; portfolio strategy | Thinking, learning, strategizing | Value other leaders' businesses succeeding | Imposes own strategy; still craves personal credit |
| 6. Enterprise | CEO | Few big decisions; direction, culture, external constituencies | Long horizon plus quarterly mechanisms | Value the enterprise over its parts | Runs a portfolio instead of one company |

Caveats. A startup "VP" may be at Passage 1. A big-tech Director may lead 60 people at Passage 2. AI orgs often have Director or Principal titles with no reports. For those roles, use the specialist-pipeline idea (Leadership Pipeline Institute) and assess scope, ambiguity, and business impact rather than people leadership.

### F2. Gap analysis (run each quarter and log to trajectory-model)
1. Place the user's current role and the target role on F1 by work. Record any ambiguity; for example, a role that is Passage 2 on reports but Passage 1 on scope.
2. For the target, list its skills, time applications, and values, merged with the org's own career ladder.
3. Collect evidence of the user's current operating level. Pipeline p. 159 lists the methods.
   - Calendar audit of the last four weeks, sorted into own IC production, enabling others, the cross-functional business team, strategy and thinking, talent (hiring, coaching, reviews), and external. Compare with the target mix.
   - Values probe: "Name the three things you're proudest of this quarter. Whose work were they? What did only you contribute?"
   - Read the user's latest plan "from a values standpoint". What does it optimize: the function, the user's visibility, or the business?
   - Listen to how the user evaluates reports and peers. Is it technical output only, or leadership terms too?
   - Lessons-learned review after both a win and a miss.
4. Write the gap as "operating level versus assigned or target level" for each of skills, time, and values, specifically enough that the user can act on it.
5. Work the values gap first.
6. Design a leap assignment that forces target-level work. For Director: own a PM's development. For VP: draft the product-function strategy. For GM: run a shadow P&L.
7. Update the level charter (D12) and set the next review.

### F3. Business-acumen translation (use on any proposal)
- Cash: when does money go out and come in? (compute commitments, prepay, revenue share)
- Margin: effect on gross margin (inference cost per task, support load, pricing power)
- Velocity: how fast do assets turn? (time to value, iteration cycle, sales cycle, stock of unlaunched work)
- Return: margin x velocity against the cost of the capital and team time consumed
- Growth: is it profitable, differentiated, sustainable, organic, or bought with discounts or subsidized usage?
- Customers: why do they buy from us rather than the alternative? Which segment? What dissatisfies them?
- Total business: what does this do to other units' numbers?
Output: one paragraph the CFO would accept without rewriting.

### F4. Execution linkage check
- Strategy: explicit assumptions, critical issues, milestones, and both the what and the how.
- People: a named owner for each critical piece, a capability assessment, bench depth, and a plan if the owner leaves.
- Operations: quarterly targets, synchronization across functions, contingencies.
- Social software: which review enforces follow-through, and does it end in decisions?
- The seven behaviors (D9) as a self-check.

### F5. Board-altitude briefing (executive-communication, board mode)
1. Purpose line: is this a decision, a request for input, or information? Which board area does it touch: strategy, risk, talent and succession, or performance?
2. Central idea: how the item advances or tests the company's central idea.
3. Choices: the two or three options considered, the recommendation, the assumptions that would reverse it, and the external context (competitors, regulators).
4. Risk: the downside that could materially hurt the company, the mitigations, and the risk appetite implied. For AI that includes model behavior, regulatory, data-rights, and reputational risk.
5. Talent: who leads the work, how deep the bench is, and which capability gaps exist.
6. Numbers: the few health metrics from F3, shown as trends, not snapshots.
7. The ask: the specific question for the board.
8. Form: a short pre-read so meeting time goes to dialogue, not a long slide deck.
Rules:
- No surprises. Pre-wire through the CEO or sponsor, and let bad news travel in between-meeting updates rather than first appearing in the room.
- Go into operating detail only when asked.
- If you don't know an answer, say so and commit to a date. The Pipeline gives this advice to new business managers, along with testing ideas privately with a trusted associate before a public position hardens.

## Diagnostic questions
- By work, which passage is the user in now, and which passage is the target? Is the move a passage turn or growth at the same level?
- Where did the last four weeks of time go, compared with the target mix?
- Which work does the user find most satisfying, and is it the next level's work?
- What did the user stop doing the last time scope grew? What should stop now?
- Which passage is the user at risk of skipping, and what is the backfill plan?
- Does the org see turn, growth, or mastery potential in the user? What is that judgment based on?
- Can the user state the effect of the top initiative on cash, margin, velocity, growth, and customers?
- For each strategic bet: who executes it, can they, and which mechanism tracks it?
- Do the user's recurring meetings end in decisions with owners and dates?
- For each report or key collaborator: what are their natural talents, their next stretch, and their blocker?
- Has the user ever recommended moving resources away from their own area?
- If the user had to brief the board or ELT next month, could they state the central-idea link, the risk, the talent, and the ask in one page?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating at the next passage):
- The share of the calendar spent on personal production falls, and the share spent on hiring, coaching, cross-functional business forums, and thinking time rises.
- The user describes wins through what team members did, and can name who grew.
- Proposals arrive with a margin, cash, or growth-quality translation without prompting.
- The user argues for resources on business need and sometimes concedes headcount to a peer.
- A written functional strategy exists (capabilities, talent, operating model), separate from the roadmap.
- The user runs reviews that end in decisions and checks follow-through in the next cycle.
- The user moves an underperforming manager, or returns a reluctant manager to an IC role, with care and speed.
- In executive or board settings the user stays at altitude and answers probing questions with judgment, including "I don't know; you'll have it Thursday".
- The user has identified one or two possible successors.
Negative (current level or below):
- The user is still the team's best spec writer or prompt engineer, says so with pride, and reviews every artifact personally.
- Managers are evaluated only on technical output.
- Status updates list activities, not business effects.
- The user defends turf with Design, Engineering, or Data ("not product's job").
- The user is indifferent or competitive when a peer's product succeeds.
- Strategy documents have no owners, milestones, or capability assessment.
- Executive reviews run on 40 slides, get pulled into the weeds, or surprise the boss in public.
- The promotion case is a volume of current-level output.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using F1 as a title map. Titles in startups and AI orgs don't track passages.
- Telling the user to stop all hands-on work. Charan's standards explicitly include the technical work only you should do. The error is valuing that work above the level's work.
- Treating a passage as a skills-training problem. Values are the gate, and courses don't shift values; assignments and reinforcement do.
- Using business-acumen vocabulary without numbers. "Margin" in a sentence is not acumen.
- Measuring a Director candidate against the Passage 5-6 descriptions and leaving them feeling permanently behind.
- Assuming the org runs a pipeline. Charan observes that most companies don't differentiate leadership requirements by level, so the user may have to write the level charter themselves.
- Using "skipped passage" as a reason to turn down a stretch role instead of accepting it and backfilling.
- In board mode: a non-CEO executive building board relationships around the CEO, or presenting to impress with detail rather than to enable governance.
- Charan's four common causes of individual failure, often visible in a user's own team: selecting the wrong person, leaving poor performers in place too long, not seeking feedback, and defining jobs poorly.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Charan vs. Ibarra.** Both agree the job changes before the person does. Charan is prescriptive and top-down: define the level, assess the gap, assign stretch jobs. Ibarra argues most transitions now are unlabeled do-it-yourself transitions, learned by acting first, where the target isn't known in advance. Charan is more right when the org has defined levels and a boss who manages the pipeline. Ibarra is more right when roles are ambiguous or changing fast, which is the typical AI product org.
- **Charan vs. the messier product-org path (Cagan, Larson, Doshi).** Cagan's product model organizes around empowered teams, and product leaders own outcomes rather than P&Ls. Larson shows Director and VP meaning very different things at different company sizes. The passages still describe the work, but a pure product career may never reach a real Passage Four: a CPO can be a functional leader permanently. Use Charan for GM or CPO-with-P&L paths, and the product sources inside a functional product ladder.
- **Charan vs. Watkins.** Charan is about getting ready over years; Watkins is about landing in the first months. Watkins' seven shifts cover roughly Charan's move from Passage Three to Four. Watkins puts more weight on diplomacy and coalitions, Charan more on values and time. They are complementary, not competing.
- **Charan vs. Doshi on staying at a level.** Doshi treats choosing to stay at a preferred level or scope as legitimate, framed as being true to oneself. Charan's framework says each passage requires learning to value new work, and that coaching should hold people to it or move them back. Charan's "mastery potential" makes room for Doshi's choice, but only as an explicit, owned decision. The coach tests whether the user's reluctance is a stable preference or untested discomfort.
- **Execution vs. Campbell and Cagan on involvement.** Bossidy and Charan want leaders deep in operational reality and follow-through. Campbell and Cagan stress empowering teams and staying out of the how. The reconciliation: be involved in the mechanisms and the people choices, not the solutions. Charan's stance is more right when execution is visibly failing.
- **Charan vs. Grove.** They are aligned on time application. Grove adds managerial leverage (output of your organization and its neighbors), which gives the time audit a way to measure payoff.
- **Charan vs. McCord.** Both put talent decisions at the center of the leader's job. McCord distrusts formal development machinery and favors candor and hiring for the company you will need. Charan favors systematic pipelines with HR auditing the quality of feedback. McCord fits fast-changing startups better; Charan fits large enterprises better.
- **Charan vs. Pfeffer.** In Charan's world, promotion follows demonstrated passage readiness if the pipeline works. Pfeffer says it follows power, visibility, and the boss's interests. Attribution-analysis needs both lenses.
- **Board doctrine vs. Minto.** They agree on answer-first. Charan pushes further, wanting the document to serve dialogue rather than replace it.
- **Robust dialogue vs. Scott.** They are aligned. Charan works on the mechanism (the meeting), Scott on the relationship.

## Limits and biases
- Context: built in large, hierarchical, multi-business corporations (the GE lineage and Fortune 500 clients). Product orgs in tech are flatter, matrixed, and often keep the P&L only with a GM or the CEO.
- Evidence: consulting observation and case examples with little independent empirical validation. The book's popularity (the Leadership Pipeline Institute cites 300,000+ copies) is not evidence that it works.
- Era: the core model dates from 2001. The 2024 edition says it covers digitization pushing decisions lower and knowledge-worker leverage, but it was not reviewed in depth here.
- Bias exposure: judgments of "turn potential" are vulnerable to similarity and visibility bias. Pair with Ibarra's sponsorship research before concluding that a gap is about capability.
- Perspective: written from the top looking down (CEO and board adviser). It says little about how a mid-level person should navigate a system that does not develop them.
- Leaders at All Levels favors spotting talent very early, which can entrench early advantage and discount late bloomers and career changers.
- Governance books reflect US public companies before and after Sarbanes-Oxley. One reviewer faults Charan's stakeholder framing for underweighting the board's role as shareholders' trustee. VC-backed boards differ.
