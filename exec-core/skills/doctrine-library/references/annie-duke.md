# Annie Duke

## Why this source matters here
Duke played poker professionally after graduate training in cognitive psychology, and now works as a decision strategist and author. She co-founded the Alliance for Decision Education. Her ideas were formed in poker, where bets repeat, feedback is fast and odds are explicit. Corporate bets are fewer, feedback is slow, and people's careers are still judged on outcomes. Her doctrine is therefore most useful in three places: stopping the user (and the coach) from learning too much from one outcome; making the user's confidence visible in how they communicate; and setting up quitting decisions before sunk cost and identity make quitting hard.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. Stop "resulting": judge a decision by its process and what was knowable at the time
- **Claim:** "Resulting" means judging the quality of a decision by how it turned out. An outcome reflects the decision plus luck plus information no one had. Duke's main example is Pete Carroll's pass call at the end of Super Bowl XLIX. It was defensible given the odds, and it was widely called the worst call ever because the pass was intercepted. A good decision can end badly (bad luck), and a bad one can end well (dumb luck). Hindsight bias makes outcomes look more predictable than they were. A single experience teaches less than it seems to.
- **Source:** Thinking in Bets, ch. 1; How to Decide (opening chapters on resulting and hindsight bias).
- **Changes coaching how:** After any launch, win or miss, the coach asks separately: what did the user know, what could they have learned and at what cost, what was within their control, and what was luck. It pushes back on rules drawn from one outcome, such as "we'll never ship without X again." The user should also expect bosses to judge by results, so the coach has them record their reasoning and probabilities before the outcome is known.
- **Boundary conditions:** Organizations do judge careers by outcomes, and "my process was good" does not excuse a run of bad results. After enough outcomes, the results themselves are evidence about the process.

### D2. State confidence as probabilities and ranges, not certainty words
- **Claim:** Treat beliefs as bets. Asking "wanna bet?", even silently, shows how sure you really are. Duke argues that saying how uncertain you are invites correction and makes you more credible, not less. How to Decide asks for a best estimate plus a lower and upper bound. The "shock test" asks whether you would be shocked if the true answer fell outside your range. Most people's ranges are too narrow, which shows overconfidence.
- **Source:** Thinking in Bets, ch. 2; How to Decide (ranges and the shock test).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach rewrites certainty language in the user's updates and recommendations. "This should land in Q3" becomes "about 70% by end of Q3, 90% by mid-Q4; the main risk is X." Every probability comes with what would move it. Pairing a clear recommendation with an honest probability is the target.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some executive audiences hear probabilities as hedging. Lead with the recommendation and put the confidence next to it. Precise-sounding numbers for things that cannot be known damage credibility. Getting better calibrated needs feedback that corporate settings rarely provide, so track your own forecasts.

### D3. Spend decision time in proportion to impact
- **Claim:** Every decision trades time against accuracy. The key question is how much it costs to make a slightly worse decision. Decide fast in these cases:
  - The choice fails the Happiness Test, meaning it won't much affect you in a week, a month or a year.
  - The option will come around again.
  - It is a freeroll: little downside. Decide fast whether to take it and slowly on how to carry it out.
  - It is a "sheep in wolf's clothing": high-impact options so close in value that you cannot go far wrong. Check with the Only-Option Test: would you be happy if this were your only option?
  - It is cheap to quit.

  For irreversible decisions, "stack" cheaper, reversible decisions in front of them, or run options in parallel. Before finalizing, ask whether there is information that would change your mind, and whether you can afford to get it. If not, decide.
- **Source:** How to Decide, "Breaking Free from Analysis Paralysis" (which cites Bezos's two-way doors).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user is stuck, the coach runs this flow explicitly. It points out when agonizing over two close options is itself the signal to decide. Before a one-way-door commitment, it proposes a stacked step such as a pilot, a test market or a limited rollout.
- **Boundary conditions:** In organizations, the cost of quitting includes the user's credibility and political capital, not just money and time. Count those costs too.

### D4. Set kill criteria before you start, and tackle the hardest part first
- **Claim:** People quit too late because sunk cost, attachment to what they own, and identity bias decisions made in the moment. Duke's theme in Quit is that quitting at the right time will usually feel premature. Fix this in advance with kill criteria of the form "if by [date] we have not reached [state], we stop." Criteria set ahead are easier to act on. "Monkeys and pedestals," taken from Astro Teller's X, means solving the hardest, most uncertain part first (training the monkey) before the easy part (building the pedestal). Easy progress creates a false sense of momentum and piles up sunk cost. Also get a quitting coach, someone who cares about you and will tell you the truth, and ask "would I start this today, knowing what I know now?"
- **Source:** Quit; "Mental Models to Help You Cut Your Losses"; Lenny interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** Every initiative the user launches gets kill criteria written into the launch memo and agreed with the boss. This makes the later stop decision the execution of an earlier agreement, which is safer politically for the user. In portfolio reviews, the coach asks "would I start this today?" for each project and checks whether the team is building pedestals.
- **Boundary conditions:** Kill criteria can be wrong. Revise them on purpose when what you have learned changes your model, not by letting them drift. Long-horizon bets (platforms, AI capabilities) need leading indicators, not end-state metrics.

### D5. Collect independent input before discussion; value accuracy over agreement
- **Claim:** Decision groups should prize accuracy, hold members accountable, and stay open to different views. Duke adapts sociologist Robert Merton's norms for science, known as CUDOS:
  - Communism: share all the data, including what hurts your case.
  - Universalism: judge claims on their merits, not by who makes them.
  - Disinterestedness: guard against conflicts of interest, including knowing the outcome.
  - Organized skepticism.

  To avoid contaminating the group, collect people's views independently before the meeting, keep them from seeing the leader's view first, discuss where they disagree, and then decide. When asking for advice, describe the decision without revealing the outcome or your own preference.
- **Source:** Thinking in Bets, chs. 4-5; How to Decide (group decisions); Lenny interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user runs a decision meeting, the coach recommends written estimates collected before the meeting, the user speaking last, and an explicit request for the case against. When the user asks mentors for advice, they should leave out the outcome. The coach treats drawing out dissent, rather than dominating the room, as a sign of senior meeting presence.
- **Boundary conditions:** These norms only work in groups whose members agree to them. Imposing them on a political executive team can backfire, so start with the user's own team or trusted peers. The process takes time and should be reserved for decisions with real impact.

### D6. Run a premortem and a backcast before committing, and turn them into tripwires
- **Claim:** Imagine the decision has failed and list why, separating reasons you could control from luck. Also imagine it has succeeded and work back to how. Estimate how likely each path is. Use the failure paths to set early-warning signals and pre-commitments.
- **Source:** How to Decide (premortems, backcasting, the decision exploration table); Thinking in Bets (mental time travel, precommitment).
- **Changes coaching how:** For one-way-door recommendations, the coach asks for a one-page premortem. The top failure paths then become monitoring signals and kill criteria (D4), and are reported in the recommendation as known risks with their probabilities.
- **Boundary conditions:** Premortems can turn into ritual lists of every conceivable risk. Keep only the failure paths that are likely and would change what you do.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Outcome-blind review** (use after any significant result):
| Question | Answer from notes made before the outcome |
|---|---|
| What did we believe, with what probability? | |
| What information did we have; what could we have gotten, at what cost? | |
| Which factors were in our control, and which were luck? | |
| Would we make the same call with the same information? | |
| What one change to the process, not the outcome, will we make? | |

**Confidence line** for executive updates: recommendation, then probability or range, then the main driver of uncertainty, then what would change it, then the next checkpoint date.

**Kill criteria template:** "If by [date] we have not reached [state, measured how], we [stop / cut scope / pivot]. Agreed with [boss/sponsor] on [date]. The hardest unknown (the monkey) we are testing first is [X]."

**Speed triage:** Is the impact low? Does the option repeat? Is it a freeroll? Is it a close call? Is it cheap to quit? If any answer is yes, decide now. If all are no, stack a reversible step first or run a premortem.

## Diagnostic questions
- Is the user drawing a rule from a single outcome?
- Did the user write down their confidence before the result came in?
- Which of the user's projects would they not start today, and why are they still running?
- Does the user's language in updates carry more certainty than their evidence supports?
- In the user's decision meetings, who speaks first, and are views gathered before the meeting?
- Is the user building pedestals while the monkey problem is still open?

## Observable signals
- Positive: states probabilities or ranges in recommendations; keeps a decision log; stops projects on pre-agreed criteria without drama; runs outcome-blind reviews; collects written input before meetings; says "I'm not sure, here's my best estimate."
- Negative: "we should have known" after every miss; takes credit for lucky wins; certainty words in every update; keeps zombie projects alive; reverses a sound strategy after one bad quarter; asks for feedback only after revealing their own preference.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using "that was just bad luck" to avoid accountability.
- Covering every statement in probabilities until there is no recommendation left.
- Setting kill criteria and then quietly moving them.
- Treating poker, a repeated game with explicit odds, as a model for one-off strategic bets.
- Coaches resulting on the user's own career moves, for example calling a promotion attempt that didn't land a bad decision.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Bezos/Amazon:** They agree on speed and reversibility. Duke adds the continuous cost of quitting and decision stacking. Bezos's 70% rule is a rule of thumb; Duke asks what specific information would change the decision.
- **Horowitz, Slootman:** They value projecting certainty and conviction under pressure. Duke values calibrated uncertainty. Make the call with conviction and report the odds honestly. When a team needs direction in a crisis, lead with the call.
- **Goldsmith:** Feedforward bans discussing the past, and Duke requires honest reviews of past decisions. Use Duke for decisions and Goldsmith for behavior.
- **Rumelt:** He treats strategy as a hypothesis, which fits Duke, but his "gnarly" challenges often can't be assigned meaningful probabilities. Use judgment and design where the odds cannot be estimated.
- **Pfeffer:** Organizations judge by results and appearances. The user can refuse to do this in their own thinking, but still has to manage how their outcomes are perceived.

## Limits and biases
The poker analogy works less well where decisions don't repeat, feedback is slow and outcomes are political. The books are popular syntheses of behavioral-science research plus anecdotes, and Duke does not present new empirical findings. Probability language requires audiences who can read it. Calibration takes feedback volume that most PMs do not get on strategic calls.
