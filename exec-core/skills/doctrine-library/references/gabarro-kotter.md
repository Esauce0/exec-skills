# John J. Gabarro and John P. Kotter

## Why this source matters here
Two Harvard Business School professors wrote "Managing Your Boss" in 1980 from studies of managers in large US companies. HBR reprinted it in 2005 as a classic. The article treats managing up as part of the job, not as politics or apple polishing, and it names the mechanism that links the boss relationship to scope: a boss who cannot rely on a subordinate's accuracy has to check every decision, and so cannot delegate. The cases come from 1970s manufacturing and use male pronouns throughout. The principles transfer well to a PM with one boss. They transfer less well to matrixed reporting lines, which the article does not cover.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. The boss relationship is mutual dependence between two fallible people, and the subordinate has to manage it
- **Claim:** Bosses need cooperation, reliability and honesty from their reports. Reports need the boss for links to the rest of the organization, for priorities and for resources. Managers go wrong when they assume the boss does not depend on them, or that they can be self-sufficient, or that the boss will somehow know what help they need. The realistic expectation is modest help. In the Gibbons/Bonnevie case, the boss's weakness with people was already known to everyone, but the subordinate paid the price: he was fired, and the company lost $2-5 million.
- **Source:** "Managing Your Boss", opening case and "Misreading the Boss-Subordinate Relationship".
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user says "my boss is bad at X", the coach accepts the diagnosis and then asks what the user is doing to compensate, because the cost will land on the user. For attribution analysis, the coach separates a boss deficit from a relationship-management deficit before recommending anything.
- **Boundary conditions:** The article admits that some pairs really cannot work together. Abuse and ethical problems are not relationship-management problems. They call for escalation or exit.

### D2. Build an explicit model of the boss's goals and pressures, and keep it current
- **Claim:** In one case a new marketing VP diagnosed the market correctly (share needed to grow), but his boss's immediate goal was fast profitability. The boss had championed the acquisition and his personal credibility depended on it. The VP took information at face value, filled gaps with assumptions and never clarified his boss's objectives. Both men were fired. Effective managers test their assumptions with the boss and with the people around the boss, and they keep doing so because priorities shift.
- **Source:** "Understanding the Boss".
- **Changes coaching how:** Before any major recommendation, the coach asks the user to state the boss's top goal this quarter, what the boss's own boss and peers are pressing on, and what the boss has personally at stake. A plan that is right on the merits but wrong on the boss's time horizon gets reframed or resequenced, not abandoned.
- **Boundary conditions:** Understanding the boss's pressures does not mean giving up a correct diagnosis. When the boss's goal conflicts with the company's interest, the question becomes one of candor and escalation (see Scott, and Bezos on backbone).

### D3. Fit the boss's intake style: readers vs listeners, and involvement vs delegation
- **Claim:** The article credits Peter Drucker with dividing bosses into "listeners" and "readers" and uses the idea only for how the boss prefers to receive information. With a listener, brief in person and then follow up with a memo. With a reader, send the memo or report first and then discuss it. Either way, both a conversation and a document exist; the style decides which comes first. The same logic applies to decision style. A high-involvement boss will get involved one way or another, so involve them early on your own initiative. A delegator wants major problems and important changes only. In the article's example, two division managers worked for the same formal new president. One sent background notes and agendas ahead and did well. The other did not and eventually resigned. The authors say the difference was sensitivity to style, not ability.
- **Source:** "Understanding the Boss" (formal-president case); "Compatible Work Styles". Drucker later applied the same distinction to self-knowledge in "Managing Oneself" (1999).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks two questions: is the boss a reader or a listener, and high-involvement or a delegator? It then designs the update cadence and format from the answers. A discursive user working for a terse boss gets the article's fix: a short agenda, and an explicit reason given whenever a digression is needed.
- **Boundary conditions:** Many executives are mixed, and style shifts under stress. Where the company has a writing norm (Amazon narratives), the institution's format overrides personal preference. Fitting the style never replaces the substance.

### D4. Know your authority reflex: counterdependent or overdependent
- **Claim:** Because the subordinate is usually the more dependent party, frustration is normal. Counterdependent managers resent authority and escalate conflict, sometimes fighting for its own sake. The boss senses the hostility, trusts them less and becomes less open. Such managers are often good at championing their own people. Overdependent managers swallow disagreement and go along with decisions they know are poor, even when the boss would change course given more information. Both views miss that bosses are fallible, have limited time and knowledge, and are not enemies. The authors say the predisposition itself rarely changes, but awareness lets you predict your overreactions. One manager broke an escalating argument cycle by proposing a pause and reconvening later.
- **Source:** "Understanding Yourself".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach infers the user's lean from evidence, such as how disagreements with the boss unfold and whether the user ever brings dissent. For a counterdependent user it prescribes pause-and-reconvene and separating winning the argument from getting the outcome. For an overdependent user it prescribes bringing one well-supported disagreement to the boss each month.
- **Boundary conditions:** Treat these as heuristics, not clinical labels (the article's psychoanalytic framing is dated). The cost of disagreeing with a boss is not the same for everyone, so calibrate how and where.

### D5. Surface expectations yourself; the burden is on the subordinate
- **Claim:** Most bosses do not spell out what they expect. Formal systems such as planning and appraisal never capture it fully, and expectations change between reviews. Techniques: draft a detailed memo covering the key aspects of your work, send it for approval, then go through it face to face. Or hold ongoing informal talks about objectives. Or learn indirectly from the boss's former reports and from what the boss has committed to their own superior. Also tell the boss your own expectations and persuade them to accept the ones that matter, especially with an overachieving boss whose standards are unrealistic.
- **Source:** "Mutual Expectations".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach has the user write a one-page memo covering what they believe is expected this half, what they will escalate, and what they will decide alone, and then review it with the boss. The same method is used to pin down what next-level evidence the boss would need to see.
- **Boundary conditions:** Level guides and career ladders set some expectations formally, but the boss's own reading of them still has to be checked.

### D6. Assume you under-inform, and bring problems early
- **Claim:** Bosses usually need more information than subordinates naturally supply, and subordinates overestimate what the boss already knows. Many bosses signal, often nonverbally, that they want only good news, and some rate people higher for not bringing problems. The boss still needs to hear about failures. Some managers route bad news through impersonal systems; others make sure potential problems reach the boss immediately.
- **Source:** "A Flow of Information".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach audits the user's last month of updates for the share and timing of bad news. A run of all-green updates is flagged as a risk, not a success. With a good-news-only boss, the coach suggests a standing mechanism, such as a risk register or dashboard, that delivers bad news without it being a personal confrontation each time.
- **Boundary conditions:** How much information a boss needs falls as their confidence in the subordinate rises. Over-informing wastes the boss's time (D8).

### D7. Dependability and honesty determine how much the boss can delegate
- **Claim:** Most undependability is accidental, caused by optimistic dates or unclear priorities. One president said he would rather have a subordinate who was consistent, even with fewer peak successes, because then he could rely on him. Shading the truth or playing down issues is easy, and today's concern becomes tomorrow's surprise. Without trust, the boss feels compelled to check every decision, which makes delegation hard.
- **Source:** "Dependability and Honesty".
- **Changes coaching how:** This is the causal link to scope. The coach treats slipped commitments and softened status reports as direct blockers to promotion, not as minor hygiene. It recommends committing to dates with a stated confidence level and re-baselining the moment a date is at risk.
- **Boundary conditions:** The preference for consistency over peaks comes from one president. Some bosses reward big swings. Surprises are expensive nearly everywhere.

### D8. Spend the boss's time and influence selectively
- **Claim:** The boss's time, energy and influence are limited, and every request draws on them. One VP spent his boss's influence getting a meddlesome secretary in another department fired. Later, when he needed help on important problems, the goodwill was gone.
- **Source:** "Good Use of Time and Resources".
- **Changes coaching how:** Before any request that uses the boss's capital, the coach asks whether it serves the most important goals: the user's, the boss's and the company's. Small items get bundled or handled another way.
- **Boundary conditions:** Asking too little is also a failure (the "self-sufficient" manager in D1). The rule is to be selective, not to avoid asking.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Boss model card** (rebuild quarterly; answer from evidence, not impressions):
| Field | Question | Evidence source |

| Decision style | High-involvement or delegator | When they intervene uninvited |
| Conflict | Thrives on it or minimizes it | Reactions to pushback |

**Self card:** strengths and weaknesses; personal style; lean toward counterdependence or overdependence, with one recent example.

**Relationship check** (from the article's checklist): Does the way we work fit both styles? Are expectations explicit and current? Is the boss informed, including of bad news? Have I been dependable and honest this month? Have I used the boss's time and influence only where it mattered?

## Diagnostic questions
- What is the boss's most urgent goal, and what are they personally on the hook for?
- Would the boss say they get problems from the user early, late or never?
- Which of the user's commitments slipped in the last quarter, and did the boss hear about it before or after the date?
- Is the user's usual failure mode fighting the boss or deferring to them?
- When did the user last confirm the boss's expectations explicitly?
- What did the user last ask the boss to spend influence on, and was it worth it?

## Observable signals
- Positive: sends pre-reads to a reader boss and briefs a listener in person with a written follow-up; reports risks before the boss asks; hits dates or re-baselines early; brings well-supported disagreement and then works the decision; takes a written expectations memo to the boss; asks for the boss's influence rarely and for things that matter.
- Negative: meetings with the boss spent reconstructing background; the boss learns of problems from others; optimistic dates that slip; arguments that escalate in a cycle, or silent compliance followed by complaints; "my boss never told me"; spending escalation capital on minor irritations.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Reading "manage your boss" as flattery or impression management. The article explicitly rejects this.
- Adapting to the boss's style so far that the user drops substance or candor.
- Using "the boss is bad at people" to explain away the user's own outcomes.
- Over-informing to seem transparent, which uses up the boss's time.
- Treating reader vs listener as a fixed personality type instead of checking it against how the boss actually responds.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Scott (Radical Candor):** Scott asks people to challenge the boss directly. Gabarro and Kotter emphasize fitting style and choosing battles. Scott is more right when the boss invites candor. G&K are more right with a directive boss or a counterdependent user who needs to control their reflex.
- **Pfeffer:** Pfeffer treats managing up as an exercise of power and accepts flattery as effective. G&K frame it as non-political work in the shared interest. Pfeffer describes zero-sum environments better. G&K describe the relationship-level mechanics better.
- **Bezos/Amazon:** A written-narrative culture removes the reader vs listener choice at the institutional level. Adapt to the individual boss only where the norm leaves room.
- **Watkins:** His "five conversations" with a new boss (situation, expectations, style, resources, development) put G&K's inventory into a transition sequence. They are complementary, and Watkins is more structured for the first 90 days.
- **Goldsmith:** Both focus on the user's own behavior as the controllable half. Goldsmith supplies the change method that G&K lack.

## Limits and biases
The article is case-based field observation, not controlled research. It comes from large 1970s US industrial firms with single reporting lines and all-male examples. It assumes a basically reasonable boss. It does not address matrix reporting, remote work, or the different penalties that people face for disagreeing with a boss depending on identity. The account of predispositions toward authority rests on psychoanalytic theory.
