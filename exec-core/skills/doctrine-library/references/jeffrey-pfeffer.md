# Jeffrey Pfeffer

## Why this source matters here
Pfeffer has taught organizational behavior at Stanford GSB since 1979. He co-developed resource dependence theory, and he runs The Paths to Power, a popular and openly controversial elective. His 2018 syllabus says the course exists because weak skill with power dynamics has cost talented graduates promotions and jobs. He is an academic observer and synthesizer of social-science research. He has not been an operator, and his examples come from politics, finance, healthcare, and large companies. For a strong AI PM, the most common stall is a belief that the work will speak for itself. Pfeffer is the corrective: he describes the terrain the user is walking on. His tactics are descriptive and ethically neutral by his own framing ("power is a tool"). This system adds its own boundary. The coach uses Pfeffer to diagnose the situation and to build legitimate power, and never to script deception, insincere flattery, credit theft, or harm to colleagues.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Performance is the entry ticket, not the mechanism
- **Claim:** Pfeffer reports that the research link between job performance and career outcomes (pay, promotion, tenure) is positive and statistically significant but small. Performance is seldom sufficient and sometimes not necessary. The belief that merit will be noticed and rewarded, which he calls a just-world belief, is the trap: it keeps capable people from taking the actions that actually produce advancement. Exceptional competence in the current role can even slow promotion, because the boss does not want to lose you.
- **Source:** Power ch. 1 "It Takes More Than Performance"; Yale Insights; Fortune 2010.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user says "my results should speak for themselves," or is frustrated after being passed over despite strong results, the coach does not default to "ship more." It runs attribution-analysis across four causes: capability, visibility to the deciders, the relationship with the deciders, and context (unit power, sponsor, timing). The first questions are who decides, what those people actually see, and what they believe about the user.
- **Boundary conditions:** Pfeffer does not say performance is irrelevant, and the coach must never use him to excuse weak work. Performance weight is higher where output is measurable and individually attributable. PM work is the opposite (lagging outcomes, shared credit), so perception carries more weight for PMs. Sponsors still treat performance as table stakes (see Hewlett).

### D2. Treat the perceptions of those above you as the operative variable
- **Claim:** The people above you control your advancement, so their view of you matters more than your own view of your work. Keep them informed and make their goals easier to reach. Pfeffer is blunt that making superiors feel good about themselves works, and that flattery is underused because people wrongly assume it is transparent. His syllabus (paraphrasing Gary Loveman) adds that at some point you cannot afford to like or dislike colleagues: relationships on your critical path simply have to work.
- **Source:** Power ch. 1; Fortune 2010; HBR IdeaCast 2010; OB377 syllabus p. 3.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks whether the user's manager, and the manager's manager, can state the user's top two or three contributions this quarter in their own words. It recommends updates framed in the manager's priorities, not the user's task list. It asks which critical-path relationships the user is neglecting because they dislike the person.
- **Boundary conditions:** System rule: the coach recommends only sincere, specific appreciation and real help with the boss's goals. It never scripts praise the user does not believe. Gabarro and Kotter's mutual-dependence method is the preferred operational form. Over-investing upward while neglecting peers and reports shows up later in 360s and skip-levels.

### D3. Get out of your own way first
- **Claim:** Rule 1. The first obstacle is usually self-imposed: impostor feelings, self-descriptions that diminish you, reflexive apologies, reluctance to self-promote, and a need to be liked. People tend to read warmth and competence as trading off, so Pfeffer's advice is to establish competence first, after which warmth is read as strength. His syllabus devotes a session to "asking for things and not obsessing about being liked."
- **Source:** 7 Rules ch. 1; OB377 Session 3; CEO Coaching International podcast.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach audits a week of the user's language in docs, chat and meetings: apologies, "just," "sorry to bother," minimizing their own contribution, "I might be wrong but." It separates calibrated uncertainty ("about 70% confident; the risk is eval coverage"), which is good evidence practice, from status-lowering hedges ("this is probably dumb"), which are pure cost. It rewrites apologies as plans.
- **Boundary conditions:** Assertiveness is penalized more for some groups. Pfeffer acknowledges that gender effects are unclear, and Hewlett's data shows a narrower acceptable band for women and people of color. The coach adjusts the tactic to the person and the setting.

### D4. Ask; people comply more often than you predict
- **Claim:** People underestimate how willing others are to help. In the studies he cites (Flynn and Lake), requesters overestimated how many people they would need to ask by roughly double, and some gave up because asking was uncomfortable. Being asked also flatters the person asked. The downside of asking is usually just staying where you are.
- **Source:** Power ch. 4; Lenny's Podcast; 7 Rules rule 2.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach turns the user's wishes into explicit, dated asks: a seat in the quarterly business review, ownership of the AI platform roadmap, a skip-level meeting, a sponsor conversation, a stretch assignment. It tracks asks made per month as a leading indicator.
- **Boundary conditions:** Asks should be specific, grounded in delivered value, and reciprocal. Repeated asks without delivery spend credibility.

### D5. Break conventions deliberately, never integrity rules
- **Claim:** Rule 2. People who violate norms are seen as more powerful (Van Kleef et al., 2011, assigned in his syllabus). Rules tend to protect incumbents, so following all of them keeps you in line behind them. Pfeffer frames this as a risk/return trade-off; his podcast example of the downside is Elizabeth Holmes.
- **Source:** 7 Rules ch. 2; OB377 Session 3 readings; CEO Coaching International podcast.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach sorts rules into two classes. Conventions are candidates to break: wait your turn to present, stay in your lane, never email a VP directly, use the standard deck template, wait for the planning cycle. Integrity rules are never candidates: law, security, privacy, honesty with data, AI safety and governance commitments, and promises made. It asks which convention is keeping the user invisible.
- **Boundary conditions:** In regulated or AI-risk contexts the downside tail is severe and long-lived. People whose norms you break may retaliate even while observers credit you. Breaking conventions pays off only when the user can deliver on the bet.

### D6. Choose where to work: unit power and trajectory matter
- **Claim:** Power ch. 3 argues that departments differ in power and that power shifts over time. He cites Fligstein's finding that CEO backgrounds moved from manufacturing to sales and marketing to finance over the twentieth century. Indicators of unit power include relative pay, physical proximity to top leadership, representation on key committees, and the backgrounds of senior executives. The trade-off is that powerful units are crowded. A rising, under-exploited area can offer faster visibility, as in the SAP case (Zia Yusuf) he teaches.
- **Source:** Power ch. 3; OB377 Session 4.
- **Changes coaching how:** For an AI PM, the coach asks whether the user's team is on the company's critical path (revenue, the CEO's strategic narrative, board airtime) or is a showcase side project. It checks where budget and headcount are moving, whose leaders get promoted, and who presents to the board. It weighs being central in a rising unit against being peripheral in a powerful one.
- **Boundary conditions:** Rising units can collapse with a hype cycle. Moving into a powerful unit where the user is peripheral can be worse than staying central in a weaker one. Unit choice is a trajectory-model decision, not a reflex.

### D7. Create resources from nothing, including information and neglected tasks
- **Claim:** Power ch. 5: resources (money, jobs, information, attention, social support) create power, and you can build them from unglamorous, neglected work that senior people value. His cases include Frank Stanton at CBS (audience research that management needed) and Lyndon Johnson's early congressional roles. The IdeaCast adds budget control, since whoever holds the gold makes the rules.
- **Source:** Power ch. 5; OB377 Session 5; HBR IdeaCast.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach looks for neglected but valued work in the user's context: owning the model-quality dashboard executives actually read, running the cross-org AI governance forum, writing the quarterly AI landscape memo for the leadership team. This is scope-expansion by creating something others come to depend on.
- **Boundary conditions:** It must be visible to deciders and must create dependence. Otherwise it becomes unrewarded glue work that consumes time. The coach tests whether the task puts the user in front of decision-makers.

### D8. Network relentlessly, and pay attention to where you sit in it
- **Claim:** Rule 5. Pfeffer cites longitudinal studies in which networking predicts career outcomes. Central positions give you information and visibility. Brokers who bridge otherwise disconnected groups (Burt's structural holes) gain advantage, while people one step removed from the broker gain little. Weak ties bring new information (Granovetter). His first principle is generosity: start by asking how you can help. His syllabus has students map whom they spend time with against whom they need, and whether they occupy any brokerage positions.
- **Source:** 7 Rules ch. 5; Power ch. 6; OB377 Session 7 and third self-reflective assignment; Lenny's Podcast.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach maps the user's network: time spent with whom, compared with who matters for the next role. It looks for brokerage openings natural to an AI PM: between research/ML and go-to-market, between legal/policy and product, between the platform team and business units. It recommends a light cadence for maintaining weak ties.
- **Boundary conditions:** Networking without delivery reads as politicking. Brokers who hoard information and play sides lose trust. Brokerage that connects people and makes both sides better off (the "iungens" orientation, Obstfeld) is the form the coach recommends.

### D9. Show up in powerful fashion, as a practiced skill
- **Claim:** Rule 3 and Power ch. 7: how you speak and carry yourself changes how much power others grant you, especially when your actual power is ambiguous. The specifics: sustained eye contact; not reading from notes; short, simple, declarative sentences; no verbal hedges or fillers; repetition of the core message; purposeful pauses. Apologizing in task settings lowers perceived competence and status. He cites Tiedens's findings that displays of anger are read as dominance and competence. He treats these as learned skills and recommends small experiments, roughly a 15% stretch beyond what feels comfortable.
- **Source:** 7 Rules ch. 3; Power ch. 7; OB377 Sessions 9–10; Lenny's Podcast.
- **Changes coaching how:** For meeting-presence, the coach rehearses the user's first 30 seconds in an executive review: the claim first, no apology for the agenda, no filler, eye contact with the camera. It picks one stretch behavior per week and reviews what happened.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some of the underlying research is weak. The power-posing study he assigned (Carney, Cuddy and Yap, 2010) failed to replicate, and Carney later disavowed it. Anger displays carry a documented backlash for women, and over repeated interactions they erode the trust of peers and reports. The coach recommends composure and declarative clarity, never performed anger or strategic interrupting.

### D10. Build a brand and make sure credit lands; perception is reality
- **Claim:** Rule 4 and Power ch. 8. Bosses are busy and inattentive, so work does not speak for itself. Reputations are self-fulfilling. One study he uses found supervisors rated employees they had helped hire above inherited staff, even after controlling for objective performance (Schoorman, as summarized). First impressions persist. Third-party praise is more credible than self-praise. A good brand is a coherent, short story of expertise and distinctiveness. His 7 Rules case of Deborah Liu shows how repeatedly telling the story of one small, high-leverage win built more credit than larger achievements.
- **Source:** 7 Rules ch. 4; Power ch. 8; OB377 Session 8.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach helps the user write a two-sentence brand anchored in real, distinctive capability. An example: "turns frontier-model capability into shipped, governed revenue product." It then checks for repeated forums where that story gets told, and makes sure peers, engineering leads or customers describe the win in their own words. It also checks attribution language ("I" versus "we") for accuracy in both directions.
- **Boundary conditions:** Inflating credit or claiming team work as your own destroys trust with the people the user will need, and senior diligence exposes a brand without substance. System rule: the goal is accurate, visible attribution, never inflation.

### D11. Use power once you have it, early and deliberately
- **Claim:** Rule 6: use power quickly, while a new role still has a honeymoon period. Put aligned people in key positions and turn opponents into stakeholders by giving them a role in your success. Where needed, move people out in ways that let them save face. In his view, using power tends to build it rather than use it up.
- **Source:** 7 Rules ch. 6; Power ch. 9 "Overcoming Opposition and Setbacks"; "Power Play" (HBR 2010).
- **Changes coaching how:** In a transition to Director or Group PM, the coach pushes for a few early, visible decisions (a portfolio cut, one owner per disputed area) rather than an indefinite listening tour. It helps the user co-opt skeptics, for example by making a doubtful engineering lead the owner of the evaluation bar.
- **Boundary conditions:** Acting on an incomplete diagnosis is dangerous (Watkins). "Moving people" is limited to legitimate, fair talent decisions made through proper process. The coach never helps plan retaliation.

### D12. Success is forgiven; the outcome narrative dominates
- **Claim:** Rule 7: once someone holds power, what they did to get it is largely forgiven, forgotten, or both. Observers infer merit from success (the just-world belief again), and the powerful write the official history.
- **Source:** 7 Rules ch. 7; jeffreypfeffer.com rule list.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach uses this to explain why a peer who cut corners was promoted. Understanding the mechanism lets the user move past grievance to an accurate model of how their organization evaluates people. The coach does not treat it as permission for anything.
- **Boundary conditions:** The claim is descriptive and heavily subject to survivorship bias, since rule-breakers who failed are invisible. Scrutiny now lasts longer (social media, #MeToo, AI incidents, regulators). For the coach this rule has diagnostic value and no ethical license.

### D13. Power changes the holder and is lost through predictable mechanisms
- **Claim:** Power erodes the skills that earned it, including empathy and attention to others (Yale Insights). Power ch. 11, as summarized, lists how power is lost: overconfidence and insensitivity, trusting the wrong people, impatience, fatigue, and failure to adapt when the context changes. Ch. 10 lists the price: scrutiny, loss of control over your time, and costs to family and health.
- **Source:** Power chs. 10–11; OB377 Sessions 16–17; Fortune 2010 (on fatigue).
- **Changes coaching how:** As the user gains scope, the coach watches for dropped peer relationships, relationship work delegated away, abrupt treatment of juniors, and energy decline. Before a promotion push, it runs an explicit conversation about whether the user wants the price.
- **Boundary conditions:** This entry converges with trust-based sources. The fastest way to lose power is to behave in ways Campbell and Scott would also flag.

### D14. Distrust aspirational leadership advice; test it against what your organization rewards
- **Claim:** Leadership BS argues that the leadership industry sells how leaders should behave (authentic, modest, truthful, trustworthy, caring). Pfeffer argues that many successful leaders do not behave this way and that following the prescriptions naively costs people their jobs. On authenticity: leaders need to be true to what others need and expect from them, not to themselves. Self-promotion beats modesty for getting hired, promoted and paid. In his syllabus he calls "authentic leadership" almost certainly neither possible nor desirable.
- **Source:** Leadership BS; Bob Morris interview; OB377 syllabus p. 2.
- **Changes coaching how:** This becomes an evidence-discipline check on every source, including the other thinkers in this roster. The coach asks: in this company, what did the last three people promoted to the target level actually do? Advice is weighted by what the environment observably rewards, not by how admirable it sounds.
- **Boundary conditions:** The critique concerns the gap between rhetoric and outcomes across many organizations. It does not show that honesty and trust fail in high-trust, long-tenure, small-senior-circle environments, where reputation for integrity compounds (Campbell, McCord). That is also where the system's ethical line sits regardless.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Power diagnostic (adapted from the OB377 Individual Power Diagnostic)
Run it for the user's current organization, or for a target organization before a move. Pfeffer's version requires interviewing 3–7 informed people, which trains the skill of asking for sensitive information.
| # | Question | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Who are the major power players here, including the user? | Who decides roadmap, headcount, promotions; who gets airtime with CEO and board |
| 2 | What are their interests and unstated agendas? | Their goals, their metrics, what embarrasses them |
| 3 | What resources or currencies does each hold? What alliances exist? Where is the user? | Budget, headcount, information, technical authority, customer access |
| 4 | What are the user's own bases of power? | Expertise, information, relationships, results, formal role |
| 5 | Which relationships with the boss and these players should the user build, or not? | Critical-path list with current state |
| 7 | What assumptions or mental models does the user bring that may cause problems? | "Merit wins," "politics is dirty," "my boss will advocate for me" |
(The syllabus skips question 6; the numbering is kept here to match the source.)

### Unit power scan
For the user's unit and one or two alternatives, score each as rising, flat or falling: relative pay and level density; proximity to the CEO's staff; representation on key committees (planning, pricing, AI governance); backgrounds of the last two promotions to VP; share of budget and headcount growth; share of board and earnings-call narrative. Decide whether to deepen centrality in place, move toward a rising unit, or create a new resource.

### Self-handicapping audit (from the 7 Rules self-assessment exercises, as summarized)
1. Collect one week of the user's written and spoken language (docs, chat, a recorded meeting).
2. Count apologies, minimizers, fillers, and unprompted self-deprecation.
3. List accomplishments from the last quarter. For each, did the user state it, understate it, or leave it unsaid to decision-makers?
4. List things the user wanted but did not ask for.
5. Choose one change per week at about a 15% stretch and review the observed reactions.

### Network structure audit (from the OB377 third self-reflective assignment)
Who does the user spend the most time with, and why? Which positions (not names) matter most for their ambitions? Which groups do they bridge? How many weak ties that bring non-redundant information do they have? What will they change this month?

### Perception ledger (coach's synthesis)
For each decision-maker for the next promotion, record: what they know about the user's work, how they learned it (directly, via the manager, via peers), when they last had a substantive contact, what they need this half, and the user's next legitimate contribution to that need.

### Legitimacy filter (system rule, not Pfeffer's)
Apply to any Pfeffer-derived tactic before recommending it:
- Transparency: would the user be comfortable if the other person knew exactly what they were doing and why?
- Truth: does it involve any false statement, spin of data, or misattributed credit? If so, reject it.
- Harm: does the user gain by damaging a colleague's standing through deception or exclusion? If so, reject it.
- Reciprocity: does the other party also get something of value?
- Rule class: is the rule being broken a convention or an integrity rule?
- Reversibility: if it is exposed, does the user lose trust they cannot rebuild?

## Diagnostic questions
- Who decides whether the user gets the next level, and what has each of those people seen of the user's work in the last 90 days?
- Can the user's manager state the user's top contributions in terms of the manager's own goals?
- Is the user's unit on the company's critical path, and is its power rising or falling?
- What neglected but valued work could the user take on that would put them in front of decision-makers?
- What has the user wanted but not asked for? What stopped them?
- Which critical-path relationship is the user avoiding because they dislike or disapprove of the person?
- Which groups does the user connect that would not otherwise be connected?
- In recent executive meetings, did the user open with their claim or with context and caveats? How many apologies?
- What did the last three people promoted to the target level visibly do in their final year before promotion?
- Is the problem under-assertion (Pfeffer's cure applies) or over-assertion (Goldsmith's cure applies)?
- What assumptions about fairness or merit is the user relying on that the evidence in this organization does not support?
- If the user gained the scope, what price would they pay, and do they want it?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating with appropriate power at the next level):
- The manager and skip-level describe the user's contributions accurately and unprompted, in business terms.
- The user makes explicit asks for scope and resources at a steady rate, and most are granted.
- The user is invited to forums above their level (QBRs, AI governance, board prep) because they hold information or a resource.
- Peers from other functions route cross-org questions through the user, a sign of brokerage.
- In reviews, the user states a recommendation in the first sentence and holds it under challenge without apologizing.
- Third parties (engineering leads, customers, finance) cite the user's wins.
- Early in a new role, the user makes a few decisive moves and converts at least one skeptic into an owner.

Negative (current-level or below):
- The user relies on the manager to relay their work upward and does not know how the skip-level sees them.
- Status updates are task lists in the user's framing, not outcomes in the boss's framing.
- The user frequently apologizes, hedges or downplays in written and spoken communication.
- The user says "I don't do politics" while being blindsided by reorgs or roadmap decisions.
- The user's network is dense inside their own team and thin elsewhere.
- The user avoids a critical stakeholder because they dislike them.
- After being passed over, the user explains it only as unfairness, with no model of the evaluation process.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Treating Pfeffer as permission to manipulate: insincere flattery, spinning metrics, hoarding information for leverage, undermining a rival. These violate the system's legitimacy filter and usually fail over repeated interactions.
- Performing dominance by copying findings on anger, interrupting or rule-breaking. In most product organizations this reads as immaturity and destroys peer trust.
- Hoarding credit in the name of branding. The fix Pfeffer implies is visibility, not theft.
- Networking and optics substituted for delivery (Doshi's warning about organizations that reward optics as the goal).
- Chasing whichever unit is hottest without a real role in it.
- Turning the realism into cynicism or grievance ("it's all politics") that stops the user from acting, which is the just-world trap in reverse.
- A coach using Pfeffer to excuse genuinely weak performance, or skipping the capability check in attribution-analysis.
- Over-investing upward while neglecting reports and peers, which eventually shows in 360s and skip-levels.
- Copying founder-style rule-breaking (for example, shipping before governance review) in AI contexts where one incident can end a career.
- Applying averaged lab findings as rules without checking the local culture, for example a candor culture where spin is punished.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Campbell (trust and team-first):** Campbell says executives reward trust, integrity, loyalty and team-first behavior. Pfeffer says performance and likability are overrated and that visibility, relationships and power decide outcomes. Campbell is more right where leadership is stable, performance is legible, and senior leaders actively coach, as in his own settings. Pfeffer is more right in politicized organizations, during leadership turnover, and where credit is contested. Note that Campbell was himself a powerful network broker, which is a Pfeffer mechanism. The coach diagnoses the environment first.
- **Scott (Radical Candor):** Scott names insincere, political behavior "manipulative insincerity," a failure mode. Pfeffer reports that flattery works and that lying is common among successful leaders. Reconcile through the legitimacy filter: specific, sincere praise (which Scott endorses) gets most of the upside without the corrosion. Scott is more right within manager–report and peer relationships over time. Pfeffer describes how upward and outside evaluators actually form impressions.
- **Horowitz (minimize politics):** Horowitz tells CEOs to design promotion, compensation and reorganization processes so that politicking does not pay. Pfeffer tells individuals that politics is inevitable and must be navigated. Each is right at its own level. When the user leads an organization, Horowitz governs how they build it. As a participant in someone else's organization, Pfeffer describes the terrain. In a well-run, process-heavy organization, lobbying outside the process backfires.
- **Lencioni (vulnerability-based trust):** Lencioni asks leaders to show weakness to build team trust. Research Pfeffer cites finds that higher-status people who disclose weakness in task settings lose influence. Lencioni fits a stable peer team with shared goals and a strong leader. Pfeffer fits evaluative, competitive or first-impression settings. The user should calibrate vulnerability by audience.
- **Goldsmith (What Got You Here Won't Get You There):** Goldsmith's derailing habits include claiming credit you don't deserve, winning too much, and speaking when angry, which are roughly what some Pfeffer tactics produce when overused. They treat opposite failure modes. Pfeffer treats the under-assertive and invisible; Goldsmith treats the over-assertive and already-successful. Attribution-analysis decides which cure applies.
- **Hewlett (sponsorship and presence):** They agree that performance is insufficient and visibility is essential. Hewlett's sponsorship is Pfeffer's network-plus-power logic made reciprocal (performance and loyalty in exchange for advocacy). They diverge on emotional display: Hewlett's top gravitas trait is confidence and grace under fire, and losing composure is a blunder, while Pfeffer cites anger as a dominance signal. For rising executives, composure is the safer default.
- **Gabarro and Kotter (managing your boss):** They agree the boss relationship is critical. Gabarro and Kotter build it on mutual dependence, compatible working styles, dependability and honesty, not ingratiation. The coach uses their method to carry out Pfeffer's diagnosis.
- **Ibarra:** Both reject a fixed, "true self" notion of authenticity, and both prize networks. Ibarra's reason is learning and identity growth ("outsight"); Pfeffer's is power. Ibarra treats authenticity as something that evolves, not a myth. The coach uses Ibarra to frame Pfeffer's behavioral stretches as experiments with a provisional self rather than as a mask.
- **Duke (resulting):** Rule 7 is resulting at the social level: observers judge by outcomes. Duke warns decision-makers not to. Use both. Duke governs how the user judges their own decisions; Pfeffer predicts how others will judge them.
- **Watkins (First 90 Days):** They agree on early wins and coalition-building. Watkins insists on diagnosing the situation first. Pfeffer's "use power quickly" is right in a turnaround or an inherited mandate. In a realignment or sustaining-success situation, Watkins's pacing is right.
- **Grove:** Grove (by reference) separates position power from knowledge power in knowledge organizations. For an AI PM, deep technical and market knowledge is a real power base, which Pfeffer would count as a resource. The coach should not let Pfeffer's emphasis on perception crowd out building real expertise.
- **McCord and Cagan:** In candor cultures (Netflix as McCord describes it) and product organizations that run on outcomes (Cagan), spin is detected and punished, and trust comes from results and product judgment. Pfeffer's tactics need the most restraint there.
- **Doshi:** Doshi treats optics as legitimate work that becomes dysfunctional when organizations reward it as the goal. He chose to step off the executive track when optics came to dominate the job. Pfeffer would say that leaves power on the table. The coach makes the user choose consciously.

## Limits and biases
- Evidence mix: Pfeffer draws on large bodies of social-science research, but many individual findings come from lab or survey studies, averaged across contexts, and some have not replicated (power posing). Treat each tactic as a hypothesis to test locally.
- Survivorship: vivid cases (Lyndon Johnson, Stanton, Valenti, successful rule-breakers) are selected on success. Rule 7 especially ignores people who broke rules and disappeared.
- Context: elite U.S. MBA students and large or political organizations; examples lean toward politics, finance, healthcare and media, with few from modern product companies. Tech companies with strong engineering cultures may punish visible power plays more heavily.
- Ethics: reviewers (for example Charter's review of 7 Rules) note that he argues more good people should hold power but spends little effort on the harms of the tactics. This system supplies the missing boundary.
- Identity: many tactics (anger, self-promotion, rule-breaking, interrupting) carry different backlash for women and people of color. His syllabus includes a session on women and power, but the per-tactic guidance is mostly group-neutral. Hewlett's data shows a narrower acceptable band.
- Era: 2010–2022 material. Remote and hybrid work, social-media scrutiny, and AI governance change the risk profile of rule-breaking and self-promotion.
- He is an observer and teacher, not an operator. He is strong on diagnosis and individual tactics, and weak on building high-trust teams, which Campbell, Lencioni and Hughes Johnson cover better.

## Skill mapping
| Doctrine | Skill(s) | How the skill should use it |
|---|---|---|
| D1 Performance insufficient | attribution-analysis, trajectory-model | Split stalls into capability, visibility, relationship and context before recommending more output |
| D2 Perceptions above you | managing-up, executive-trust | Maintain a perception ledger; write updates in the boss's goals; sincere appreciation only |
| D3 Get out of your own way | executive-posture, meeting-presence, executive-prose | Audit apologies and minimizers; keep calibrated uncertainty, drop status-lowering hedges |
| D4 Ask | scope-expansion, sponsorship | Convert wishes into dated asks; track the ask rate |
| D5 Break conventions | organizational-politics, decision-making | Separate conventions from integrity rules; never breach governance, law or data honesty |
| D6 Where to work | trajectory-model, scope-expansion, enterprise-thinking | Run the unit power scan before moves and re-scoping |
