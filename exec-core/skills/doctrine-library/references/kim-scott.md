# Kim Scott

## Why this source matters here
Scott ran sales and operations teams for AdSense, YouTube, and DoubleClick at Google, then helped design management courses at Apple University and coached tech CEOs before writing Radical Candor (2017, fully revised 2019) and Just Work (2021). Her doctrine was formed in large, well-resourced, relatively high-trust tech companies, from the seat of a manager of managers; she was at Google in the same years Bill Campbell coached its leaders. Its value here is procedural rather than inspirational: a concrete technique for extracting criticism, which is the raw input for attribution analysis; a safe sequence for giving feedback upward; and a model of growth trajectories that stops a coach (or the user as manager) from assuming everyone should climb. Just Work is her own correction: candor costs more for some people than others, and the coach must account for that before prescribing directness.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Care personally and challenge directly at the same time; the quadrants label moments, not people
- **Claim:** Good guidance requires both dimensions at once. Challenge without care is obnoxious aggression ("front-stabbing"). Care without challenge is ruinous empathy. Neither is manipulative insincerity: flattery to the face, criticism behind the back, the quadrant Scott explicitly calls political and passive-aggressive, driven by wanting to be liked, seeking advantage, or fatigue. Her site insists the quadrants describe behaviors anyone slips into, not personality types.
- **Source:** Radical Candor, part I; radicalcandor.com/our-approach.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach uses the quadrants to debrief specific interactions ("in Tuesday's roadmap review, which quadrant were you in with the data lead?") rather than to label the user. It also reads other people: manipulative insincerity is the observable signature of political actors (praise in the meeting, objections raised later through someone else). When the user reports "they said it was great, then it died," the coach tests for that pattern before assuming the idea was weak.
- **Boundary conditions:** The quadrant is judged by intent and effect together, and intent is invisible to observers; the coach should label behavior only from what happened next.

### D2. Get criticism before you give it, with a specific technique
- **Claim:** Candor starts with soliciting criticism, from the boss as well as from reports and peers. Her procedure: have a go-to question you can ask naturally (hers asks what she could do, or stop doing, to make it easier to work with her; she notes a CEO friend who uses a blunter version, and says to use whatever sounds like you); embrace the discomfort by staying silent and counting to six in your head; listen to understand rather than to respond; then reward the candor by fixing the problem quickly and visibly, or, when you disagree, by explaining your reasoning fully; she notes that sometimes a fuller explanation is the only reward you can offer.
- **Source:** Intercom interview (her four steps, in her words); talk transcript; Radical Candor ch. on getting guidance.
- **Changes coaching how:** This is the primary data-collection instrument for attribution analysis. The coach prescribes running it with the boss, the skip-level, and two or three peers, asking about a specific recent event ("in yesterday's pricing review, what should I have done differently?"), logging the answers, and tracking the visible fix. The act itself changes perception: an executive who sees the user solicit criticism and act on it updates their read of the user's coachability.
- **Boundary conditions:** People with power over the user, or who fear the user, may not answer honestly; triangulate. Asked too often or anxiously, the question becomes reassurance-seeking. If the user cannot act on or explain a criticism, asking again erodes trust.

### D3. Ruinous empathy is the most common failure, and it is why people do not know where they stand
- **Claim:** Scott says most management mistakes happen in ruinous empathy: people care, so they stay quiet or sugar-coat. The result is that the person who most needs the information never gets it.
- **Source:** Talk transcript; radicalcandor.com/our-approach.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user is surprised by a promotion outcome or a lukewarm review, the coach's default hypothesis is months of withheld feedback, not sudden judgment. That does not exonerate the user; it assigns them the job of extracting the information (D2). For the user as manager, the coach asks whether each report could state, today, how the user rates them and why.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some silence is strategic rather than empathetic (the boss has already decided and is avoiding conflict); the coach should consider both.

### D4. Upward candor: earn the right, ask permission, start small
- **Claim:** Before criticizing a boss, solicit their criticism of you, and praise sincerely and specifically so your intent is clear. Then ask permission, in effect whether it would help to hear your view on a specific thing, start with something small, deliver it privately and soon after the event, and watch the response before going further. Her company's CORE model structures the content: context, observation, result, next step. If a boss repeatedly punishes candor, Scott's advice is blunt: start looking elsewhere.
- **Source:** Scott, LinkedIn post (2023); radicalcandor.com upward-feedback post; Radical Candor podcast S1E8 page.
- **Changes coaching how:** For managing up, the coach sequences upward feedback: solicit, praise, ask, small item, observe, escalate only if the first item landed well. Executives are chronically short of accurate information about their own effect; a subordinate who can give specific, private, well-timed feedback becomes more valuable to them. The coach also treats the boss's reaction to the first small item as data about the boss, for the trajectory model.
- **Boundary conditions:** Candor to a boss's boss that bypasses the boss, or in public, is a different act with different risks. In politicized environments (Pfeffer) upward criticism can be career-limiting even when well delivered.

### D5. Candor is measured at the listener's ear
- **Claim:** What counts is how the feedback was heard, not what was intended; the giver's job is to gauge the reaction and adjust. Scott recommends delivering both praise and criticism in person where possible so you can see the reaction.
- **Source:** radicalcandor.com gauging posts; Intercom interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** The user's self-report ("I was direct but kind") is not evidence. The coach asks what the recipient did next and whether the relationship improved. In executive meetings, the coach asks the user to recount how the room reacted, not what they said.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some listeners will hear any criticism as an attack; gauging does not mean withholding.

### D6. Praise is guidance and should be as specific as criticism
- **Claim:** Scott argues praise and criticism both need care and challenge. Specific praise tells people what to keep doing and, in public, shows a whole team what good looks like; she rejects the idea that specific praise makes people complacent. Vague praise is closer to ruinous empathy or insincerity.
- **Source:** Intercom interview; radicalcandor.com/our-approach.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user praises their team to executives, the coach pushes for specifics: who did what, and why it mattered to the business. That doubles as visibility for the team and as evidence of the user's judgment. Upward, specific sincere praise to the boss is part of candor; generic praise to a boss reads as flattery.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some people and cultures experience public praise as exposure; ask how they prefer it.

### D7. Rock star or superstar: growth trajectory is a current state, not a verdict on potential
- **Claim:** Scott separates performance from growth trajectory. Rock stars are excellent and on a gradual trajectory: masters of their work who do not want the boss's job. Superstars are on a steep trajectory and will go stir-crazy doing the same job for long. Teams need both. She replaced "potential" with "growth trajectory" because "potential" implies a permanent judgment, and she stresses people shift modes with life circumstances. She admits to having undervalued rock stars and warns that managers in superstar mode push everyone toward it.
- **Source:** Radical Candor, growth chapter; radicalcandor.com growth-stability-career and team-growth-and-stability; podcast S4E1.
- **Changes coaching how:** Three uses. First, the user's own trajectory: the coach asks rather than assumes whether the user is in superstar mode now and whether their life can sustain it for the next 18 months; the system's executive-track premise should not override the answer. Second, the user as manager: map reports on performance and trajectory and plan differently for each; promoting a rock star into management by default is a manager error. Third, managing up: a boss may value the user as a rock star (relied on, not developed); the coach tests for this by asking whether the boss has ever discussed the user's next role unprompted.
- **Boundary conditions:** Trajectory is partly shaped by opportunity; people denied stretch work can look like rock stars. Check for that before accepting the label, particularly for people affected by bias (D10).

### D8. Career conversations: life story, dreams, 18-month plan
- **Claim:** Scott adopted Russ Laraway's three-conversation method from Google: learn a person's life story and the reasons behind their choices, then their long-range dreams, then build an 18-month plan with concrete steps toward those dreams. The conversations recur; dreams change.
- **Source:** Radical Candor, growth chapter; radicalcandor.com how-to-have-career-conversations.
- **Changes coaching how:** The user should run these with every report (managerial leverage and talent). Upward, the user can volunteer their own dream and 18-month plan to the boss unasked. A boss cannot sponsor a direction they do not know, and most never ask.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a low-trust relationship, disclosing dreams that lie outside the company or the boss's area can backfire; stage what is shared.

### D9. The Get Stuff Done wheel: listen, clarify, debate, decide, persuade, implement, learn
- **Claim:** Collective work cycles through seven steps. Listen to the team's ideas; clarify them, because new ideas are fragile and easily crushed; debate ideas rather than egos; decide, ideally by the people closest to the facts rather than the most senior, and with facts rather than recommendations, since recommendations harden into positions people defend; persuade those who were not in the room; implement; learn. Her company advises separate meetings for debate and for decision so everyone knows when discussion ends.
- **Source:** Radical Candor, part II; radicalcandor.com decisions-to-get-shit-done.
- **Changes coaching how:** When an initiative stalls, the coach asks which step it is stuck on. PMs commonly skip clarify (a half-formed idea gets shot down in a big forum) and persuade (assuming a decision carries itself to people who were absent). In executive meetings, the user should know whether the meeting is for debate or decision and behave accordingly: exploratory in the first, crisp in the second.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a hierarchy where executives do decide, pushing decisions to the facts means the user brings facts that make the decision obvious, not that the user takes the decision.

### D10. Bias changes who can afford candor
- **Claim:** Scott acknowledged, after a Black woman CEO told her Radical Candor was harder for her to practice, that the framework carries unequal costs. Women who challenge directly are labeled abrasive for behavior praised in men; men withhold criticism from women, starving them of guidance. Just Work separates bias (unconscious; respond with an "I" statement inviting reflection), prejudice (a consciously held belief; respond with an "it" statement naming the line), and bullying (intentional harm; respond with a "you" statement that puts the spotlight on the person). Layered with power, these become discrimination and harassment.
- **Source:** "Thoughts on Gender and Radical Candor," First Round Review; Just Work, via First Round interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user receives style feedback ("too aggressive," "needs polish," "not executive enough"), the coach runs a swap test (would the same behavior from someone of a different gender or background get the same label?) before treating it as a capability gap. The coach also checks whether vague feedback reflects others' ruinous empathy toward the user. As a leader, the user should be an upstander and set shared language for flagging bias.
- **Boundary conditions:** The swap test is a check, not a verdict; some style feedback is accurate regardless of who receives it.

### D11. Collect guidance about managers on their teams' behalf
- **Claim:** Scott used skip-level "manager guidance" sessions in which a manager's team names one or two things their boss should do differently, compiled and shared with the manager.
- **Source:** First Round Review (2015).
- **Changes coaching how:** At director level (managing managers), this becomes a managerial-leverage mechanism for the user. It also tells the user their own boss's boss may be collecting this about them; the coach asks what the user's team would say in such a session.
- **Boundary conditions:** Requires trust and prior agreement with the manager; sprung as a surprise it undermines them.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Soliciting-criticism protocol (run with boss, skip-level, 2-3 peers each quarter)
1. Pick a recent, specific event with the person present.
2. Ask a go-to question in your own words, about that event or about working with you in general.
3. Stay silent; count to six in your head. Do not fill the silence with explanation.
4. Listen to understand; ask one follow-up for an example.
5. Thank them without defending.
6. Within a week, make a visible change or explain in full why not; tell them which.
7. Log: who, what was said, the change made, and whether the next conversation got more or less candid.

### Upward feedback ladder
| Step | Action | Proceed only if |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Solicit the boss's criticism of you; act on it | You have visibly acted on at least one item |
| 2 | Give specific, sincere praise | It is true and specific |
| 3 | Ask permission for a perspective | They say yes |
| 4 | Offer one small item (context, observation, result, next step), privately, soon after the event | They engage rather than defend |
| 5 | Raise larger items | Steps 1-4 have gone well at least twice |
| Stop | Repeated punishment of candor | Treat as data about the boss; reassess the role |

### Quadrant self-audit after an important meeting
- Where did I challenge without showing care?
- Where did I stay silent or soften to be liked?
- Did I say anything about someone that I would not say to them?
- What did the recipient do next (listener's ear)?

### Team growth map (for the user as manager)
| | Gradual trajectory now | Steep trajectory now |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent performance | Rock star: recognize mastery, protect from default promotion into management, give them influence as a teacher or expert | Superstar: new challenges, bigger scope, plan for succession since they will move on |
| Weak performance | Test fit, clarity, and skill; if persistent, change role or part ways | Check for wrong role, new-to-role, or life circumstances before judging |
Quadrant labels for the weak-performance row are this dossier's paraphrase; exact book labels not verified. Revisit the map every half; people move.

### GSD stuck-point diagnostic
For a stalled initiative, ask in order: Did we listen to the people closest to the problem? Is the idea clear enough to survive debate? Was there a real debate, about ideas not egos? Who decides, and do they have facts rather than positions? Have the people not in the room been persuaded? Is implementation owned? What did we learn, and did it change the next cycle?

### Bias check before a capability attribution
1. Write down the feedback verbatim.
2. Swap test: same behavior, different person; same label?
3. Is the feedback specific enough to act on? If not, whose ruinous empathy is producing the vagueness?
4. Seek one outside perspective from someone who saw the same event.
5. Only then classify it as capability, visibility, perception, or context.

## Diagnostic questions
- When did the user last ask their boss for criticism, and what did they change as a result?
- Could the user state, today, the one thing their boss most wants them to do differently?
- In the user's last difficult conversation, which quadrant were they in, judged by what the other person did next?
- Who around the user shows manipulative insincerity (agreeable in the meeting, opposed afterward)? How is the user handling it?
- Has the user's boss ever discussed the user's next role without being asked? If not, is the boss treating the user as a rock star?
- Is the user in superstar mode right now, by their own honest account of the next 18 months?
- Has the user told their boss their long-range dream and 18-month plan?
- Which reports on the user's team are rock stars being pushed toward management by default?
- Where in the GSD cycle is the user's most important initiative stuck?
- Is the feedback the user is getting specific, or is someone being ruinously empathetic with them?
- Would the same "style" feedback be given to someone of a different gender or background?
- How specific was the user's last piece of praise for their team in an executive forum?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating at the next level, per this source):
- Asks senior people for criticism about specific events, stays quiet while they answer, and returns within a week with a visible change.
- Gives the boss private, specific feedback soon after an event, framed by context, observation, and impact.
- Praises team members to executives with concrete detail about the business effect.
- Knows whether a meeting is for debate or decision and adjusts.
- Runs career conversations with reports; can describe each report's trajectory and plan.
- Raises disagreement in the room rather than after it.

Negative (current-level or below):
- "Any feedback for me?" asked at the end of a meeting, with nothing changing afterwards.
- Defends or explains before the feedback-giver has finished.
- Complains about the boss to peers but has never told the boss.
- Praise that is generic ("great job, team") in executive updates.
- Surprised by review outcomes; relies on the boss to volunteer where they stand.
- Assumes every strong report wants a promotion; pushes the team to match the user's own ambition.
- Kills or ships ideas without a clarify step; assumes decisions persuade themselves.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- "In the spirit of radical candor" used as cover for rudeness; Scott's site calls that what it is, being a jerk. The HBO series Silicon Valley parodied exactly this misuse.
- Criticism first, soliciting never. The order matters; Scott's rule is to prove you can take it before dishing it out.
- Treating upward candor as a one-shot act of courage rather than a relationship built step by step.
- Using the quadrants to label colleagues ("she's a ruinous empathy person") instead of behaviors.
- Coaches misapplying the rock star/superstar split as a fixed talent category, or assuming the user must be a superstar because the system is an executive-track coach.
- Soliciting feedback and then not acting or explaining, which teaches people that candor is wasted.
- Prescribing the same directness to users who face double standards, without the bias check.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Pfeffer (power):** Scott assumes candor, delivered with care, builds trust and results. Pfeffer holds that flattering the boss and making them look good is often what advances careers, and that truth-telling upward is risky. Scott is more right with bosses who have shown they reward candor and in cultures that value it; Pfeffer is more right with insecure bosses, in politicized organizations, and in public forums. The upward ladder (D4) is the bridge: test candor with small items and treat the response as evidence.
- **McCord (honesty):** Both demand honesty; they differ on what it serves. Scott anchors it in caring about the individual; McCord anchors it in the business's future needs. On growth, Scott protects rock stars as stable excellence; McCord's own example of a skilled QA engineer whose job was automated away is a rock star whose craft stopped fitting the plan. For an AI PM whose team's roles are being reshaped by automation, McCord's lens decides, and Scott's governs how it is done.
- **Campbell:** Convergent; the Trillion Dollar Coach authors cite Radical Candor. Scott supplies the missing warning that Campbell's bluntness, copied without his relationships, lands as obnoxious aggression.
- **Goldsmith:** Goldsmith's feedforward asks for suggestions about the future and trains people to respond with only "thank you." Scott asks for criticism of past behavior and requires a visible response. Feedforward is gentler and better for peers or senior people who will not criticize; Scott's version yields sharper data.
- **Gabarro & Kotter:** Managing your boss as a relationship of mutual dependence, adapted to the boss's style, is compatible with the upward ladder; they add the discipline of diagnosing the boss's pressures before offering feedback.
- **Charan:** The leadership pipeline assumes progression through passages; Scott insists many excellent people should not be pushed up. Charan is more useful for the user's own passage; Scott for how the user manages a mixed team.
- **Hewlett (sponsorship):** A superstar trajectory that is not communicated does not get sponsored. Scott's career conversations are the mechanism for making it known.
- **Bezos/Amazon, Lencioni:** Disagree-and-commit maps onto debate-then-decide; Lencioni's fear of conflict and artificial harmony describe ruinous empathy at the team level.
- **Grove:** Grove's "constructive confrontation" at Intel shares the challenge-directly axis but not the explicit care axis; in a PM's hands it can drift toward obnoxious aggression.

## Limits and biases
- Context: Google and Apple in their growth years, with high trust, abundant resources, and strong managers. Candor is cheaper where layoffs are rare and performance is legible.
- Evidence: anecdote-driven; no controlled evidence that the quadrant model changes outcomes. Radical Candor LLC sells training, which gives the framework commercial incentives to stay simple.
- Identity: Scott herself documents that the approach carries unequal costs for women and people of color (the "abrasive" trap) and wrote Just Work partly as a correction. She also describes legal fear pushing managers into silence ("mandated manipulative insincerity").
- Culture: directness norms vary widely across countries and companies; her company has published guidance on cultural differences, which is a concession that one calibration does not fit all.
- Power: the upward advice assumes a boss who will not retaliate. Scott's own fallback, leave, is not always available.
- Naming: the word "radical" invited the misreading she now spends effort correcting; the revised edition reportedly leans on "compassionate candor" language in places (attribution uncertain).
