# Barbara Minto

## Why this source matters here
Minto joined McKinsey in 1963, the firm's first female MBA hire (Harvard Business School 1963), and moved to London in 1966, where she stayed until 1973. While editing consultants' reports she noticed she kept rearranging their ideas into the same shape. She formalized that shape as the Pyramid Principle and taught it within McKinsey and later to other consultancies, corporations and government offices. Her site describes the textbook as a standard text at the major consulting firms. She is credited with naming MECE. The context is consultant-to-client reporting, where a busy senior reader has commissioned an answer and needs to absorb it fast. That transfers almost directly to a PM's upward communication: status updates, escalations, decision requests, funding asks, board pre-reads. It transfers less well to exploratory writing, team working documents, persuading readers who never asked the question, and cultures that expect context before conclusions. For a PM moving toward Director and VP, the pyramid is the default form for anything sent upward, and failing its tests is one of the most visible signs of below-level work.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Lead with the governing thought
- **Claim:** A reader grasps your thinking most easily when the ideas arrive top-down: one summarizing point first, then the supporting ideas grouped under it. A reader given the details first has to guess where you are heading and hold unconnected items in memory. Minto invokes the limit on how many items the mind can hold at once, often cited as Miller's "seven plus or minus two." The writer usually thinks bottom-up (facts, then groupings, then insights) but must present top-down.
- **Source:** Pyramid Principle Part One ("Why a pyramid structure?"); barbaraminto.com home and /concept.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach reads the first two sentences of any upward document or opening statement. Does it state the recommendation, conclusion or ask? If the user cannot write the governing thought in one sentence, the problem is unfinished thinking, and the coach sends them back to the analysis rather than to wordsmithing.
- **Boundary conditions:** When there is no answer yet, the governing thought becomes the current state of knowledge plus the decision or help needed. When a hostile reader needs shared diagnosis first, or a culture expects context first, see D11.

### D2. The reader's question determines the structure
- **Claim:** A document exists to answer a question in the reader's mind. The pyramid is a question-and-answer dialogue: the governing thought raises a question (why, how, which, is it true?), the key line directly beneath answers it, each key-line point raises the next question down, and so on until the reader has no questions left. Her course's second part is identifying the reader's question and then using the rules to discover and organize the points.
- **Source:** Pyramid Principle ch. 2 (vertical relationship); barbaraminto.com/course.
- **Changes coaching how:** Before drafting, the coach asks who exactly the reader is, what question they are asking or should be asking, and what they will do with the answer. Typical executive questions: What should we do? Should we fund this? Are we on track, and what do you need from me? What happened, how bad is it, and what are you doing about it? Which option, and why? A draft that answers a question the reader isn't asking gets rewritten from the top.
- **Boundary conditions:** Several readers may have different questions (the board, the operating executives, the working team). Use separate documents, or a layered summary per audience, rather than one pyramid serving everyone badly.

### D3. Introduce with Situation, Complication, Question; then Answer
- **Claim:** The introduction tells a short story the reader already accepts, then turns it. Situation: stable, uncontroversial context. Complication: what changed or created tension. Question: what that raises. Answer: the governing thought. The introduction reminds the reader rather than informing them, stays brief, and contains nothing the reader would dispute. Minto's book gives standard introduction patterns for recurring document types (directive, request for funds, explaining how to, choosing among alternatives; recalled from the book) and alternative orders that lead with the answer, the complication or the question (labels by reference).
- **Source:** Pyramid Principle chs. 2 and 4; barbaraminto.com (the SCQ framework).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach rewrites escalations and updates into SCQA. For example: Situation, we committed to launch the AI assistant to enterprise tenants in Q3. Complication, the evaluation pass rate on regulated-industry prompts is 81% against a 95% bar. Question, do we slip or narrow scope? Answer, launch to non-regulated tenants on date and gate regulated tenants behind the eval bar, needing a decision from you by Friday. For boards, the situation is the strategy the board already approved.
- **Boundary conditions:** If the situation itself is contested, SCQ breaks down: you first need a document that establishes the shared facts, which is a different question. An introduction longer than a short paragraph has become throat-clearing.

### D4. Vertical logic: each level summarizes the level below
- **Claim:** Ideas at any level must be summaries of the ideas grouped below them. You cannot derive a summary from a grouping unless the ideas below belong together and appear in a logical order. Read the top point, ask the question it raises, and check that the points beneath answer exactly that question: nothing missing, nothing off-question.
- **Source:** Pyramid Principle ch. 1 (the three rules) and ch. 2.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach runs a "so what / why / how" check on every section heading against its content, and on every slide title against its body. Supporting points that do not answer the heading's question are cut or moved.
- **Boundary conditions:** The strict pyramid suits documents whose job is to transfer a conclusion. Reference material (specs, runbooks) is organized for lookup, not argument.

### D5. Horizontal logic: group only ideas of the same kind, in a defensible order, and prefer induction at the key line
- **Claim:** Ideas in a grouping must be the same kind of idea: you should be able to name them with one plural noun such as reasons, steps, problems or recommendations. They must also be in a logical order. There are four sources of order: deductive (argument order), chronological (steps or cause-and-effect sequence), structural (the parts of an existing whole, such as org units, regions or process stages), and comparative (ranked by degree or importance). A deductive group runs a statement, a comment on it, and the implication. An inductive group is several like ideas that support a generalization. Minto prefers inductive groupings at the key line, because deduction makes the reader walk through the reasoning before reaching the point. She keeps deductive chains short, at about four steps at most (recalled from the book).
- **Source:** Pyramid Principle chs. 1, 5 ("Deduction and induction: the difference") and 6 ("Imposing logical order").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach flags lists that mix actions with observations, lists whose order is arbitrary, and key lines that are long deductive chains. It asks "what single plural noun covers these?" and "why is this item first?" For decision memos it pushes the options into a comparative order with explicit criteria.
- **Boundary conditions:** A skeptical technical reviewer may need to see the deductive chain explicitly. Leading with it is a legitimate choice when proving the case matters more than speed of uptake.

### D6. Make groupings mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, and count what you claim
- **Claim:** A grouping should not overlap internally and should cover the whole of what it claims to cover (MECE). Minto names MECE as her invention from her McKinsey work while tracing the idea back to Aristotle. Her course includes "critical grouping analysis": if you say there are three reasons, there must be exactly three.
- **Source:** barbaraminto.com/course; McKinsey Alumni interview (as reported); Pyramid Principle ch. 6.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks the user's option sets for overlap and for missing standard options: do nothing, delay, build, buy, partner, narrow scope. It checks risk lists for double-counting and for whole missing categories (regulatory, reputational, cost-to-serve, model drift). It verifies that stated counts ("three reasons," "four risks") match the content.
- **Boundary conditions:** Real categories overlap, and forcing MECE can distort the analysis. Critics note that MECE does not exclude irrelevant items and rules out redundancy even where redundancy helps, for example repeating the key risk in a safety context. Exhaustiveness is only relative to the question.

### D7. Replace intellectually blank headings with the insight
- **Claim:** A summarizing point must state what the grouping means, not announce that a grouping exists. "There are three problems," "Findings," or "Key considerations" says nothing. Minto's method has you group similar facts, draw the insight from seeing the similarity, then group related insights. For action ideas, the summary states the result achieved by taking the actions. For situational ideas, it states what they have in common or what they imply. Vague action verbs ("improve," "strengthen," "leverage") signal that the thinking stopped early, and the fix is to specify the measurable end state.
- **Source:** Pyramid Principle ch. 7 ("Summarizing grouped ideas"); barbaraminto.com/concept; course part 5 (action ideas that "provoke action").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach rewrites topic headings and slide titles into assertions. "Churn analysis" becomes "Churn is concentrated in SMB accounts onboarded without the assistant." Action items become outcomes: "Cut eval turnaround from five days to one so we can ship weekly." A deck should pass a title-only read: the argument survives if you read nothing but the titles.
- **Boundary conditions:** Navigation labels in appendices or reference sections can stay as topics. An assertive heading must be supported underneath; confident claims without evidence are worse than topic labels (see evidence-discipline).

### D8. Unclear writing is usually unclear thinking; use the rules to find out what you think
- **Claim:** Minto presents the pyramid as a way to organize and discover your thinking, not only a way to format it. Her site says the rules help you discover and clarify the points you want to make. Building bottom-up (group, then find the insight, then group again) and testing top-down exposes gaps, non-sequiturs and conclusions that don't follow from the evidence.
- **Source:** barbaraminto.com home, /concept, /textbook.
- **Changes coaching how:** Before a high-stakes meeting, the coach asks the user for a five-line pyramid: the governing thought plus a key line of two to four points, each with one piece of evidence. The coach then attacks it. A pyramid that won't come together means the user is not ready for the meeting, whatever the deck looks like.
- **Boundary conditions:** Early discovery benefits from divergence. Forcing a pyramid too early locks in a hypothesis prematurely. Use the pyramid to converge, not to explore.

### D9. Define the problem before solving or writing about it
- **Claim:** The 1996 edition adds logic in problem solving. Define the problem by the starting situation, the disturbing event, the current undesired result (R1) and the desired result (R2); the question becomes how to get from R1 to R2. Structure the analysis (diagnostic frameworks, logic trees) before collecting data. Her course's fourth part covers documents that must explain both how a problem arose and how to resolve it. (Framework labels by reference.)
- **Source:** Minto Pyramid Principle (1996) Part Three; barbaraminto.com/course.
- **Changes coaching how:** For strategy docs and escalations, the coach has the user state R1 and R2 in measurable terms before proposing anything. For example, R1: AI feature weekly active use at 8% of eligible seats. R2: 25% by Q2 without raising cost per request. That keeps the document answering the right question ("how do we get from 8% to 25%?") rather than a pet one ("should we build agents?").
- **Boundary conditions:** Some executive problems are ill-structured. The desired result is itself contested, and the first document must surface that disagreement rather than assume an R2.

### D10. Make the pyramid visible on the page and on the screen
- **Claim:** The book's presentation part covers showing the structure through headings, numbering and indentation that mirror the key line, and on slides through titles that state the point. It also covers storyboarding the pyramid before building slides. (Details by reference.)
- **Source:** Minto Pyramid Principle (1996) Part Four.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach reviews decks and memos for three things. Do the headings reproduce the key line? Do slide titles state assertions? Does a title-only read carry the argument? It has the user storyboard titles before designing any visuals.
- **Boundary conditions:** Heavily formatted structure (numbered hierarchies, bullet trees) can look rigorous while hiding missing connections between points. This is the Amazon critique (D11).

### D11. Know when the pyramid is the wrong primary form
- **Claim (coach's synthesis, not Minto's claim):** The pyramid assumes a reader who has asked, or will accept, the question and mainly needs to absorb an answer efficiently. It is weaker when the writer is still exploring; when the reader must understand the causal texture of a situation before judging it (Amazon's narrative memos, read silently in a protected reading period); when the audience is skeptical or hostile, so that an answer up front triggers defensiveness before the diagnosis is shared; when a culture expects context before conclusions; and when bad news needs empathy framing. Minto's own toolkit covers part of this through alternative introduction orders and deductive groupings, so the conflict is narrower than it looks.
- **Source:** Coach synthesis; Bezos 2017 letter; Bezos 2004 email (by reference).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach chooses the form by the reader's state. Aligned and busy: a strict pyramid. Skeptical, with a high-stakes decision and protected reading time: a narrative memo with tenets, alternatives and objections addressed, still topped by a short answer-first summary. Exploring: a problem statement, what we know, open questions, the proposed next learning step. Hostile: establish the shared diagnosis first, then the answer.
- **Boundary conditions:** Even narrative formats benefit from a governing thought the writer can state in one sentence. The discipline survives when the format changes.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Top-down pyramid build (run for any upward document)
1. Topic: what is this about, in a few words?
2. Reader: who exactly, and what do they already know and believe?
3. Question: what is the reader's question? Write it as a question.
4. Answer: write the governing thought as one sentence. If you can't, stop and go back to the analysis.
5. Situation: one or two sentences the reader will accept without argument.
6. Complication: what changed or went wrong that makes the question live.
7. Check that the situation and complication actually raise the question and the answer resolves it.
8. Key line: what question does the answer raise (why, how, which)? List two to five answers of the same kind, in a stated order.
9. Repeat downward until the reader has no more questions.
10. Tests: vertical (each level summarizes and answers the question above); horizontal (same kind, stated order, MECE, the count matches); no blank headings; title-only read.

### Bottom-up build (when you have material but no answer)
1. List every point you believe is relevant.
2. Group points that are alike and name each group with a plural noun.
3. For each group, write the insight the grouping supports: an effect for actions, an implication for situations.
4. Group the insights and repeat until a single thought emerges.
5. Test that thought as an answer to a real reader question. If there isn't one, you have findings, not a document.

### Pyramid QA checklist
- The first two sentences contain the answer or the ask.
- The introduction is short and contains only what the reader will accept.
- Every heading and slide title is an assertion, not a topic.
- Every grouping can be named by one plural noun.
- The order of every grouping is explainable (chronological, structural, comparative, deductive).
- There is no overlap within groupings; standard options and risk categories aren't missing.
- Stated counts match the content.
- Deductive chains at the top are short, or replaced by an inductive key line.
- Action statements specify measurable end states.
- The confidence of each assertion matches its evidence.

### SCQA templates for common executive documents
| Document | Situation | Complication | Question | Answer (governing thought) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Status update | Plan and target the reader approved | Deviation (or none) and its cause | Are we on track; do you need to act? | On/off track, what we're doing, the one thing needed from the reader |
| Escalation | Commitment and owners | Blocker, impact and deadline | What should we do? | Recommended option, the trade-off, the decision needed by a date |
| Decision request | Goal and constraints | Options conflict on criteria | Which option? | Recommended option and the deciding criterion; alternatives in comparative order |
| Funding request | Strategy and current capacity | Gap blocking a valued outcome | Should we invest? | Amount, expected return, what we stop doing if funded or not |
| Incident or postmortem | Normal operation | What failed and the impact | How bad, why, what now? | Impact bounded, root cause, fixes with owners and dates |
| Board pre-read | Strategy the board endorsed | Market, technology or performance change | What does it mean for the plan? | Implication for the plan and the board input requested |

### Form selection (coach synthesis)
| Reader state | Primary form | Must still include |
|---|---|---|
| Aligned, time-poor | Strict pyramid, one page or less | Answer in the first two sentences |
| Skeptical, high-stakes, reading time protected | Narrative memo (Amazon style) | A summary paragraph up front; alternatives and objections addressed |
| Hostile to the conclusion | Shared diagnosis first, then answer | The complication stated in their terms |
| Exploring, no answer yet | Problem statement plus open questions | The decision or help needed, and by when |
| Live meeting, verbal | A 30-second answer-first opening | The key line as two or three spoken points |

## Diagnostic questions
- What question is this document answering, and is it the question the reader has?
- Can the user state the governing thought in one sentence without "and"?
- Would the reader dispute anything in the situation paragraph?
- Does each heading or slide title assert something a reader could disagree with?
- Can each list be named with one plural noun, and why is it in this order?
- Are the options MECE, and are "do nothing" or "narrow scope" missing?
- Does a title-only read of the deck carry the argument?
- Is any assertion stated more confidently than the evidence supports?
- Is the reader aligned, skeptical, hostile or exploring, and does the chosen form fit?
- When the user was cut off after 30 seconds in the last executive meeting, had they already said the point?
- Are R1 and R2 stated, and does the document answer how to get from one to the other?

## Observable signals
Positive:
- The user's upward emails and memos open with the recommendation or ask. Executives reply with a decision rather than a clarifying question.
- Slide titles are full-sentence assertions, and executives quote them back.
- The user's option sets survive challenge without a reviewer naming a missing option.
- In meetings, the user answers an executive's question in the first sentence, then offers structured support: "Yes, for two reasons..."
- The user can compress a complex AI trade-off into a governing thought and two to four supporting points on request.
- Reviewers' comments concern substance, not "what are you asking for?"

Negative:
- Documents open with background, methodology or chronology, and the ask appears on page three.
- Headings read "Background / Analysis / Findings / Next steps."
- Lists mix observations, actions and risks.
- A deck of 30 slides whose titles are topics, which cannot be understood without the presenter.
- Executives regularly respond with "so what do you need from me?" or "what's your recommendation?"
- The user answers direct questions with context before the answer.
- Recommendations use vague verbs ("improve," "optimize," "explore") with no measurable end state.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Treating the pyramid as a bullet-point format. Minto's rules apply to prose, and bullets without connecting logic are exactly the gaps her vertical test is meant to catch.
- Stating a crisp answer that the analysis does not support, to look decisive. The structure then amplifies a weak conclusion.
- Faking MECE: forcing clean boxes onto overlapping realities, or labeling a list "exhaustive" when it isn't.
- Running SCQ as a ritual: long "situation" paragraphs that retell history the reader lived through.
- Answer-first to a hostile audience with no shared diagnosis, which triggers defensiveness and closes the discussion.
- Using the pyramid to bury disagreement: dissenting data left out because it does not fit the key line. Put material counter-evidence where the reader will see it.
- Over-compression: removing the nuance, uncertainty or conditions an executive needs to decide well.
- A coach enforcing pyramid form on exploratory or team working documents, where it forces premature convergence.
- Confusing executive brevity with pyramid structure. A short message can still bury the ask.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Bezos and Amazon (narrative memos):** Amazon bans slide presentations for decision meetings in favor of narratively structured six-page memos, read silently at the start. Bezos's 2017 letter says great memos take a week or more of writing, rewriting and feedback. His 2004 argument is that narrative forces the writer to show what matters more than what and how ideas connect, which bullet slides let you skip. Where they agree: writing is a thinking discipline, and topic-bullet decks are bad. Where they differ: Amazon narratives often build context, tenets and alternatives before or alongside the recommendation, and the protected reading time removes the reader-effort problem that motivates answer-first. Minto is more right for time-poor readers, inbox communication, and decisions already framed. Amazon is more right for complex, contested decisions with a room committed to deep reading. Amazon's PR/FAQ, which starts with the press release, is itself answer-first. The best hybrid is a narrative body under a pyramid-shaped summary.
- **Doshi (question-led influence):** Doshi advises helping stakeholders reach an idea themselves through questions, so they own it. That is the opposite of answer-first. Minto fits executive updates and decision requests. Doshi fits contested changes where buy-in is the bottleneck and time allows. Doshi's point that perception runs partly on confidence also favors answer-first delivery with executives.
- **Duke (calibration):** The pyramid pushes toward crisp assertions. Duke wants beliefs expressed with calibrated uncertainty. Reconcile by making the governing thought a calibrated recommendation: "We recommend X; we're about 70% confident; the key uncertainty is Y; we'll know by Z." Minto's rules constrain structure, not confidence, and overclaiming is a misuse.
- **Larson:** Larson applies SCQA and summary-first directly to executive meetings, adding the goal of learning what executives think. They agree. Larson adds that sending an early draft to one attendee de-risks the meeting.
- **Rumelt:** Rumelt's "fluff" and blank goal statements are the strategy equivalent of Minto's intellectually blank assertions. His kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions) maps well onto a pyramid whose key line follows that sequence.
- **Grove:** Grove (by reference) valued reports mainly as self-discipline for the writer, which is compatible with Minto's view of writing as thinking.
- **Pfeffer and Hewlett (presence):** Answer-first delivery signals decisiveness and confidence, which Hewlett lists as gravitas and Pfeffer as powerful speech. Pfeffer's rhetorical devices (repetition, simple words, contrasts) persuade through delivery. Minto persuades through logical structure. Both can be used, but a strong delivery on weak structure is exposed at higher levels.
- **Cagan (product vision):** Vision and strategy narratives meant to inspire and align teams rely on story and imagery. Minto's form transfers conclusions, and it is poor at inspiration. Use a pyramid for the decision about the vision, and narrative for the vision itself.

## Limits and biases
- Origin context: developed for 1960s–70s consulting reports to clients who commissioned an answer. It assumes a reader-writer relationship with an agreed question, which internal product organizations often lack.
- Evidence: it rests on professional practice and editorial experience, consistent with basic findings on working memory and cognitive load, but it has not been tested as a method against alternatives.
- Cultural fit: answer-first is a low-context, Anglo-American business norm. In cultures and companies that expect context before conclusions, a blunt answer-first can read as presumptuous.
- MECE critiques: exhaustiveness is relative, overlaps are real, and redundancy can be useful.
- It covers written and presentational structure, not the political work of pre-wiring, choosing the audience, or timing. Pairing with Pfeffer, Hewlett and Larson is needed.
- It predates chat-first and asynchronous workplaces. Short-form channels need the principle (answer and ask first) without the full apparatus.
- The book is known to be dense and hard to read, so second-hand versions often shrink it to "SCQA plus MECE" and lose the vertical and horizontal logic tests, which are the most valuable part.
