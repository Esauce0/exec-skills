# David Maister, Charles H. Green, Robert Galford

## Why this source matters here

*The Trusted Advisor* was written about professional-services advisors earning the trust of clients, which is structurally close to a PM or product leader earning the trust of executives: the advisor has expertise, no formal authority over the client, and succeeds only when the client acts on their judgment. Its trust equation turns "they don't trust me" into four components that can each be diagnosed from evidence. The context (consulting, law, accounting) means it underweights organizational power and politics; pair it with Pfeffer.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Trust decomposes into credibility, reliability, intimacy, and self-orientation
- **Claim:** Trustworthiness = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) / Self-orientation. Credibility is about words (expertise, accuracy, honesty in what you say). Reliability is about actions over time (doing what you said, consistently). Intimacy is about safety (the other person feels secure sharing difficult things with you). Self-orientation is the degree to which you appear focused on yourself rather than on them.
- **Source:** *The Trusted Advisor*, ch. 8 (the trust equation).
- **Changes coaching how:** "The VP doesn't trust me" becomes four separate questions with different evidence and different fixes. Credibility gaps show up as challenges to the user's facts; reliability gaps as buffers added to their dates; intimacy gaps as the executive not sharing problems early; self-orientation as the executive discounting the user's recommendations when they benefit the user.
- **Boundary conditions:** a heuristic, not a measured model. Use it to structure diagnosis, not to score people.

### D2. Self-orientation is the denominator, so it dominates
- **Claim:** High self-orientation (visibly caring about one's own agenda, credit, or career; needing to look smart; not listening) divides everything else. Excellent credibility and reliability can't overcome an executive's sense that the person is working an angle.
- **Source:** *The Trusted Advisor*, trust equation chapter; Green's later essays.
- **Changes coaching how:** for an ambitious PM, self-orientation is the specific risk. The coach checks whether the user's recommendations conveniently expand the user's scope, whether they talk about their career in rooms about the business, and whether they listen or wait to talk. Links directly to Horowitz's distinction between ambition for the company and ambition for oneself, and Lencioni's humility.
- **Boundary conditions:** some self-advocacy is necessary (Pfeffer, Hewlett). The issue is appearance of self-interest in business judgment, not ever stating ambitions.

### D3. Reliability is built from many small, visible promises kept
- **Claim:** Reliability accrues from repeated experiences of the advisor doing what they said, including small things: sending the note when promised, being on time, following up.
- **Source:** *The Trusted Advisor*, trust equation chapter.
- **Changes coaching how:** the coach looks for small broken promises in the evidence log (late follow-ups, slipped dates without notice). They erode reliability faster than the user thinks, and fixing them is cheap.
- **Boundary conditions:** reliability without credibility makes someone a dependable executor, which can trap them at the current level.

### D4. Intimacy means the other person can tell you bad news safely
- **Claim:** Intimacy (in the book's sense) is the client's sense that difficult topics can be raised with you without being punished or exposed.
- **Source:** *The Trusted Advisor*.
- **Changes coaching how:** an executive sharing a problem early, before it's public, is a strong trust signal. The coach asks: does the executive bring you problems, or only tasks? And does the user react to hard news in ways that make people want to bring it again?
- **Boundary conditions:** intimacy is not friendliness. Social warmth without discretion lowers trust.

### D5. Trust develops through a sequence: engage, listen, frame, envision, commit
- **Claim:** Trusted advisors engage on the client's issue, listen to understand it as the client sees it, frame the real problem, envision a better outcome together, and then commit to action.
- **Source:** *The Trusted Advisor*, trust-building process chapters.
- **Changes coaching how:** used in meeting preparation: most PMs jump to framing and solutions before the executive feels heard. The coach builds in listening and reframing before recommendation, especially with executives the user doesn't yet have trust with.
- **Boundary conditions:** in fast operating contexts executives want the answer first (Minto, Larson). Use the full sequence for new relationships and hard problems, not for status updates.

## Frameworks in operational form

**Trust equation diagnosis.** For a specific executive:

| Component | Evidence to look for | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Credibility | Do they challenge your facts? Do they check your claims with others? | Be precise; say "I don't know"; bring the number |
| Reliability | Do they add buffers to your dates? Chase your follow-ups? | Keep small promises; flag slips before they happen |
| Intimacy | Do they share problems with you early? | Handle hard news calmly; keep confidences |
| Self-orientation | Do they discount recommendations that benefit you? | Recommend against your own interest when it's right; credit others |

## Diagnostic questions

- Which of the four components is weakest with this executive, on evidence?
- When did you last recommend something that cost your own area or scope?
- When did this executive last bring you a problem before it was public?
- What small promise to this person did you last miss?

## Observable signals

- Positive: the executive shares problems early; accepts your estimates without buffering; asks your view on things that don't benefit you.
- Negative: they verify your claims elsewhere; they add buffers; they route sensitive matters around you.

## Anti-patterns and misuse

- Treating the equation as arithmetic.
- Mistaking intimacy for personal closeness.
- Suppressing all ambition to minimize self-orientation, which makes sponsorship impossible.

## Tensions with other sources

- **Pfeffer:** argues that self-promotion and projecting power help careers, and that being liked is overrated. Maister implies visible self-interest destroys trust. Both can be right: self-promotion can win visibility and advancement while lowering the specific trust that leads executives to delegate ambiguity. The coach separates the two goals.
- **Campbell and Lencioni:** broadly aligned (trust from caring about the team and admitting weakness).

## Limits and biases

Consulting context; client relationships rather than hierarchy. No large-sample empirical validation of the equation. Treat as a diagnostic structure.
