# Claire Hughes Johnson

## Why this source matters here
Hughes Johnson spent about ten years at Google in sales and operations leadership (Gmail, Google Apps, AdWords, and later the self-driving car project), then was Stripe's COO from 2014 to 2021, while Stripe grew from under 200 to over 7,000 people. Scaling People (Stripe Press, 2023) and its free workbook set out how she built Stripe's company "operating system" and how managers should run their own. Her doctrine was formed as a COO with authority to install company-wide systems in a well-funded, founder-led, writing-heavy hypergrowth company. For a PM, the transferable part is working that way inside their own scope: chartering their area, making decision rights explicit, writing candid business reviews, and publishing how they work. Senior leaders read these as evidence that someone can run a larger system without being watched.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Make the implicit explicit: leadership at scale is building an operating system others run
- **Claim:** As an organization grows, how work gets done has to be written down: why the team exists, what it is trying to achieve, how success is measured, who owns what, and on what cadence progress is reviewed. She calls the set founding documents plus operating system plus operating cadence. It should repeat at every level, with team missions laddering to the company's. One of her four operating principles is to come back to your operating system, rather than improvising a new fix each time.
- **Source:** Scaling People, ch. 2; Workbooks, "Organizational Foundations"; Stripe Press launch post.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks whether the user's area could run for a month without them. It asks the user to fill in the Organizational Foundations table (mission, objectives with owner, key metrics, accountability mechanisms, operating cadence) for their scope. Blanks are the gap between PM-level and Director-level operation. When the user describes a recurring fire, the coach asks which part of the system is missing, not just how to put out this one. (managerial-leverage, organizational-design)
- **Boundary conditions:** She also warns against piling on mandatory processes, and against confusing forward-looking processes with defensive controls added after a past failure. Early discovery work needs lighter systems than a mature business line.

### D2. Build self-awareness in order to build mutual awareness
- **Claim:** Management starts with knowing your own values, work-style preferences, and innate versus acquired strengths and gaps, then sharing them so colleagues can work with you. Her exercises include narrowing ten values to three (adapted from Stan Slap), placing yourself on a work-style grid, and separating innate from acquired strengths. She also suggests marking calendar days as good or bad to see which activities give or drain energy.
- **Source:** Scaling People, ch. 1; Workbooks, ch. 1 exercises; Newcomer podcast.
- **Changes coaching how:** Before advising on a conflict with a manager or peer, the coach asks what the user knows about their own defaults (decision speed, conflict style, need for data) and whether the other person knows them. When feedback surprises the user, it is treated as a possible self-awareness gap before it is treated as unfair. (executive-posture, managing-up, attribution-analysis)
- **Boundary conditions:** Introspection can become a substitute for acting and changing (see Tensions: Ibarra). Self-assessments and personality tools are rough instruments.

### D3. Publish a working-with-me document, and ask for your manager's
- **Claim:** Leaders should write down how they operate: cadence of 1:1s and reviews, what to loop them in on, how they decide, how they give and receive feedback, their communication channels, what makes them impatient. Otherwise people learn these rules by breaking them. Her own "Working with Claire" says, for example, that she can be slow to decide so urgent decisions must be flagged, that she will usually hand a question back with "what do you want to do?", that deadlines should be renegotiated before they pass, and that anyone who vents to her about a colleague will be helped to tell that colleague directly. Stripe's onboarding asks new leaders to write one by about week nine.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 3 ("Working with Claire", "Working with Me Template", "New Leader Experience"); High Growth Handbook interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** For managing up, the coach asks the user to reconstruct their manager's implicit working-with-me rules from evidence (what they escalate, how they like updates, what irritates them). If the manager has not written one, the user can ask. The coach also has the user draft their own and share it with their team and key peers. A Director candidate who has made their operating rules explicit is easier to trust with more scope. (managing-up, executive-trust)
- **Boundary conditions:** A document does not replace behavior. If practice contradicts it, it damages trust. Some cultures and managers will find it self-regarding. Offer it as a working tool, not a manifesto.

### D4. Charter your area: mission, vision, customers, metrics, importance, risks, interfaces
- **Claim:** Every team should have a charter setting out its mission, a vision of the end state, who its customers are, its metrics with targets, why it matters strategically, its major risks, the interfaces it provides to others, and the interfaces it depends on. Charters are long-lived (three to five years, refreshed yearly). Short-term plans (quarterly OKRs or similar) sit under them.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 2 ("Team Charter"); High Growth Handbook interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach has the user write or update a charter for their product area. Provided and dependent interfaces are where cross-team conflict and scope expansion come from. A PM whose charter names the interfaces they own and depend on is doing Director-level organizational design. The "strategic importance" section is where enterprise-thinking is tested: can the user link their area to company economics and strategy in a paragraph? (organizational-design, enterprise-thinking, scope-expansion)
- **Boundary conditions:** A charter written by one PM without the engineering lead, design lead and their manager is a claim on territory, not a charter. Co-author it and get it ratified.

### D5. Goals are outcomes with owners, counterweights and a stated level of commitment
- **Claim:** Stripe's guidance (written with CTO David Singleton and included in her book) calls for 3-5 objectives and 1-5 key results each. Key results are outcomes, not activities. Aspirational goals are expected to score around 70-80%. A minority (20-30%) are marked must-hit, expected at 95% or more, and other goals are sacrificed to hit them. Do not turn a roadmap into OKRs. Her metrics checklist asks whether each metric is leading, has a counterweight metric, has a way to calculate it and an owner, is relative rather than absolute, and excludes growth that would have happened anyway. Changes mid-quarter are documented and communicated to affected teams.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 2 ("Objectives and Metrics Checklist", "Writing Good OKRs", "Iterating and Scoring").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach reviews the user's goals against the checklist and asks which are must-hit and what they would give up to hit them. It flags any metric without a counterweight and any key result that is a shipped feature. When plans change, the coach checks that the user documented what was dropped and told dependent teams. Senior leaders notice silent reprioritization. (evidence-discipline, executive-communication, decision-making)
- **Boundary conditions:** Early products may be better served by milestones than metric targets, a distinction she draws herself. Heavy OKR machinery on a small team is overhead.

### D6. Business reviews are candid written narratives about results and investment
- **Claim:** Stripe's quarterly business reviews use a six-page narrative plus an appendix, shared at least 24 hours ahead, with reading time in the meeting. Guidance: focus on results against the existing plan, be candid about what the team needs to do better, show how much is being invested relative to what the area produces, and leave with clear mutual expectations, known obstacles, and what leadership must provide. Afterward, notes go to participants and action items into a shared tracker. She has said that the narrative, the color on how the team was doing, was the most valuable part of these memos.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 2 ("Quarterly Business Reviews"); Tunguz notes.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach uses the QBR list of statements that should be true afterward to review the user's quarterly and executive updates. Is there a trajectory, an explanation of misses, investment versus return, and an explicit ask? For executive communication, candor about misses is treated as a trust builder, not a risk. (executive-communication, enterprise-thinking)
- **Boundary conditions:** Six-page memos suit written cultures. Where executives expect slides, keep the discipline (results, candor, investment, asks) and change the format.

### D7. Decide who decides before debating, and keep problem-solving local
- **Claim:** Say at the outset whether a meeting is for discussing or for deciding, and who makes the call. Org structure maps decision rights only partly; after that, rights must be stated. Sort decisions by impact and reversibility. High-impact, irreversible ones get rigor and more stakeholders. Low-impact, reversible ones are made by individuals using the operating principles. Principles should be clear enough that people reach the decision a founder would. She warns that failing to localize problem-solving quietly kills companies, and that leaders, especially the CEO, must own decisions to stop things because organizations default to continuing. Stripe's leader principles expect leaders to make the call, or drive one, when ownership is murky.
- **Source:** High Growth Handbook interview; First Round Review article; Workbooks, ch. 2 (Stripe operating principles, "Make decisions; be accountable").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user to state, for their next contested decision, whether it is reversible, who decides, and whether the meeting is for discussion or decision. It looks for decisions the user escalates that their principles already answer (not localizing), and for murky-ownership situations where the user could step in and drive a decision. Doing that is a visible next-level behavior. (decision-making, executive-posture)
- **Boundary conditions:** Driving decisions under murky ownership can be read as a land grab in political organizations. Make the move openly and bring the other plausible owners along.

### D8. Escalate by co-writing the disagreement, not by lobbying the boss
- **Claim:** Stripe's unblocking process (created by CTO David Singleton, published in her workbook) says: first ask whether the decision is reversible and whether dissenters can commit to the path favored by those doing most of the work. If an important, hard-to-reverse disagreement persists, the parties co-write a half-page document stating the goal, options, and trade-offs, and send it to both managers. Resolution is expected within about five business days, recursing up the chain if needed. Escalation should be joint. It may be unilateral only after the other party refuses and is told. Managers are asked to refuse to hear one side alone. Raising an issue with your manager and then presenting their view as a decision to someone who disagrees counts as a request to unblock, and the other party should be present.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 4 ("Stripe's Unblocking Process").
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user wants to "escalate" a peer conflict, the coach asks for the co-written trade-off document first, and asks whether they have told the peer they are going up. Joint escalation reads as senior. Unilateral escalation reads as political, and peers remember it. The coach also uses the format for executive decision memos: goal, decision sought, options, trade-offs each side accepts. (organizational-politics, executive-communication, decision-making)
- **Boundary conditions:** It works when managers enforce the norm against one-sided hearings. Without that, the user may lose ground to a peer who lobbies. Use the format anyway, but understand the local game.

### D9. Say the thing you think you cannot say
- **Claim:** Another of her four operating principles: name the undiscussable issue, constructively, rather than filter it out. She warns against over-filtering, suggests watching body language and tension for unspoken concerns, and recommends feedback that explores (asking the person how they think it went first) rather than lectures, aimed at the work, not the person.
- **Source:** Scaling People (operating principles); Newcomer podcast; First Round podcast page.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks what the user is not saying to their manager, their peers or their team, and helps them script it about the work and its effects. In meetings, the coach looks for moments the user saw the unspoken problem and did or did not name it. Naming it well is a strong executive-presence signal. (meeting-presence, executive-trust, managing-up)
- **Boundary conditions:** Timing, relationship and power still matter. Saying it in private first is usually better than in a large meeting. For people whose directness is penalized more (by gender, race or culture), the costs are not equal (Hewlett's research on executive presence; see Limits and biases).

### D10. Distinguish management from leadership, and hold high standards with deep devotion
- **Claim:** The third operating principle. Management is the knowable process of getting from A to B: goals, systems, development. Leadership sets vision and standards and pushes the organization past what seems possible. Drawing on Frances Frei and Anne Morriss, she argues good leaders combine high standards with deep devotion to their people. She says she herself had to move toward the standards side over time. Stripe's leader principles ask leaders to elevate ambitions, set the pace and energy, and obsess over talent.
- **Source:** Scaling People (operating principles); Stripe Press launch post; Workbooks, ch. 2 (Stripe's leader principles, Dec 2021).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks which side the user defaults to. Excellent-process PMs often under-lead: they run clean systems toward modest goals. Visionary PMs often under-manage, with no cadence or owners. For Director readiness, the coach looks for evidence of both: a raised bar on quality or ambition and a system that makes it achievable. (next-level-readiness, managerial-leverage)
- **Boundary conditions:** The split is a lens, not a hierarchy. Many organizations reward management reliability more than leadership ambition at the Director level and the reverse at VP.

### D11. Team-building is the main lever: hire with rubrics, judge leaders by the teams they build
- **Claim:** Hiring runs on explicit rubrics, structured interviews, written exercises (including one for product managers) and a separate candidate review step. A telling interview question for leaders is to ask about their current team and why they structured it that way. It shows whether they shape talent or accept what they inherit. Reference checks should ask where the person ranks (top 50%, 20%, 10%). Stripe's leader principles ask leaders to read candidate packets and push back on hires who are merely fine.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 3 (interview framework and rubric, PM written exercise, candidate review); First Round podcast page; Stripe leader principles.
- **Changes coaching how:** Even before they have reports, the coach asks the user how they influence who joins their area (interview loops, rubric design, closing candidates). Once they have reports, it asks why the team is structured as it is. A future Director is judged heavily on team quality. (talent, managerial-leverage)
- **Boundary conditions:** Stripe's hiring bar and funding allowed selectivity that many companies cannot match. Rubrics can encode bias if never recalibrated.

### D12. Growing into larger roles means learning ahead of the role and demonstrating it over time
- **Claim:** She describes high performers as "learning organisms" who anticipate what they will need to learn for their future role. In hypergrowth, the company becomes a different company every 6-12 months, so executives are hired for the next 12-18 months, not forever. Stripe's promotion template asks how long the person has been showing next-level capabilities and behaviors, with examples. A leader's job includes working themselves out of their current job.
- **Source:** First Round Review article; High Growth Handbook interview; Workbooks, ch. 5 (performance review template); Lattice interview.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach frames next-level readiness as a duration of observable behavior, not a single standout project: "for how many quarters have you been doing the Director job, and who has seen it?" It also asks what the user is learning now that the next role will require (P&L, managing managers, board-level narrative, AI platform economics), and what parts of their current job they have handed off. (next-level-readiness, role-transition, trajectory-model)
- **Boundary conditions:** "Demonstrate before promotion" can become unpaid stretch work that never converts. The coach should pair it with sponsorship and explicit promotion criteria.

### D13. Performance systems: no surprises, results and how they were achieved, explicit written reasoning
- **Claim:** Her management prerequisites include weekly or biweekly 1:1s with shared agendas and actions, quarterly goals, feedback both ways every three to six months, and formal performance conversations covering what was achieved and how. Stripe's review template makes the reviewer write one sentence on why they did not choose a higher rating and one on why not a lower one. The test of a working system is that no one is surprised by their review.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 1 ("Management Prerequisites Checklist") and ch. 5 (performance review template); interview coverage.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks that the user's relationship with their own manager meets the prerequisites (regular 1:1s, agreed quarterly goals, access to company context). Gaps are managing-up work to fix now, not grievances. If the user is surprised by feedback, the coach treats it as a sign the feedback loop is broken and helps them repair it. (managing-up, talent)
- **Boundary conditions:** The template assumes a calibration culture and ratings scale like Stripe's. Adapt the discipline, not the form.

### D14. Leadership forums need standing questions about decisions and what cascades
- **Claim:** Stripe's leadership team sent written "snippets" before each meeting (action updates, topics and why they need discussion, initiative status, customer notes, talent notes). The meeting had standing questions: what decisions were made, what should be communicated to the wider leadership team, and does anyone object to a topic being passed down.
- **Source:** Workbooks, ch. 4 ("Leadership Team Snippets and Updates").
- **Changes coaching how:** For meeting presence in senior forums, the coach has the user arrive with a pre-written snippet (status, the one topic that needs discussion and why, the decision sought) and leave with an explicit note of what cascades to their team. The coach watches whether the user closes the loop downward after leadership meetings, which marks a leader rather than an attendee. (meeting-presence, executive-communication)
- **Boundary conditions:** Only useful if the forum actually makes decisions. In status-only forums, the snippet is still a good way to be visible.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Operating system audit (run for the user's scope, then for their manager's)
| Element | Individual (the user) | Team | Area or division |
| Mission |
| Objectives with owner (DRI) |
| Key metrics (with counterweights) |
| Accountability mechanisms |
| Operating cadence (weekly, monthly, quarterly) |
- Is each item written down and easy to find? If the user struggles to fill it in, their team is struggling more.
- Missing cells become the user's organizational-design backlog.

### Team charter (one page per product area)
Mission. Vision (end state). Customers. Metrics with targets. Strategic importance. Major risks. Interfaces provided. Interfaces depended on.
- Co-author with engineering and design leads; get the manager to ratify; refresh yearly.

### Goal and metric checklist
- 3-5 objectives, each an outcome in the user's voice. 1-5 key results each, outcomes not activities.
- Label each must-hit (95%+) or aspirational (70-80%). Name what gets sacrificed for must-hits.
- For each metric: leading? counterweight? calculation defined? owner? relative to a baseline? net of growth that would happen anyway?
- Mid-quarter: green/yellow/red with on-track/at-risk/off-track. Document changes and notify affected teams.

### Business review test (statements that should be true after the review)
- Leadership saw long-term and short-term metrics with a trajectory.
- Misses were explained with causes and a plan to close them.
- Investment versus output was visible, so leaders could decide on funding.
- Mutual expectations for next period are explicit.
- Known obstacles are named.
- Leadership knows what the team needs to accelerate.

### Working-with-me document
My role and goals. About me (personality, communication preferences, boundaries). Operating approach (cadence, what to loop me in on, planning rhythm, how I stay close to the team). Management style (how I decide, give and take feedback, context I need). Supporting you (careers, development, how I join your work).
- Managing-up variant: write the inferred version of the manager's doc, then test it in the next 1:1.

### Unblocking document (half a page, co-authored)
Authors and date. Goal (in the user's or customer's voice). Decision sought. Options considered. Trade-offs for each option, agreed by both parties. Then send to both managers together. Target resolution: five business days.
- Pre-checks: is it reversible? Does it matter at the end state? Can the dissenter commit to the path chosen by whoever does most of the work?

### Decision sorting
| | Reversible | Hard to reverse |
| High impact | Decide fast with a small group; revisit on data | Rigor, broad consultation, explicit decider, written rationale |
| Low impact | Individual judgment using principles | Short written rationale; inform affected teams |

### Career conversation (for the user's reports, and as a template for asking their own manager)
Where you grew up, schooling, first choices and why, favorite and least favorite jobs, what work you see yourself doing in five years (not a title). End with two or three development goals to track together.

## Diagnostic questions
- Could your area run for a month without you? What written artifacts would it run on?
- Can you fill in mission, objectives with owners, metrics, accountability and cadence for your scope right now?
- What does your manager's working-with-me document say, even if they never wrote one? What evidence did you use?
- Have you published your own? Who has read it?
- What does your charter say about the interfaces you provide and the ones you depend on? Who else would dispute it?
- Which of your goals are must-hit, and what would you drop to hit them?
- Which metric in your last update had no counterweight?
- In your last business update, where were you candid about a miss, and what did you ask for?
- For your current disagreement with a peer: is it reversible? Have you co-written the trade-offs? Have you told them you are going up?
- What is the thing you think you cannot say, and to whom?
- For how many quarters have you been doing next-level work, and who has seen it?
- What do you need to learn now for the role after this one?
- After your last leadership forum, what did you cascade to your team?

## Observable signals
Positive (next-level operation, per Hughes Johnson):
- The user maintains a current charter and goals page that others use without asking.
- The user writes and shares a working-with-me doc and adjusts to their manager's stated or inferred preferences.
- Escalations arrive as co-authored trade-off documents, and peers say they were told first.
- Business updates are narratives with results, misses, investment versus return, and asks.
- Goals carry owners, counterweights and must-hit labels. Changes are documented and communicated.
- The user steps in to drive decisions where ownership is unclear and says openly that they are doing so.
- In meetings the user names the unspoken issue constructively.
- After senior forums the user cascades decisions to their team within a day.

Negative (current-level):
- Knowledge of how the area works lives in the user's head.
- Recurring fires handled one by one with no system change.
- Goals that are roadmap items or activities, or metrics without baselines.
- Unilateral escalation or lobbying the boss about a peer.
- Silent reprioritization discovered by dependent teams.
- Updates that report only good news.
- Surprise at review time without having asked for feedback.
- Waiting for someone else to clarify decision rights.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Process as performance: producing charters, OKRs and docs nobody uses, or copying Stripe's full apparatus into a team of six. She herself limits mandatory processes.
- Using the unblocking format as a weapon: writing a one-sided "joint" document or escalating unilaterally while claiming to follow the process.
- A working-with-me doc that lists demands on others with no commitments from the author, or that practice contradicts.
- Self-awareness exercises as a stand-in for behavior change.
- Assuming explicit decision rights remove politics. They reduce ambiguity, but power and relationships still decide what happens.
- Charter as a land grab: claiming interfaces and scope without the owners' agreement.
- Treating "say the thing you cannot say" as license for bluntness without care or timing.
- An IC PM trying to install a company-wide operating system without the COO's mandate. Work within the user's own scope and offer it to others as a model.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Slootman (systems-building vs. leader-driven intensity):** Hughes Johnson scales through explicit systems that let many people decide without the leader. Slootman scales by the leader raising standards and pace directly and changing people quickly. Hers fits steady hypergrowth where the risk is chaos and inconsistency. His fits stalls and turnarounds where the risk is complacency. Stripe's own leader principles ("set the pace and energy", "elevate ambitions") show she does not reject intensity. She wants it carried by the system, not only by one leader.
- **Grove (lineage):** Stripe's OKR guide uses Grove's two MBO questions (where do I want to go; how will I pace myself), and her counterweight metrics are Grove's paired indicators. Grove leaves more to the live meeting and the manager's judgment. She documents more up front.
- **Bezos/Amazon:** Stripe's six-page QBR narrative and pre-read are close to Amazon's memo culture. Her reversible/irreversible sorting matches Amazon's one-way/two-way doors, and "disagree and commit" appeared among Stripe's classic slogans. The difference is escalation: Stripe formalizes joint, bilateral escalation, while Amazon leans on single-threaded owners and principles.
- **Ibarra:** Hughes Johnson puts self-awareness first ("know yourself"). Ibarra argues that people change their identity mainly by acting in new ways and then reflecting, so introspection-first can keep someone stuck in an outdated self-image. For a PM making the jump, do both: use her exercises to find defaults, and use Ibarra to try new behaviors before feeling ready.
- **Horowitz:** Horowitz stresses that early-stage and wartime companies often need founder judgment more than process. She argues planning structure should arrive earlier than founders expect. The trigger is the rate at which coordination failures cost more than the process would.
- **Gabarro & Kotter:** Their "managing your boss" is the research base for her working-with-me contracts. Both put the burden on the subordinate to understand the boss's style and pressures.
- **Scott:** Radical Candor and "say the thing you think you cannot say" agree on challenging directly with care. Both authors came up through Google.
- **McCord:** Both favor context over control. McCord's Netflix practice moves people out faster than Hughes Johnson's documented improvement plans do.
- **Pfeffer:** Pfeffer would say explicit systems do not remove power dynamics, and that a working-with-me doc is also impression management. Hers is the better guide to how legitimate authority should work. His is more realistic about how it does work.
- **Watkins:** Stripe's new-leader onboarding (structured meetings, 90-day 360, a working-with-me doc by week nine) is an institutional version of Watkins's first-90-days discipline.

## Limits and biases
- Context: a founder-led, well-capitalized, private hypergrowth company with high talent density and a strong writing culture. She was COO with a mandate to install company-wide systems. Much of the apparatus assumes authority a PM does not have.
- Survivorship: Stripe is a success, and the systems are described from the inside by their architect. Stripe still overhired and cut about 14% of staff in late 2022 (after she had left the COO role), which shows operating systems do not prevent strategic misjudgment about headcount.
- Evidence: practitioner experience and templates, not research. Several templates were written by other Stripe leaders (for example the OKR guide and unblocking process with CTO David Singleton, and QBR guidance by the finance team) and are included in her book.
- Culture: assumes norms of written communication, explicitness and direct feedback common at Google and Stripe. In relationship-driven or hierarchical cultures, heavy documentation can read as bureaucratic or confrontational.
- Identity: she writes as a woman executive and encourages bringing the whole self to work. "Say the thing you cannot say" and working-with-me docs do not carry the same social cost for everyone. The book gives this limited attention.
- Coverage: little on boards, investors, or company politics at the top. She stays within operating the company, not governing it.
