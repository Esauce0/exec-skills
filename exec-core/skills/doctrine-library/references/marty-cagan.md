# Marty Cagan

## Why this source matters here
Cagan ran product and engineering at HP, Netscape and eBay, then founded Silicon Valley Product Group, which has spent roughly two decades coaching product organizations and, more recently, whole companies through a move to what SVPG calls the product operating model. His material is the most explicit available job description for the product leader role: what the leader produces (strategic context, coached people, a team structure), what the leader stops doing (assigning feature roadmaps), and what the leader owes executives in exchange for autonomy. The doctrine was formed in consumer and B2B tech product companies and in large "transformation" clients. It is normative and written from the consultant's seat, so it describes the target state more precisely than it describes how to survive in a company that is far from it. For an AI PM aiming at Director or VP, it defines the destination role; other sources (Pfeffer, Horowitz, Doshi) are needed for the political path to get there.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. The product leader's primary output is strategic context, not a roadmap
- **Claim:** Empowered teams can only make good decisions if they have the context to make them. Strategic context has six parts: company mission, company scorecard (the KPIs that describe business health), annual company objectives, product vision, product strategy, and product principles. The mission and company objectives mostly come from the CEO and executive team; the product vision and principles, team topology, product strategy, and the specific objectives given to each team come mostly from product leadership. A leader who hands teams a list of features with dates has skipped the job and made the teams dependent.
- **Source:** "Coaching – Strategic Context" (2020); "Product Leadership Is Hard" (2020); "The Alternative to Roadmaps" (2015); Empowered.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user describes their leadership contribution, the coach asks which of the six elements the user authored, maintains or evangelizes, and whether a PM on the user's teams could make a disputed prioritization call using only what is written down. A Director candidate whose artifacts are all roadmaps and status decks is scored as operating at PM level, however senior their audience.
- **Boundary conditions:** Where the company mission, scorecard or objectives are missing or incoherent, the user cannot supply them alone; the coach shifts to managing up (proposing a scorecard, asking for objectives) rather than blaming the gap.

### D2. Diagnose which kind of team you actually run before prescribing anything
- **Claim:** Cagan distinguishes delivery teams (executives decide what to build; the PM administers a backlog), feature teams (stakeholders hand over a prioritized feature roadmap; stakeholders own value but the team is blamed when results disappoint; the PM acts as project manager), and empowered product teams (the team is given a problem, and the PM owns value and viability, the designer usability, the tech lead feasibility). Most companies that believe they have empowered teams have feature teams.
- **Source:** "Product vs. Feature Teams" (2019); Empowered.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach classifies the user's current teams from evidence (who wrote the roadmap, what teams are measured on, who decides when a launched feature fails to move the metric) before advising. Advice appropriate for empowered teams (argue outcomes, refuse dates) backfires in a feature-team company; there the user's move is to earn one problem-shaped assignment and deliver it.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some work is legitimately delivery work (regulatory mandates, contractual commitments). Classification is per team and per initiative, not a verdict on the company.

### D3. Coaching and staffing are the management job; developing people is the single most important responsibility
- **Claim:** Product leaders have management duties (coaching; staffing, meaning sourcing, recruiting, onboarding, evaluating, promoting and replacing) and leadership duties (vision and principles, team topology, product strategy, team objectives, ongoing evangelism). Cagan puts coaching at roughly half of a first-line manager's time and says you cannot be a good manager without being a good coach. HR supports but never substitutes for the hiring manager.
- **Source:** "Product Leadership Is Hard" (2020); "Product Managers vs Product Leaders" (2023); "Product Director Job Description"; "Coaching Tools – The Plan" (2019).
- **Changes coaching how:** For a user moving into management, the coach audits the calendar: hours per week in 1:1 coaching, hiring loops and written coaching plans versus hours in roadmap and delivery meetings. A new manager still acting as the best PM on the team is flagged as a role-transition risk. Promotion evidence for a Director case should include people the user developed, not only products shipped.
- **Boundary conditions:** For an IC track (Principal PM), coaching is informal and the leverage comes through influence and artifacts; the coaching share applies to people managers.

### D4. Product strategy means focus, insight, action and active management
- **Claim:** Strategy starts with being willing to pick very few pivotal objectives (Cagan draws on Rumelt here); leaders routinely believe they are focused while carrying 20 to 30 "critical" initiatives. Insights come from quantitative data, customer and user learning, enabling technology and industry change. Action means deciding which team pursues which objective and giving it the context to do so. Spreading engineering capacity "fairly" across business units (his Pandora example) produces many features and little impact. Product strategy is distinct from business strategy and go-to-market strategy; many companies have the latter two and no product strategy.
- **Source:** "Product Strategy – Focus" and "– Management" (2020); strategy series overview; "Changing How You Decide Which Problems To Solve" (2022).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user to list current top priorities for their area. More than three or four is treated as a focus failure and a signal that the user is absorbing organizational FOMO rather than leading. The coach asks for the insight behind each priority (what data or customer learning makes it pivotal) and rejects priorities justified only by a stakeholder request or a competitor's move.
- **Boundary conditions:** Focus is set within constraints the user may not control. In a company run by a CEO who adds priorities weekly, the realistic move is to make the cost of each addition visible, not to refuse.

### D5. Manage strategy through weekly 1:1s and servant leadership, not by taking the decisions back
- **Claim:** No product strategy survives first contact with reality; the leader's job during the quarter is to find out which teams are stuck (primarily through weekly 1:1s with PMs), then remove obstacles: resolve cross-team dependencies, secure technology, handle customer crises, mediate between teams, and share learnings across teams. The leader responds to requests for help rather than taking control.
- **Source:** "Product Strategy – Management" (2020).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user reports a team missing its objective, the coach asks when the user first knew and what the weekly 1:1 surfaced. A leader who learns about stalls at the quarterly review has a management-system failure. The coach steers the user toward removing the specific obstacle, and away from rewriting the team's solution.
- **Boundary conditions:** When a team is repeatedly stuck because of skill gaps, servant leadership is insufficient; the issue becomes coaching or staffing (D3).

### D6. Ownership is proportional to credibility; blaming executives is theater
- **Claim:** Cagan calls it product leadership theater when leaders blame senior executives, complain about weak PMs they have not developed, claim a lack of empowerment, or resent the CEO asking for a roadmap without having built an alternative source of confidence. Executives reasonably distrust product organizations whose people are weak. The product organization has to raise its game first; empowerment follows demonstrated competence.
- **Source:** "Product Leadership Theater" (2024).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user frames a problem as "leadership won't let us," the coach converts each complaint into a credibility question: what evidence has the user given this executive that the team can be trusted with a problem instead of a feature list? The coach tracks credibility deposits (a delivered high-integrity commitment, a discovery result that changed an executive's mind) as trajectory evidence.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some environments are structurally hostile (a CEO who will not delegate regardless of evidence). Credibility is necessary but may not be sufficient; see Pfeffer on power and Horowitz on political systems.

### D7. Earn executive trust with an explicit two-way pledge
- **Claim:** In exchange for being given problems rather than features, product teams commit to executives to: share openly the data and reasoning behind decisions; take responsibility for outcomes by solving for customers, business and technology; back any risky or counterintuitive proposal with discovery evidence; treat high-integrity commitments as binding; preview potentially risky solutions with the relevant executive before building (no surprises); and work to earn trust. They ask executives, in return, to keep quarterly focus stable.
- **Source:** "Pledge to Executives" (2023).
- **Changes coaching how:** The pledge becomes a trust checklist for the user's relationship with each senior leader. The coach asks: when did the user last show this executive the reasoning (not just the conclusion)? Has any executive been surprised by something the user shipped? When the user disagreed with an executive, did they bring an argument or a test result?
- **Boundary conditions:** The pledge assumes executives accept the product model. In a company that has not, previews and evidence still build trust, but the user should not present the pledge as a contract the executive signed.

### D8. Stakeholder trust comes from knowing their constraints and showing solutions early, one-on-one
- **Claim:** Stakeholders (executives, finance, legal, compliance, sales, business development) are people who can block a launch. They trust a PM who understands their constraints, is visibly committed to solving for them as well as for customers, and keeps them informed. Cagan recommends substantial weekly 1:1 time with key stakeholders, showing prototypes during discovery rather than finished work after build, and moving opinion disputes toward tests. Large stakeholder group meetings tend toward design by committee.
- **Source:** "Stakeholder Management" (2013); "Stakeholders and the Product Model" (2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** When a launch is blocked late by legal, sales or finance, the coach treats it as a discovery failure by the user, not an obstacle created by the stakeholder. The coach asks the user to name each blocking stakeholder's top constraint in their own words; if the user cannot, that is the gap.
- **Boundary conditions:** One-on-one previewing scales poorly as the user's scope grows; at Director level the user must build this discipline into PMs rather than do it personally.

### D9. Integrity is dependability, company interest and owned accountability
- **Claim:** Trust rests on competence and character. Integrity shows up as: keeping commitments (and shipping things that actually work), being seen to act in the company's interest over one's own team's, and taking responsibility for results, including asking what you could have done to manage the risk better even when others share the fault.
- **Source:** "Coaching – Integrity" (2019).
- **Changes coaching how:** In post-mortems and difficult updates, the coach checks the user's language for deflection. It asks the user to write the "what I could have done" paragraph before the "what others did" paragraph. It also looks for cases where the user traded away company interest for team optics, such as refusing to lend engineers to a higher-priority effort.
- **Boundary conditions:** Owning outcomes does not mean absorbing blame for decisions the user was overruled on; the user should document the dissent and still own their share.

### D10. A product manager is an individual contributor; a product leader is a people manager with different work
- **Claim:** Cagan reserves "product manager" for the IC (APM through Principal) and "product leader" for managers of product people (manager, Director, VP, CPO), with the group product manager as a player-coach bridge. The leader's defining responsibility is developing people's skills and setting strategic context. He also argues that leaders must be genuine experts in the craft they lead; people-only managers lack the credibility to guide expert teams, and as AI reduces the cost of building, leadership over vision, strategy, team structure and outcomes becomes more important, not less.
- **Source:** "Product Managers vs Product Leaders" (2023); "Experts Lead Experts" (2026).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user which track they are pursuing and scores evidence against that track. Readiness for a Director role requires evidence of coaching, hiring and context-setting, not only larger products. For an AI PM, the coach checks that the user's craft depth in AI product work is visible, since Cagan's argument predicts that technically shallow product leaders lose standing with AI-era teams.
- **Boundary conditions:** Many companies use titles inconsistently (Cagan notes this, especially in Europe). Map the user's company ladder before applying the distinction.

### D11. Team topology is a leadership decision that determines how much empowerment is possible
- **Claim:** How work is split among teams (scope, structure, dependencies) is part of the strategic context the product leader owns. The goal is teams that are loosely coupled but highly aligned, so each can own a meaningful problem.
- **Source:** "Product Leadership Is Hard" (2020); Empowered (team topology chapters).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user reports chronic cross-team dependency pain, the coach considers topology before process. The question is whether the teams are drawn around problems they can own end to end, or around org-chart history.
- **Boundary conditions:** Re-drawing topology is disruptive and usually needs executive sponsorship; a Director can propose it but rarely decides it alone. See Horowitz on org design always being a tradeoff.

### D12. Date commitments are rare, made only after discovery, and then kept
- **Claim:** Feature roadmaps fail because most initial ideas do not produce the intended outcome and teams do not know why they are building things. The alternative is product vision plus outcome-based team objectives. Where the business genuinely needs a date (a contract, an event, a dependency), teams make a high-integrity commitment, but only after enough discovery to understand the solution, and then treat it as binding.
- **Source:** "The Alternative to Roadmaps" (2015); "Pledge to Executives" (2023); "Stakeholders and the Product Model" (2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** When an executive asks the user for a date, the coach does not advise refusal. It advises: identify whether the need is real, time-box the discovery needed to commit responsibly, then commit and deliver. A user who has missed several dates has a bigger trust problem than any argument about roadmaps can fix.
- **Boundary conditions:** In sales-led enterprise contexts, dates are part of the product being sold; there the share of high-integrity commitments will be much higher than Cagan's norm.

### D13. In a transformation, product leaders are the critical variable
- **Claim:** The product operating model changes how a company builds (frequent, small, reliable releases), how it solves problems (teams given problems and measured on outcomes), and how it decides which problems to solve (vision and insight-driven strategy). Cagan says the choice of product leaders will likely prove critical to a transformation; if experienced leaders are not in place, recruit them or back existing leaders with outside coaching before starting.
- **Source:** "The Product Operating Model: An Introduction" (2023); "Product Model Competencies" (2023); Transformed (2024).
- **Changes coaching how:** If the user's company is "moving to the product model," the coach looks for chances for the user to become one of the leaders the transformation depends on, for example by piloting an empowered team and producing a visible outcome. The coach treats the transformation as sponsorship terrain: who is its executive sponsor, and does the user's work make that person's bet look right?
- **Boundary conditions:** Transformations often stall; tying one's reputation to an under-sponsored transformation carries career risk. Check sponsor strength first.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Team-type diagnostic (run per team the user leads or influences)
| Question | Delivery team | Feature team | Empowered product team |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides what gets built? | Executives | Stakeholders via roadmap | The team, against an assigned problem |
| What is the team measured on? | Output shipped | Features shipped on date | Outcome metric moved |
| What happens when a launch misses the metric? | Nothing | Team blamed, stakeholder moves on | Team iterates; it owns the result |
| PM's main activity | Backlog administration | Project management, stakeholder wrangling | Discovery on value and viability |
Score each team. Advice for the user depends on the column, not on the company's self-description.

### Strategic context audit
For each element (mission, scorecard, company objectives, product vision, product strategy, product principles, plus team topology and team objectives), record: exists in writing (yes/no); owner; last updated; whether PMs can cite it. Then ask the user which gaps sit inside their authority to fill. Gaps the user can fill are Director-level leverage; gaps above them are managing-up agenda items.

### Product strategy check (focus, insight, action, management)
1. Focus: how many objectives are "top priority"? More than three or four fails.
2. Insight: for each, what data, customer learning, technology shift or industry change makes it pivotal?
3. Action: which team owns it, with what objective and key result, and what context have they been given?
4. Management: what is the weekly mechanism that surfaces stalls, and what obstacles did the leader remove last month?

### Executive trust ledger (from the Pledge to Executives)
For each senior stakeholder, list evidence in the last quarter for: reasoning shared; outcome ownership; disagreement backed by evidence; commitments kept; no surprises (risky solutions previewed); focus protected. Empty rows are the coaching agenda.

### Theater-to-credibility conversion
Take each complaint the user makes ("execs want dates," "my PMs are weak," "engineering doesn't care," "we're not empowered"). Rewrite it as the credibility action Cagan would expect ("deliver one high-integrity commitment," "write a coaching plan per PM," "put engineers in front of customers," "win one problem-shaped objective and report the outcome").

### Leader time audit
Classify a typical week: coaching 1:1s; staffing; strategy and context work; stakeholder previews; delivery and roadmap meetings. A people manager spending more on delivery meetings than on coaching and staffing is operating below the role as Cagan defines it.

### PM assessment and coaching plan (Cagan's coaching tools)
Rate each PM (or the user, as self-assessment) on three groups, then write one action per gap: a resource to study, a person to build a relationship with, or a practice assignment, with a date to review.
| Group | Areas |
|---|---|
| Product knowledge | users and customers; data; industry and domain; business and company (sales and marketing, finance, legal, partnerships); product operations |
| Process skills | discovery techniques; optimization techniques; delivery techniques; the development process |
| People skills | team collaboration; stakeholder management; evangelism; leadership |
Cagan expects a new PM to spend a few hours a day for roughly two to three months building the product-knowledge base. For a user preparing for a Director role, the coach runs the same grid on each direct report and checks that every gap has a written, dated action.

## Diagnostic questions
- Which of the six elements of strategic context exist in writing for the user's area, and which did the user write?
- If the user were out for a month, could their PMs resolve a contested prioritization decision using the written strategy alone?
- How many priorities does the user's area currently carry? What was the last good idea the user said no to?
- For the teams the user leads, who decides what gets built, and what are they measured on?
- When did an executive last get surprised by something from the user's area? What was not previewed?
- For each blocking stakeholder, can the user state their top constraint in the stakeholder's own terms?
- What high-integrity commitments has the user made in the past two quarters, and were they kept?
- What share of the user's week is coaching and staffing? Is there a written coaching plan for each direct report?
- Which complaints about executives or other functions does the user repeat? What would credibility-building look like for each?
- Is the user aiming for the IC track or the management track, and does their evidence match that track?
- If the company is transforming, who is the executive sponsor, and does the user's work make that sponsor's bet look right?
- How visible is the user's hands-on depth in AI product craft to the builders they would lead?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating at product-leader level):
- Produces and maintains a written product strategy with explicit focus and named insights; PMs reference it in their own decisions.
- Gives teams objectives framed as problems with measurable outcomes and a stated reason, not feature lists.
- Previews risky solutions with the relevant executive before build; can point to no recent executive surprises.
- Makes few date commitments, times them after discovery, and hits them.
- Holds weekly 1:1s with PMs and can name each PM's current development goal.
- Brings evidence (a prototype test, usage data) when disagreeing with an executive, and changes their own position when evidence goes the other way.
- Can say which initiatives were deliberately not funded this quarter and why.

Negative (PM-level or below-level behavior):
- Describes their job mainly as managing a roadmap and fielding stakeholder requests.
- Distributes capacity across stakeholders "fairly" rather than concentrating it.
- Repeats that leadership won't empower the team, with no plan to earn it.
- Shows stakeholders finished work for the first time at launch review.
- As a new manager, rewrites PMs' specs instead of coaching them to write better ones.
- Misses self-set dates, then argues against dates in principle.
- Cannot explain the company scorecard or how their area moves it.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using "empowered teams" as a slogan to refuse executive requests. Cagan frames empowerment as earned through credibility (D6); invoking it before earning it destroys trust.
- Treating "no roadmaps" as "no dates." Cagan keeps high-integrity commitments; the user who refuses all dates will be seen as evasive.
- Diagnosing every problem as "we're a feature team." The coach should also check the user's own skill, strategy quality and stakeholder work before blaming the operating model.
- Writing a vision deck with no insight behind it. A vision is persuasive, but strategy has to rest on data and customer learning.
- Applying Cagan's IC/manager split as if titles meant the same thing everywhere. Map the user's actual ladder first.
- Reading the model as anti-process. Cagan's product leaders manage actively (weekly 1:1s, obstacle removal); "servant leadership" does not mean absence.
- Coach misuse: holding the user to the target state in a company years away from it. Grade the user on moving their part of the organization in the right direction, not on replicating a best-in-class product company.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Horowitz (wartime command).** Cagan: leaders give context and problems; teams choose solutions; dissent is resolved by discovery evidence. Horowitz: in wartime the leader violates protocol, obsesses over details and neither seeks nor tolerates dissent. Cagan is more right when the company has time to learn and the main risk is building the wrong thing; Horowitz is more right in an existential crisis with a single bullet. The coach should first ask which mode the user's company is in.
- **Horowitz (Good PM/Bad PM).** Horowitz's Netscape-era PM is the "CEO of the product," manages the team and takes written positions. Cagan says the PM is nobody's boss and owns value and viability within a team of peers. Both reject the PM as project manager. Horowitz's version fits small, founder-driven teams; Cagan's fits larger organizations with strong design and engineering leads.
- **Pfeffer (power).** Cagan's theory of advancement is merit-based: credibility buys ownership. Pfeffer argues performance is often insufficient and that visibility, relationships and resource control determine who gains power. In political organizations, the coach should run both lenses: build credibility (Cagan) and map power (Pfeffer).
- **Doshi (optics and busyness).** Both argue that most planning pain comes from missing strategy. Doshi adds that optics work legitimately grows with scope and can crowd out leadership; Cagan barely treats internal visibility as work, beyond the no-surprises norm. For a user heading toward VP, Doshi's warning is the more realistic description of the calendar.
- **McCord (Netflix).** Both draw on the Netflix line of leading with context rather than control. McCord is skeptical of manager-driven development programs and puts more weight on hiring great people and being honest when they no longer fit; Cagan puts coaching at the center of the manager's job. McCord fits high-talent-density firms that can pay top of market; Cagan fits companies building capability from the people they have.
- **Hughes Johnson and Larson (operating cadence).** Scaling-oriented operators give more weight to planning rituals, operating reviews and explicit goal systems than Cagan's anti-roadmap rhetoric suggests. These are reconcilable (Cagan also uses quarterly objectives), but a user in a company with heavy operating cadence should use Cagan's content inside that cadence rather than fighting it.
- **Bezos/Amazon.** Amazon relies on single-threaded owners and written narratives (six-pagers, PR/FAQ). This agrees with Cagan on problem ownership and differs on mechanism: Amazon resolves disagreement through documents and "disagree and commit," Cagan through discovery tests. When stakes are high and testing is cheap, Cagan's approach wins; when a test is expensive or slow, a document-driven decision may be better.
- **Rumelt.** Cagan borrows Rumelt's focus on pivotal objectives. Rumelt's kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action) is more demanding about the diagnosis than Cagan's "insights" step; the coach can use Rumelt to stress-test the user's strategy.

## Limits and biases
- Consultant's vantage: SVPG sells coaching and transformation work, and the doctrine describes the target state vividly and the political path to it thinly. Evidence is case-based and anecdotal, drawn from companies that engaged SVPG or that Cagan admires, which carries survivorship bias.
- Built on tech-product companies and digital transformations. It transfers less cleanly to heavily regulated, services-led or sales-led enterprise settings, where commitments and stakeholder control are structural rather than cultural.
- Normative team-type labels ("feature team") can read as judgments on the people in them; used carelessly by the user, they alienate stakeholders.
- The AI claims (2026) are recent opinion, not yet borne out by evidence.
- I found no documented gender or identity critiques specific to Cagan. The general caution applies: advice to "earn credibility through competence" assumes competence is perceived fairly, which research on evaluation bias does not support uniformly.
