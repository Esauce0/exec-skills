# Formats

Templates for recurring executive formats. Treat them as skeletons and drop any section that has nothing decision-relevant in it this time.

## Pyramid or narrative?

| Use the pyramid (answer first, grouped support) | Use a narrative memo (connected prose, read in silence) |
|---|---|
| The reader needs the answer and will trust the reasoning | The reader needs to evaluate the reasoning itself |
| Status, updates, recommendations with clear support | Strategy, big bets, novel problems, contested tradeoffs |
| Time-poor reader, async | A meeting will be spent discussing it |
| The logic is simple enough for bullets | Bullets would hide gaps in the logic |

Minto's test for a pyramid: every point at one level summarizes the points grouped under it, and each point raises the question the level below answers. If you can't write the governing sentence, you don't have a point yet.

## Weekly update

```
<The one thing that matters most this week, stated as a change or a call, with its implication.>

<Decisions I made (one line each, with the reason if non-obvious).>

<What's off track: what, why, what I'm doing, whether I need anything.>

<Asks, if any: the decision, by when, my recommendation.>

<Metrics that moved, against plan or last period. Only the ones that moved or matter.>
```

Rules: keep it under 250 words and leave out meetings held. If nothing changed, say "No material change" and stop.

## Escalation / decision request

```
Decision needed: <one line>. By: <date>. Why that date: <consequence of delay>.

Context: <the minimum the reader needs; 2-4 sentences>.

Options: <2-3, each with its real cost>.

My recommendation: <option> because <reason>. What we give up: <cost>.

<If joint with a peer: "X and I are sending this together. We agree on the facts and disagree on the ranking.">
```

Before sending, confirm the decision actually exceeds your authority. Most escalation drafts contain at least one call the writer should just make.

## Recommendation memo (1-2 pages)

```
Recommendation: <what, in one or two sentences>.

Why now: <what changed or what window is open>.

The case: <3-5 paragraphs of reasoning, with the numbers that matter>.

What it costs: <money, people, time, what we stop doing>.

Risks and how we'd know early: <the 2-3 real risks, each with a leading indicator>.

What I'd need: <decision, people, budget, air cover>.

What would change my mind: <the evidence that would make this the wrong call>.
```

## Narrative memo outline (for a complex decision)

This is the order a good argument follows.

1. The situation, stated so a smart outsider would understand it.
2. The problem or opportunity, with evidence of its size.
3. What we've learned (data, customers, experiments) that makes our view differ from the obvious one.
4. The options we considered, honestly, including doing nothing.
5. The recommendation and the reasoning.
6. What it costs and what we'll stop.
7. How we'll know it's working, and when we'd reverse.
8. Open questions we want the room's help on.

Write in paragraphs and put data in tables or an appendix. Plan for readers to read it silently at the start of the meeting.

## Slack or chat to an executive

```
<Point.> <Implication or ask.> <Link to detail if any.>
```

If it needs more than four sentences, it probably needs to be an email or a doc, or it needs a clearer point.

## Post-meeting follow-up (send within 24 hours)

```
Decisions: <what was decided, by whom>.
Owners and dates: <who does what by when>.
Open: <what's still unresolved and how it gets resolved>.
```

Send it when you own the decision or the outcome, or drove the meeting. Then it turns a conversation into a record the executive can rely on and makes you the person who closes loops. Being the default note-taker for other people's meetings sends the coordinator signal instead (see executive-posture).

## Bad news

```
<What happened, sized.> <Impact on the things the reader cares about.>
<What I've already done.> <What happens next and when.>
<What I need, or "Nothing needed from you.">
<What changes so it doesn't recur, if it's a process failure.>
```

Send bad news early and yourself. An executive who hears it from someone else first draws conclusions about more than the bad news.
