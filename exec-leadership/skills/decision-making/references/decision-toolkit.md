# Decision toolkit

## 1. Decision frame (after Grove)

1. What exactly is being decided? (One sentence; options listed.)
2. By when, and what happens if it slips?
3. Who decides? (One name.)
4. Who must be consulted before?
5. Who can ratify or veto?
6. Who must be told afterward, and by whom?

If peers are stuck, name the "peer-plus-one" (the person above both) who will force the call.

## 2. Pricing reversibility (after Bezos, with Doshi's caution)

For a decision labeled "reversible," list what reversal would actually cost:
- Commitments made to customers or sales on the strength of it.
- The narrative told to executives or the board.
- Team morale and credibility from a reversal.
- Data, contracts, or integrations that would need unwinding.
- Opportunity cost of time spent before reversing.

If the total is high, treat it as a one-way door.

## 3. Premortem protocol (after Doshi and Duke)

1. "It's six months from now and this failed. Why?" Everyone writes reasons independently first.
2. Sort the reasons:
   - **Tigers:** real threats that could kill it.
   - **Paper tigers:** look scary, aren't (say why).
   - **Elephants:** things everyone knows and nobody is saying.
3. For each tiger: a mitigation, an owner, and a tripwire (the observable signal that it's happening).
4. Backcast: "It succeeded. What had to go right?" Check those are in the plan.

## 4. Kill criteria (after Duke)

```
If by <date> we haven't reached <observable state>, we will <stop / shrink / change approach>.
Agreed with: <boss / sponsor>, on <date>.
```

Tackle the hardest unknown first ("monkeys before pedestals"): the part most likely to kill the idea gets tested before building the easy parts.

## 5. Independent input (after Duke)

- Before the meeting, collect each person's view in writing, separately.
- Share the spread before discussion.
- The decider speaks last.
- Value accuracy over agreement: reward people who changed their mind on evidence.

## 6. Outcome-blind review (after Duke)

After a significant decision's outcome is known:
1. Before discussing the outcome, restate what was known at the time, the options, and the probabilities assigned.
2. Was the information-gathering proportionate?
3. Were the probabilities reasonable given what was knowable?
4. Was the outcome within the expected range, or a real surprise?
5. What would we do differently in the process, regardless of outcome?

Separate luck from skill explicitly. Don't draw rules from one result.

## 7. Voice-then-commit protocol (after Campbell, Grove, Bezos)

- **Before:** send a short pre-read naming the principles you think should govern the decision.
- **During:** state dissent once, clearly, with evidence. Ask what would change the decider's mind.
- **After:** commit publicly. Describe the decision to your own team as yours ("we decided," never "they made us"). Don't relitigate outside the room.
- **If you're the decider:** hear every position, speak last, name the principle that broke the tie, set a date to review.

## 8. Decision rights table for the Career Brain

Keep `self/current-role.md § Decision rights` current. When a decision type comes up that isn't listed, add it after agreeing with the manager. Ambiguous decision rights are the most common source of escalation leaks and of accidental overreach.
