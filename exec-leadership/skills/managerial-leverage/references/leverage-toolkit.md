# Leverage toolkit

## 1. Leverage audit (after Grove)

Run on the last two weeks of calendar and work.

| Activity | People whose work it changed | How long the effect lasts | Unique knowledge at decision time? | Leverage (high / medium / low / negative) | Keep / redesign / delegate / drop |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- Mark negative leverage explicitly: reviews that blocked a team, reopened decisions, rewrites of others' work.
- Target: convert one recurring low-leverage block per month into a high-leverage form (a written decision frame, a reusable review template, a teaching session).
- Output check: name one number in a neighboring team's output that moved because of the user this quarter.

## 2. LNO sort (after Doshi)

| Type | Examples for an AI PM | Effort standard |
|---|---|---|
| Leverage | Area strategy; the eval bar other teams adopt; a hiring decision; the decision memo on build vs buy | Excellence; protect time for it |
| Neutral | Most PRDs; routine reviews; recurring updates | Good and done |
| Overhead | Status formatting; tool admin; meeting logistics | Minimum; delegate or drop |

Ask: which L task has been postponed longest, and why?

## 3. Task-relevant maturity to supervision style (after Grove)

| TRM on this task | Style | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Low | Structured | Clear outcome and steps; frequent check-ins; fast feedback |
| Medium | Two-way | Agree the approach together; review key decisions; coach on tradeoffs |
| High | Objectives and light monitoring | Agree outcome and indicators; spot-check; stay out of the way |

A strong PM new to pricing or model evaluation is low-TRM on that task. Re-rate per task.

## 4. Conviction and consequence (after Rabois)

| | Low consequence | High consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Low conviction | Delegate fully; let them learn | Discuss; bring in more views; decide together |
| High conviction | Delegate; share your view as input | Decide or override, and explain your reasoning |

Every override spends social capital. "Okay, you're the boss" is the sound of it being spent.

## 5. Meeting classifier (after Grove)

- **Process meeting** (recurring): cadence, standing agenda, indicators reviewed. Would anyone miss it if cancelled?
- **Mission meeting** (one-off): stated objective, one accountable owner, small attendance, pre-read, written decision and actions within 24 hours.
- If more than about a quarter of the week goes to ad hoc mission meetings, find the unclear decision right or interface causing them.

## 6. Operating cadence checklist (after Hughes Johnson)

For an area the user leads or influences:
- Mission and charter written (customers, metrics, importance, risks, interfaces).
- Goals as outcomes with owners, counterweight metrics, and a stated commitment level (must-hit vs aspirational).
- A weekly mechanism that surfaces stalls early.
- A periodic business review as a candid written narrative about results and investment.
- Decision rights stated for recurring decision types; problem-solving kept local.
- A co-written escalation path for disagreements.
- Leadership forums with standing questions: what did we decide, and what cascades?

## 7. Time allocation by level (after Charan, adapted)

Charan describes only the direction of each shift. The percentage ranges below are this system's working heuristic for a typical week, useful for spotting a mix stuck at the previous level. The company's reality overrides them.

| Bucket | Senior PM | Director | VP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Own IC production (specs, analysis, hands-on AI work) | 40-60% | 10-20% | under 10% |
| Enabling others (coaching, reviews, unblocking) | 10-20% | 25-35% | 20-30% |
| Talent (hiring, performance, development) | under 5% | 10-20% | 15-25% |
| Cross-functional business work (peers, planning, tradeoffs) | 15-25% | 20-30% | 25-35% |
| Strategy and thinking time | 5-10% | 10-15% | 15-20% |
| External (customers, partners, industry) | 5-10% | 5-10% | 10-15% |

The signature failure at every transition is keeping the previous level's allocation because that work is satisfying.
