# Managerial Leverage

## Purpose

A leader's output is the output of their organization plus the neighboring organizations they influence (Grove). Past a certain level, working harder on one's own tasks stops moving anything that matters; changing the mix of activities toward high-leverage ones does. This skill makes that shift concrete, and it applies before the user has reports: Grove counted influential individual contributors as managers, judged on the output of the people they influence.

## Use when

- Overload: "I can't get to the strategic work."
- Taking on reports, a larger area, or a cross-team program.
- Designing or fixing team rituals, reviews, planning, goals.
- Readiness gaps in "leverage through others" or "operating rhythm."
- Inside `/audit-my-time`.

## Don't use when

- The question is one specific decision (use `decision-making`).

## Standards

Everything this skill produces follows the three exec-core standards. If exec-core isn't loaded, apply them as stated here:
- **Evidence:** separate what happened from inference; label any claim about another person's view or motive as a hypothesis with confidence and a falsifier; never state it as fact.
- **Posture:** when the user's behavior is part of the problem, fix the behavior before the wording.
- **Prose:** answer first, recommendation over options, owners and dates, no filler.

## Core doctrine

1. **Output = your organization's output plus your neighbors' under your influence** [Grove]. The test for any quarter: name a number in a neighboring team's output that moved because of the user.
2. **Choose activities by leverage** [Grove]. High leverage: affects many people, has long-lasting effect, or supplies unique knowledge at a key moment. Change the mix before trying to work harder.
3. **Negative leverage is real and grows with seniority** [Grove]. It shows up as reopened decisions, blocking reviews, rewriting others' work, and meddling; cost it explicitly.
4. **Sort work by leverage; spend perfectionism only where it pays** [Doshi, LNO]. Leverage tasks deserve excellence; neutral tasks deserve "done"; overhead tasks deserve minimum effort. A strategy doc postponed for a quarter is a fear signal.
5. **Chronic busyness usually means missing strategy** [Doshi]. Overload at senior levels is often many work items tracing to unmade or unagreed decisions. Fix the strategy before the calendar.
6. **Delegate by task-relevant maturity** [Grove]. With low maturity on the task, use structured, frequent check-ins; with medium, discuss reasoning together; with high, agree outcomes and monitor lightly. Re-rate when the task changes. Delegation without follow-through is abdication.
7. **Delegate by conviction and consequence; overrides spend social capital** [Rabois]. Unsure and low stakes: delegate fully and let people learn. Very sure and catastrophic stakes: don't let someone make the mistake, and explain why.
8. **Build systems others run** [Hughes Johnson]. Mission, objectives with owners, metrics with counterweights, accountability mechanisms, and an operating cadence. Make the implicit explicit, because at scale leadership runs through systems like these.
9. **Talent is the highest-leverage activity** [Grove; Horowitz; McCord; Charan]. Hiring, coaching, performance reviews, and hard people calls outlast almost anything else a leader does. Build the team the future needs.
10. **The executive's job is editing** [Rabois]. Simplify, clarify, keep one voice; cut the agenda to what people can repeat.
11. **Time application changes at every passage** [Charan]. Each level up shifts time from doing to enabling, then to selecting and developing leaders, then to cross-functional business work and protected thinking time.
12. **Meetings are the medium of managerial work** [Grove]. Process meetings need cadence and indicators; mission meetings need one owner, a pre-read, and a written decision within 24 hours. Too many ad hoc meetings means an unclear decision right or interface.
13. **Raise standards, narrow focus, take work off people's plates** [Slootman]. Sequence priorities; stop things explicitly.

## Evidence to gather

- The calendar for the last two weeks (with permission), or a described typical week.
- `self/current-role.md`: reports, teams influenced, forums run.
- `self/level-model.md` and `models/readiness.md` for the target time mix.
- Product Brain `knowledge/org/rituals.md` for recurring meetings.
- Evidence of negative leverage: decisions reopened, reviews that stalled a team.

## Reasoning procedure

1. **Establish the user's influence footprint:** reports (direct, dotted-line, none), teams influenced, forums they run or shape.
2. **Leverage audit** of the last two weeks of calendar and work (`references/leverage-toolkit.md`): each activity's reach, durability, and whether it supplied unique knowledge at decision time; mark negative leverage.
3. **Compare time allocation with the target level's** (Charan's time applications; `next-level-readiness`).
4. **Find the root of overload:** missing strategy, unclear decision rights, low-maturity delegation, meetings with no owner, or the user's comfort work (the competency trap).
5. **Redesign:** what to stop, delegate (with the right TRM style), systematize (a template, a decision frame, a review cadence), or teach once so it stops coming back to the user.
6. **For leaders of people:** audit coaching and staffing time, 1:1 ownership, review quality, and whether the team could run a week without the user.
7. **Set one change per month** as a commitment, with the observable output it should produce.

## Diagnostic questions

- Which number in a neighboring team's output moved because of you this quarter?
- What did you do last week that only you could have done?
- What would break if you disappeared for two weeks?
- Which decision did you reopen, or which review did you slow, that you could have left alone?
- Which leverage task have you postponed longest?
- Who could take over something you still do?

## Failure modes

- Being the best individual contributor on the team, permanently.
- Hoarding the interesting problems.
- Delegating once and disappearing (abdication), or delegating and hovering (no real delegation).
- More meetings as the fix for coordination problems caused by unclear strategy or decision rights.
- Negative leverage: reopening decided things, rewriting others' docs, "small improvements" to a team's plan.
- Perfectionism on neutral tasks, which earns a reputation for polish without a record of impact.

## Tensions in the doctrine

- **Systems vs intensity.** Hughes Johnson builds operating systems others run; Slootman drives change through leader-driven intensity and urgency. Systems scale and outlast the leader; intensity works in turnarounds with real authority. An aspiring leader without the mandate should build systems.
- **Coaching vs talent density.** Cagan and Charan center the manager's job on developing people; McCord centers it on hiring great people and being honest when fit ends. The right emphasis depends on whether the company can hire top-of-market.
- **Process vs speed.** Grove's disciplined decision process vs Bezos's high-velocity, reversible decisions. Use process for one-way doors and speed for two-way doors (see `decision-making`).
