# AI product operators and researchers on leading under capability uncertainty

## Why this source matters here
This dossier combines attributable views from three vantage points. Researchers ran field experiments inside real firms: Mollick with Dell'Acqua, Lakhani and colleagues at BCG (2023) and Procter & Gamble (2024-2025). Product-management thought leaders adapted their frameworks to AI: Cagan and SVPG (2024-2025). Chief product officers at frontier labs described how they roadmap when capability shifts every few months: Weil at OpenAI and Krieger at Anthropic, both on Lenny's Podcast in 2025. MIT NANDA's 2025 enterprise study and METR's 2025 developer trial serve as empirical counterweights. The common lesson for an aspiring AI product executive: AI capability is uneven, moves quickly, and is badly perceived. The leader's value lies in mapping it empirically, betting on where it is going without promising what it cannot yet do, and redesigning how the organization works so individual gains show up in business results. Transfer caveats: lab executives run the model roadmap and benefit from customers expecting improvement; researchers' experiments cover specific tasks and model generations. Everything here is dated and some of it will be stale within a year.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. The frontier is jagged and invisible, so map capability task by task
- **Claim:** In the BCG experiment (758 consultants, GPT-4, 2023), tasks inside the frontier saw consultants with AI complete 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with about 40% higher-quality output. On a task designed to sit outside the frontier, consultants with AI were 19 percentage points less likely to reach the correct answer, often persuaded by fluent but wrong output. Tasks that look equally hard can fall on opposite sides of the line, and nobody can see it in advance.
- **Source:** Dell'Acqua et al. (HBS WP 24-013, Sep 2023); Mollick, "Centaurs and Cyborgs" (Sep 2023).
- **Changes coaching how:** When an executive proposes "use AI for X" because a demo of a similar task impressed them, the coach helps the user ask for a task-level map with evidence (eval results on the company's own cases), not an inference from the demo. Explaining the jagged frontier to executives is a core AI-leadership communication skill. It replaces both "AI can do anything" and "AI is unreliable."
- **Boundary conditions:** The specific findings are for GPT-4 in 2023. The frontier has moved. The jaggedness persists, but which tasks fall where has changed (Lakhani, 2026).

### D2. The frontier moves, so bet on its direction without promising its arrival
- **Claim:** Mollick's fourth rule (2024) is to assume the current AI is the worst you will ever use. Weil (Apr 2025, per the episode notes) describes building for capabilities that are just emerging, with model generations arriving every few months, and says the quality of a team's evals caps what its AI product can become. Krieger (Jun 2025, per the notes) says product teams embedded with researchers deliver far more than teams wrapping a standard API. Ng (Oct 2025) observes teams removing scaffolding as models improve.
- **Source:** Co-Intelligence (2024); Lenny's Podcast with Weil (Apr 2025) and Krieger (Jun 2025); Ng, The Batch (Oct 2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach helps the user keep "frontier-riding" bets in the portfolio: work that is just beyond today's capability, with an eval threshold that triggers investment and a rule to re-run evals on every major model release. The coach also blocks customer or executive commitments that depend on capabilities the evals do not yet show.
- **Boundary conditions:** Frontier-lab leaders control their own model roadmap and benefit when the market expects improvement. A company consuming models controls neither timing nor direction. Plan for improvement as a probability, not a schedule (see ai-economics-a16z on not banking on cost declines).

### D3. Under capability uncertainty, plan in lanes with triggers, not fixed feature dates
- **Claim:** The show notes attribute to Weil (2025) an emphasis on continuous planning over fixed plans, since roadmaps change as capability shifts. Mollick (May 2025) tells the internal "Lab" to build benchmarks from the company's own real tasks and to test new models the moment they appear, and to make "provocations," demos that show what could soon be possible. Husain's experiment-based roadmap (see ai-evals-practitioners) is the team-level version.
- **Source:** Weil episode notes (Apr 2025); Mollick, "Making AI Work" (May 2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach converts the user's AI roadmap into three lanes. Committed outcomes use proven capability. Capability-gated bets carry explicit eval thresholds. Frontier probes are cheap, time-boxed and re-tested on each model release. Each lane gets its own communication to executives: dates for the first, triggers for the second, learning for the third.
- **Boundary conditions:** Enterprise customers and sales teams need commitments. Commit to outcomes and service levels you can meet with current capability. Never commit to a model's future behavior.

### D4. Individual gains do not add up to organizational gains without organizational redesign
- **Claim:** Mollick (May 2025) observes that workers self-report large productivity gains and controlled experiments confirm many of them, yet firms report modest results. His prescription has three parts. Leadership paints a vivid picture of future work, states plainly what efficiency gains will mean for jobs and pay, rewards people who find transformative uses, and models use publicly. The Lab (technologists and domain experts) spreads what works quickly, builds internal benchmarks, and prototypes ahead of capability. The Crowd (all employees) discovers uses in their own work, given clear zones where experimentation is permitted.
- **Source:** Mollick, "Making AI Work: Leadership, Lab, and Crowd" (May 2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user owns AI adoption, or wants to, the coach asks which of the three parts is missing. It then asks what the user has said, or can get leadership to say, about job security, and whether incentives reward revealing productivity gains or punish it.
- **Boundary conditions:** The three-part model is Mollick's prescription from observation and advising. It has not been tested experimentally at the organizational level. Promises about jobs must be ones leadership will actually keep.

### D5. Shadow AI is a signal to harvest, not only a risk to suppress
- **Claim:** Mollick's "secret cyborgs" hide their AI use out of fear of punishment, losing credit for their work, or triggering layoffs and higher quotas. NANDA (Jul 2025) found workers at over 90% of surveyed companies used personal AI tools for work, while only about 40% of companies had bought an official LLM subscription. Shadow use often outperformed official initiatives.
- **Source:** Mollick (May 2025 and earlier posts); NANDA, "The GenAI Divide," section 3.3 (Jul 2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach frames shadow usage as the cheapest use-case discovery the user has. Surface it safely (amnesty, sanctioned tools, permitted zones), learn what works, and move it into supported channels. Security and privacy leaders co-own the move from shadow to sanctioned.
- **Boundary conditions:** Regulated or confidential data in personal tools is a real exposure. Harvesting insight must not mean ignoring leakage.

### D6. Pilot purgatory is the default; measure P&L, and take buy/partner seriously
- **Claim:** NANDA (Jul 2025) reports that despite heavy enterprise investment, about 95% of organizations saw no measurable P&L return. Some 60% evaluated enterprise AI tools, 20% reached pilot, and 5% reached production. Failures traced to brittle workflows, systems that do not retain feedback or adapt, and misalignment with daily work. External partnerships reached deployment about twice as often as internal builds (about 67% vs. 33%). Successful organizations bought more than they built, gave line managers rather than central labs the power to adopt, and chose tools that integrate deeply and adapt.
- **Source:** MIT NANDA, "The GenAI Divide" (Jul 2025), executive summary and section 6.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach pushes the user to report P&L or operational impact, not pilot counts. It asks two questions of any internal build: will the system learn from feedback in the workflow, and has a buy or partner option been costed? It treats "we launched eight pilots" as a warning sign, not an achievement.
- **Boundary conditions:** Interviews with 52 organizations and a survey of 153 leaders, self-reported, with success defined differently across organizations. The report itself warns that the buy/build gap may reflect organizational capability rather than approach. The authors are also associated with an agent-infrastructure initiative whose thesis the report supports. Use it as a strong signal, not settled fact.

### D7. Probabilistic behavior reshapes all four product risks
- **Claim:** Cagan and Nika (Apr 2024) run SVPG's four risks through AI. Feasibility: the same input can produce different outputs, so teams must decide what error rate the use case tolerates, and data quality becomes critical. Usability: users need transparency and a reason to trust the output. Value: AI features must beat existing alternatives, not exist for marketing. Viability: unit economics, data rights and copyright, regulation, ethics and misuse. They note that viability mistakes tend to dominate the headlines.
- **Source:** Cagan & Nika, "AI Product Management" (SVPG, Apr 2024).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks that the user's AI bets and executive reviews address each risk with AI-specific questions. Viability gets explicit treatment (cost, legal, reputational, safety), because that is where executives and boards feel exposed and where the user can show enterprise judgment.
- **Boundary conditions:** The four-risk frame assumes empowered product teams in a tech company. In IT-led or regulated settings, risk and compliance functions hold some of these decisions.

### D8. As delivery is automated, discovery and judgment become the job
- **Claim:** Cagan (Dec 2024) argues the PM role is becoming more essential and harder, not easier, and cautions against handing AI tools to people without product fundamentals. In "A Vision For Product Teams" (Feb 2025) he sketches a 3-10 year future of much smaller teams, where delivery-oriented roles are most exposed and the discovery trio (PM, designer, engineer) endures. Krieger (Jun 2025, per the notes) reports most of Anthropic's product code being AI-written, which moved the bottleneck to deciding what to build and to review and merge. Ng (Jul 2025) independently calls this the product management bottleneck.
- **Source:** Cagan, SVPG (Dec 2024, Feb 2025); Krieger episode notes (Jun 2025); Ng, The Batch (Jul 2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach treats the quality and speed of product decisions, and the structure that produces them, as the user's executive contribution. That structure includes team shape, PM-to-engineer ratios, and review capacity. The coach also asks where the new bottleneck sits in the user's organization (decisions, review, legal, design) and who owns fixing it.
- **Boundary conditions:** These are forecasts with wide timelines. Leaders who cut teams on the promise of capability that has not yet arrived create delivery risk. Measure first (D10).

### D9. AI levels skill and breaks silos, but can also erode judgment and expertise
- **Claim:** In the BCG study, lower performers gained more than top performers. In the P&G experiment (776 professionals, summer 2024), individuals with AI performed as well as two-person teams without it. With AI, R&D and commercial specialists produced more balanced solutions, and participants reported more positive and fewer negative emotions. On the risk side, Dell'Acqua's recruiter study (2022) found people using a high-quality AI put in less effort and judged worse ("falling asleep at the wheel"). Lakhani (2026) reports follow-up findings that AI can defend flawed recommendations persuasively, and that "self-automators" who hand over whole tasks may not develop expertise.
- **Source:** Mollick (Sep 2023; Mar 2025); Dell'Acqua (2022, by reference); Lakhani (Mar 2026).
- **Changes coaching how:** In org design, the coach asks the user to keep humans exercising judgment on out-of-frontier work, to design review so that humans check outputs rather than rubber-stamp them, and to protect how junior people build expertise. It also asks the user to use AI's silo-breaking effect deliberately, for example in cross-functional planning.
- **Boundary conditions:** Effects were measured on specific tasks with specific models over short periods. Long-term effects on expertise are unknown.

### D10. People misjudge AI's effect on their own work, so measure it
- **Claim:** In METR's trial (Jul 2025), experienced developers forecast a 24% speedup, were measured 19% slower, and afterward still believed they had been about 20% faster. Mollick (2025) notes the same gap at company level: large self-reported gains, modest firm-level results.
- **Source:** METR (Jul 2025); Mollick, "Making AI Work" (May 2025).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach insists that the user's adoption programs report measured outcomes (cycle time, quality, cost, revenue) against a baseline, not usage or satisfaction surveys. This is how the user earns credibility as a hype-discipline leader with finance and the board.
- **Boundary conditions:** METR studied a small, specialized population using early-2025 tools, and says itself that the result does not generalize to most developers. The lesson is about measurement, not about whether AI helps.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Jagged-frontier map (from D1, D9):**
| Task in the workflow | Eval evidence (pass rate on our cases) | Cost of an error | Can the error be detected? | Human role (centaur split / cyborg blend / human only) | Re-test trigger |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
Fill one row per task, not per product. Tasks with high error cost and low detectability stay human-led regardless of the demo.

**Three-lane AI roadmap (from D2, D3):**
- Committed: outcomes built on capability that evals already show. Communicate dates.
- Capability-gated: bets with a named eval threshold. Communicate the trigger and the investment it unlocks.
- Frontier probes: cheap, time-boxed, re-run on each major model release. Communicate what was learned.
Review lane membership after every major model release and quarterly.

**Leadership-Lab-Crowd audit (from D4, D5):**
- Leadership: Is there a concrete picture of future work? An explicit position on jobs? Incentives for revealing gains? Visible personal use?
- Lab: Who spreads what works? Is there a benchmark built from our own tasks? Who tests new models on day one?
- Crowd: Are there permitted zones? Sanctioned tools? Amnesty for shadow use? Training that is hands-on?
Score each part red, amber or green. The user's scope opportunity is usually the red part nobody owns.

**Four-risk AI review (from D7):** for each bet, one line each on feasibility (tolerable error rate, eval evidence), usability (how users calibrate trust), value (compared with the current alternative), and viability (unit cost, data rights, regulation, reputation, misuse).

**Adoption outcome scorecard (from D6, D10):** production deployments, not pilots. Measured change in a business metric against a baseline. Cost per outcome. The share of workflows where the system learns from feedback. The share of initiatives that were bought or partnered versus built, with results for each.

## Diagnostic questions
- Where exactly is the frontier for the user's product's core tasks, and what evidence says so?
- Which roadmap items depend on capabilities no eval has shown yet, and has anyone promised them externally?
- What happens in the user's organization the day a major model is released? Does anyone re-run evals?
- Is the user's company stuck in pilots? How many AI initiatives reached production with measured impact?
- What has leadership said about AI and jobs, and do employees believe it?
- Where is shadow AI use happening, and who is learning from it?
- Where has the bottleneck moved as building got faster, and who owns it?
- Are adoption results measured or self-reported?

## Observable signals
Positive:
- The user explains the jagged frontier to executives with the company's own examples and eval evidence.
- The user's roadmap separates committed, capability-gated and frontier work, and executives use that language.
- The user re-evaluates bets promptly after major model releases and reports what changed.
- The user reports adoption in production deployments and measured outcomes, not pilot or login counts.
- The user has converted shadow use into sanctioned patterns together with security and legal.
- The user raises job-impact and expertise-development questions in AI planning before HR has to.

Negative:
- The user extrapolates from a demo to a broad capability claim.
- The user commits customers or executives to capabilities expected from "the next model."
- The user celebrates pilot counts or license usage as success.
- The user bans or ignores shadow AI use without learning from it.
- The user plans headcount reductions on forecast capability rather than measured outcomes.
- The user repeats vendor or lab-executive claims about capability trajectories as fact.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using "assume this is the worst AI you'll ever use" to justify shipping products that fail today.
- Using "the frontier is jagged" as a reason to avoid committing to anything.
- Copying frontier-lab habits (ship early, iterate in public) into high-risk enterprise contexts.
- Reading NANDA's 95% figure as "AI doesn't work" rather than "most implementations are designed badly." Or overlooking its sampling limits.
- Setting up a central Lab and then routing all adoption through it, which recreates the bottleneck NANDA warns about.
- Citing Cagan's small-team vision as a current staffing plan.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Mollick ("always invite AI to the table," broad experimentation) vs. Casado and Bornstein (narrow domains, reduce model complexity):** Mollick is right for individual and organizational learning. The a16z view is right for what to productize and sell. The user needs both, applied to different lanes.
- **Mollick's central Lab and Ng's central AI unit vs. NANDA's line managers over central labs:** centralize benchmarks, platforms and the spreading of what works. Push ownership of adoption and results to line leaders. A Lab that becomes a gate reproduces pilot purgatory.
- **Lab leaders (ship early, refine in public; Weil) vs. risk calibration (ai-evals-practitioners; Cagan on viability):** labs have brand, user bases and safety teams built for public iteration. Most companies should keep public iteration for low-risk tiers.
- **Grove on strategic inflection points and Horowitz on wartime leadership:** both support urgency when AI changes the basis of competition. The jagged frontier says urgency must still be task-specific. Being urgent about the wrong tasks wastes the window.
- **Pfeffer on power:** AI adoption redistributes credit and control (secret cyborgs hide gains to keep them). The user should expect political resistance where AI makes someone's expertise or headcount less central, and model the stakeholders accordingly.
- **Lencioni on trust:** employees who fear layoffs will not reveal what works. Adoption depends on the trust conditions Lencioni describes, which leadership has to earn with actions, not statements.
- **Cagan (empowered product teams) vs. Mollick's Crowd:** Cagan puts discovery in product teams; Mollick locates much discovery in every employee's daily work. For internal-productivity AI, Mollick fits better. For customer-facing products, Cagan does.
- **Duke on decisions under uncertainty:** frontier bets are bets. Score them on whether the process was good, including eval triggers and kill criteria, not on whether the next model happened to arrive in time.

## Limits and biases
- Incentives: Weil and Krieger lead product at model providers whose business benefits from expectations of rapid improvement. Their claims here come from episode show notes, not full transcripts, and should be treated as summary-level.
- Evidence: the BCG and P&G studies are strong field experiments, but they cover specific tasks, firms and model generations (GPT-4 in 2023; 2024 models at P&G). Mollick's organizational prescriptions go beyond what the experiments tested.
- NANDA: small, self-reported sample; varying definitions of success; possible confounding (the report says so); affiliation with an agent-infrastructure initiative.
- METR: 16 developers, one setting, early-2025 tools. It cautions against perception, not against AI.
- Cagan: the SVPG frame comes from product consulting for tech companies. The small-team vision is an explicit 3-10 year forecast.
- Staleness: this is the most time-sensitive dossier in the roster. Re-verify capability claims and adoption statistics before using them in coaching, and prefer the user's own eval evidence over any figure here.
