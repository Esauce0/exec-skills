# Will Larson

## Why this source matters here
Larson has been an engineering leader and executive at Uber, Stripe, Calm, Carta and Imprint, and has written about it publicly on lethain.com for over a decade. His Staff Engineer guidance speaks to senior people who lead without formal authority. That is structurally the situation of a senior PM, so it transfers almost directly. His executive material (The Engineering Executive's Primer) covers what changes once you hold an executive seat. Filter out the engineering-specific parts: migrations, technical quality, capitalization of engineering costs. Keep the parts about how authority, rooms, executives and strategy work.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. Your authority is on loan; stay aligned with the person lending it
- **Claim:** A title's authority is lent by a larger organizational authority, usually your manager as sponsor. People expect seniority to bring autonomy. What it actually brings is the job of acting as the organization's agent, with less of a safety net. Nothing destroys trust faster than surprising your manager. Manage the information flow in both directions so neither of you is surprised. Pass up context (frustrations, scaling problems) as information, not as problems for them to solve.
- **Source:** "Staying aligned with authority"; "Ways engineering managers get stuck" (only-managing-down and never-managing-up failure modes).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user reports being "blindsided," or reports that a senior leader was, the coach looks for a surprise the user caused or allowed. It prescribes controls: a weekly written update, a shared list of open risks, and a standing one-on-one agenda item for "things that could surprise either of us." It treats every surprise like an incident, with a short review of what failed.
- **Boundary conditions:** Alignment does not mean replacing your judgment with your manager's, or using "managing up" to hide problems. Where the sponsor is weak or leaving, the user needs other sponsors (see Hewlett).

### D2. Get into rooms by bringing something they lack; stay by being low-friction
- **Claim:** Getting into a decision forum takes three things: something useful the room doesn't have, a perspective different from current members, and a sponsor willing to spend capital to bring you in. Staying requires continuing to deliver, staying aligned, disagreeing and then committing rather than withholding consent, and understanding what the room is really for. People get removed for misreading the room's purpose, dominating the airtime, or embarrassing their sponsor. Leave rooms that don't need you.
- **Source:** "Getting in the room."
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user wants a seat at a leadership forum, the coach asks what unique input the user brings (customer data, AI model behavior, market signal) and who would sponsor them. After a forum, it reviews the user's airtime, their fit with the room's real purpose, and whether they made their sponsor look good.
- **Boundary conditions:** Rooms are not where all the work happens. Some high-status forums are status displays, and time spent there is preening (D5).

### D3. Present to executives to get their view, not to win; lead with the answer
- **Claim:** Executive meetings are for planning, reporting status or resolving misalignment. The goal is to learn what the executives think. Open with situation, complication, question and answer, putting the summary first (borrowed from Minto). Failure modes: fighting feedback, which makes executives stop giving it; hiding problems; raising a problem without a proposed answer; an academic, build-up structure; and fixating on your preferred outcome. Send an early draft to one attendee and ask what to change.
- **Source:** "Present to executives."
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach reviews the user's deck or memo for the answer in the first paragraph and for problems that are disclosed rather than hidden. Before the meeting it has the user pre-wire one attendee with a draft. In the debrief it asks, "What did you learn about their priorities?" rather than "Did they approve it?"
- **Boundary conditions:** Some meetings really are decision meetings, where a clear recommendation with an explicit ask is right. Getting their perspective is not the same as having no position.

### D4. Extract the kernel; translate rather than oppose
- **Claim:** Executives are generally directionally correct but specifically wrong. Behind a specific suggestion ("just use an off-the-shelf model") there is usually a real concern ("this timeline feels too slow"). Say the concern back to confirm it and address it, rather than debating the suggestion. If you can steer the executive to a better option, do. If not, turn their idea into something workable, such as a narrow test instead of a rewrite. Don't translate so far that you are pursuing your own agenda.
- **Source:** "Extract the kernel"; "Executive translation."
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user describes an executive's "bad idea," the coach asks what worry sits under it and drafts a confirming question. It warns against winning the argument on specifics while leaving the real concern unaddressed.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some executive ideas are wrong in direction too. Ethical, safety and legal issues call for direct escalation, not translation.

### D5. Work on what matters: avoid snacking, preening and chasing ghosts
- **Claim:** Senior time is scarce. Avoid snacking (easy, low-impact work), preening (low-impact, high-visibility work) and chasing ghosts (bringing in fixes from a previous context that don't apply). Put time into existential issues; areas that matter to the company and still have room to act; growing the team; editing (small changes that unblock others); finishing work; and work only you can do.
- **Source:** "Work on what matters."
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach sorts the user's current project list into these categories and pushes toward "matters and has room." That is often an unglamorous problem under-served by senior people, not the crowded flagship bet. Visibility requests get checked against the preening definition.
- **Boundary conditions:** In some orgs, visibility work is how you earn sponsorship. The coach should treat it as a deliberate, limited investment, not avoid it entirely (see Ibarra, Hewlett).

### D6. Peers are your first team; build one story out of CEO, peer and org views
- **Claim:** An executive must put the peer team ahead of their own organization and act as a company-first problem solver. The CEO, peers and your own org each have views of a problem that are incomplete but valid. The executive's job is to combine them into one account, not to anchor on one. Executives are supported, tolerated or resented, and delivering a small number of meaningful changes moves you toward supported. Leadership teams fall apart when members believe they gain more by taking capacity from each other than by creating it for the organization.
- **Source:** "Make your peers your first team"; "Balancing your CEO, peers, and Engineering"; "Gelling your engineering leadership team."
- **Changes coaching how:** In cross-functional conflict the coach has the user write each party's account in its strongest form before proposing a solution. It asks which state the user is in with each peer (supported, tolerated or resented) and what evidence shows it.
- **Boundary conditions:** Larson judges executives over years, not quarters. Short-term political pressure can punish a slow approach, so pair it with visible early delivery.

### D7. In a new role, understand before you change; avoid looking busy
- **Claim:** The top onboarding trap is changing things before you understand how the problem is shaped. Others: judging without context, "at my last job we...", and too many lightly held opinions creating churn. Learn first (listening tours, shadowing, explicit expectations from your manager). Share what you observe weekly. Then limit yourself to one or two changes, because organizations can only absorb a few at once. New leaders feel pressure to be seen doing something valuable. Solve for both the real problem ("physics") and how progress looks ("perception"). Pressure without a plan fails, and perfect plans nobody can see lose support.
- **Source:** "Your first 90 days as CTO or VP Engineering"; "Physics and perception."
- **Changes coaching how:** For a new scope or promotion, the coach builds a 30/60/90 plan that sequences learning, shared observations and one or two changes. It flags opinion volume as a risk.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a real crisis, triage comes before a listening tour (compare Watkins's turnaround situations).

### D8. You can do strategy from any level: write five, then synthesize
- **Claim:** Strategy is a diagnosis plus guiding policies that explain tradeoffs. You don't need an executive title. Document five related past decisions and pull out the common pattern ("take five, then synthesize"). Where you lack a mandate, model the approach, document it and share it. Recording how past decisions were made ("strategy archaeology") clarifies the present.
- **Source:** "Who gets to do strategy?"; "Write five, then synthesize" (lethain.com). The diagnosis and guiding-policy structure comes from Rumelt.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user wants to "own strategy" without a mandate, the coach has them write a diagnosis of their AI product area from real past decisions, rather than a vision deck. That artifact becomes promotion evidence that they think at the enterprise level.
- **Boundary conditions:** A strategy nobody enforces is commentary. Get the sponsor to adopt it (D1).

## Frameworks in operational form
**Surprise-prevention controls:** weekly written update (progress, risks, asks); a shared risk list; a standing one-on-one question, "anything that could surprise you or me?"; a short review after any surprise.

**Room entry and retention checklist:** (1) What unique input do I bring? (2) Who is my sponsor, and do they agree? (3) What is this room actually for? (4) Pre-reads sent. (5) Airtime under my fair share. (6) After the meeting: did my sponsor look good?

**Executive pre-flight:** answer in the first paragraph (situation, complication, question, answer); problems disclosed along with a proposed answer; early draft sent to one attendee; one question prepared to draw out their perspective; plan to take feedback without defending.

**Kernel script:** "It sounds like the most important thing here is [concern]. Is that right?" If yes, address the concern. If you can't redirect, propose a scoped, fast test of their idea.

**Portfolio triage:** label every project as snack, preen, ghost, existential, room-and-attention, editing, finishing or only-me. Stop or delegate the first three categories.

## Diagnostic questions
- When did the user last surprise their manager, and what control would have prevented it?
- For each forum the user wants to join, what do they bring that the room lacks, and who sponsors them?
- Does the user's last executive deck state its answer in the first paragraph?
- In the last pushback from an executive, what was the underlying concern, and did the user address it?
- What share of the user's time goes to snacking or preening?
- Is the user supported, tolerated or resented by each peer?

## Observable signals
- Positive: sends updates unprompted; pre-wires executives; confirms the underlying concern before responding; opens with the answer; drops low-value forums; works on unglamorous problems that matter; writes a diagnosis from real decisions.
- Negative: the manager learns news from others; argues specifics with executives; decks build up to the answer; reopens decisions after "disagree and commit"; chases flagship visibility; imports practices from a previous job; many half-finished initiatives.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Reading "stay aligned" as "always agree." Larson says explicitly to keep your own view.
- Translating executive ideas so heavily that you are pushing your own agenda under their name.
- Using "work on what matters" to avoid necessary visibility or glue work.
- Treating engineering-specific material (migrations, capitalization) as general doctrine.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Pfeffer:** Larson advises low friction and alignment with authority, while Pfeffer advises building power and sometimes breaking norms. Larson fits someone who is already sponsored and inside the room. Pfeffer fits someone who is blocked or has no sponsor.
- **Grove and Scott:** They favor direct confrontation. Larson favors translation. Confrontation works when stakes are high and the user has credibility; translation works when standing is thin.
- **Watkins:** Watkins pushes for early wins, Larson for understanding first. They reconcile as one or two visible changes after a learning phase.
- **Cagan:** Cagan distrusts roadmap theater. Larson treats managing perception as a legitimate part of the job.
- **Minto:** They agree. Larson applies SCQA and the pyramid principle directly.

## Limits and biases
Engineering-executive context at venture-backed tech companies (Uber, Stripe, Calm, Carta). Largely one practitioner's experience, written in public; not empirical research. Fits high-growth US tech cultures that value writing. Less relevant to heavily hierarchical or non-tech enterprises.
