# Richard P. Rumelt

## Why this source matters here
Rumelt is a strategy professor at UCLA Anderson. His early research was on diversification and on firm resources, and he has consulted to companies and governments. His two books criticize what organizations actually produce under the label "strategy": ambitions, lists of initiatives, and jargon. A PM moving to Director or VP will be asked for strategy by executives and board members who can tell a real diagnosis from fluff. The kernel gives the user a test for their own documents and a spine for executive and board narratives. His examples are corporate, military and government cases, not product management, so the coach has to translate them to product scope.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. A strategy responds to a specific challenge and has a kernel: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions
- **Claim:** Strategy is a form of problem solving. The **diagnosis** names the challenge and explains it, simplifying a complicated situation by picking out the aspects that matter most. The **guiding policy** is the overall approach for dealing with the obstacles the diagnosis found; it narrows the options without spelling out every step. The **coherent actions** are coordinated steps that commit resources and reinforce each other to carry out the policy. If any of the three is missing, there is no strategy. Good strategy concentrates effort on a few pivot points where it has the most effect.
- **Source:** Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, ch. 5 ("The Kernel of Good Strategy"); McKinsey Quarterly 2011.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks every strategy document the user writes against the kernel. Can the diagnosis be stated in two sentences, and does it explain why the problem is hard? Does the guiding policy rule anything out? Do the actions commit resources, and do they reinforce each other or merely sit side by side? The coach then reorders the product strategy document into kernel order.
- **Boundary conditions:** The kernel tests the logic of a strategy. It is not a template to fill in, and Rumelt criticizes fill-in-the-blank strategy. At Director level, the user's kernel sits inside the company's strategy. Their diagnosis should build on the company's diagnosis, not ignore it.

### D2. Fluff is a warning sign: inflated language that imitates strategic thinking
- **Claim:** Fluff restates the obvious in buzzwords and abstract language to create the appearance of high-level thought. Rumelt's example is a bank whose stated strategy was "customer-centric intermediation." Translated, it means "we are a bank."
- **Source:** Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, ch. 3 (bad strategy hallmarks); McKinsey Quarterly 2011 (by reference for the example).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach flags abstract, jargon-heavy strategy sentences and runs two tests. First, translate the sentence into plain words: if the result is obvious, it is fluff. Second, ask what the team would do differently tomorrow if the sentence were true: if nothing, it is fluff. AI strategy language is especially prone to this ("AI-first," "leverage GenAI to unlock customer value"). The coach requires the plain-words version before any executive audience sees the document.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some shared vocabulary is legitimate shorthand inside an organization. Plain wording alone does not make something strategy; a plainly stated goal is still a goal (D3).

### D3. Goals are not strategy; ambitions without a diagnosis are bad strategy
- **Claim:** Rumelt names four hallmarks of bad strategy:
  - Failure to face the challenge. If you have not identified and analyzed the obstacles, what you have is a stretch goal, a budget or a wish list.
  - Mistaking goals for strategy. Statements of desire and exhortation are presented as a plan. His example is a CEO's "20/20" plan of 20% growth and a 20% margin.
  - Bad strategic objectives. Some are a "dog's dinner," a long, unfocused list labeled strategic. Others are "blue sky," a restatement of the desired end state with no idea how to reach it.
  - Fluff (D2).

  Good objectives are proximate: close enough to be feasible, and clear enough that the organization can act on them. The Crux restates the central idea: leaders become strategists by focusing on challenges rather than goals.
- **Source:** Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, chs. 3 and 7 ("Bad Strategy", "Proximate Objectives"); McKinsey Quarterly 2011; The Crux (publisher description).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user answers "what's your strategy?" with OKRs or targets, the coach steers back to the obstacle. Why hasn't this already happened? What stands in the way, and why is it hard? The coach also helps the user set proximate objectives for their team: the next objective that is achievable and clears up the most uncertainty.
- **Boundary conditions:** Goals and OKRs are useful for aligning execution once a strategy exists. Rumelt's objection is to using them in place of a strategy, not to goals as such.

### D4. Strategy requires choice, and choice disappoints someone
- **Claim:** Bad strategy is common because leaders are unwilling or unable to choose among competing interests. Two other causes are template-driven planning (vision, mission and values filled into a form) and "New Thought" optimism, the belief that positive thinking produces results. Good strategy focuses resources, which means saying no to some constituencies.
- **Source:** Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, ch. 4 ("Why So Much Bad Strategy?").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks what the user's strategy deprioritizes and who will be unhappy about it. If the answer is "no one," the coach suspects no choice has been made. It then plans with the user how to communicate with and build support among the losing constituencies (organizational politics).
- **Boundary conditions:** At Director level the user often lacks the authority to make the choice. Their job is then a recommendation that frames the choice clearly for the person who decides, including what each option costs.

### D5. Find the crux: the most important challenge that you can actually address
- **Claim:** Every organization faces many challenges. The strategist concentrates on the addressable ones and finds the crux, the part of the problem that can be overcome and promises the most progress. Rumelt suggests weighing each challenge by importance and by addressability. He calls the hardest problems "gnarly": they arrive without clear structure, the options have to be invented, and the link between action and outcome is fuzzy. His "Strategy Foundry" is a working method in which a small group of senior leaders focuses on challenges before discussing goals or solutions (details by reference).
- **Source:** The Crux (2022), by reference; Hachette publisher description (consulted).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach has the user list the challenges facing their product or organization, rate each on importance and addressability, name the crux, and build the plan around it. At enterprise level, the coach asks whether the user's crux is a company problem or only a problem for their function.
- **Boundary conditions:** Naming the crux is a judgment and can be wrong. Rumelt treats good strategy as a hypothesis about what will work, so the user should state what evidence would show the crux was misdiagnosed.

### D6. Build executive and board narratives in kernel order, and present the strategy as a hypothesis (coach synthesis)
- **Claim:** This entry applies Rumelt; it is not his explicit guidance on boards. The kernel gives a spine for a board or executive narrative:
  - What changed, and the diagnosis of the challenge.
  - The guiding policy, including what we will not do.
  - The few coherent actions and the resources committed to them.
  - The signals that will show whether the hypothesis is right.
- **Source:** Derived from D1 to D5 and from Rumelt's treatment of strategy as a hypothesis (Good Strategy/Bad Strategy, Part III, "The Science of Strategy").
- **Changes coaching how:** The board-communication skill uses the kernel as its outline. It rejects decks that open with vision and targets and never state the challenge. It requires a "what we are not doing" section and a set of signals to test the hypothesis. For executive prose, a one-paragraph kernel summary must survive the fluff test before any slides are built.
- **Boundary conditions:** Boards also need financial, risk and governance content. The kernel is the strategic spine of the deck, not the whole deck. When the audience expects the answer first (Minto), lead with the guiding policy and put the diagnosis immediately after it.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Kernel test** (run on any strategy document):
| Element | Pass condition | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis | Names the challenge and why it is hard, in 2 sentences | Restates a goal or lists market trends |
| Guiding policy | A choice that rules options out | "Be customer-centric," "win in AI" |
| Coherent actions | Commit resources and reinforce each other | A list of unrelated initiatives |

**Bad-strategy screen:** Is the challenge ever stated? Are the "strategies" really goals or targets? Is the objectives list a dog's dinner (more than 5 items, not prioritized) or blue sky (the end state with no path)? Does any sentence fail the plain-words test?

**Crux worksheet:** list challenges → score importance (1-5) and addressability (1-5) → choose the one with the best combination of leverage and feasibility → write the guiding policy for it → pick one proximate objective → name the signal that would falsify the diagnosis.

**Board spine:** what changed → diagnosis → guiding policy (including what we won't do) → actions and resources → signals and next review.

## Diagnostic questions
- Can the user say in two sentences what is hard about their area and why?
- Is the user's "strategy" a set of goals, a budget, or a list of initiatives?
- What did the strategy make the user give up?
- Which sentences in the user's last strategy document would a skeptical board member mark as fluff?
- Is the user's crux addressable with resources they control or could get?
- Is there a proximate objective the team can reach this quarter?

## Observable signals
- Positive: opens with the challenge, not the ambition; states what the team will not do; actions connect to one guiding policy; objectives are near-term and achievable; plain language survives questioning; names what would show the diagnosis was wrong.
- Negative: vision and targets presented as strategy; long flat lists of priorities; buzzword-heavy statements that say nothing new; no losers from the plan; objectives that restate the end goal; revising the strategy by adding items rather than choosing among them.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using the kernel as a fill-in template, which is exactly the template-style planning Rumelt criticizes.
- Using "fluff" to dismiss any abstraction, including legitimate conceptual framing.
- Dismissing goals and OKRs entirely instead of placing them after the strategy.
- Choosing a crux because it is the most interesting problem rather than the most important and addressable one.
- Writing a diagnosis that is really a disguised argument for a solution the user already prefers.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Doshi, Grove (OKRs):** OKR-driven planning can produce exactly the goals-as-strategy Rumelt criticizes. Resolution: the kernel comes first, and OKRs measure the coherent actions. When the strategy is already clear and the issue is execution, the OKR view is more useful.
- **Cagan:** Cagan puts product vision first and builds product strategy on insights. Rumelt distrusts vision statements that are not tied to a diagnosis. Cagan is more right for motivating product teams, and Rumelt is more right when testing whether a plan will work.
- **Bezos/Amazon:** Working backwards starts from the customer outcome, which resembles a goal. It is consistent with Rumelt only if the internal FAQ contains a real diagnosis of the obstacles.
- **Minto:** Minto says lead with the answer; the kernel starts with diagnosis. Lead with the guiding policy when the audience already shares the diagnosis. Put the diagnosis first when they don't, as with a board new to the issue.
- **Slootman:** Slootman stresses execution intensity and treats strategy as secondary. Rumelt counts coherent action as part of strategy. Slootman is more right where the direction is obvious and speed decides the outcome. Rumelt is more right where the challenge itself is unclear.

## Limits and biases
Rumelt's cases are chosen to illustrate his points and are analyzed in hindsight, so survivorship applies. Whether something counts as "fluff" or as a "diagnosis" is partly a judgment call. The books say little about product-level strategy, how resources are allocated in large companies, or the politics of getting a strategy adopted. The Strategy Foundry is a consulting method, not a validated process. This dossier relies more on knowledge of the books than on primary text read this session, because the McKinsey pages were unavailable.
