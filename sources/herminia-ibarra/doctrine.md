# Herminia Ibarra

## Why this source matters here
Ibarra is an organizational-behavior professor at London Business School who spent many years at INSEAD and Harvard Business School. She studies how professional identity changes during transitions. Her research covers professionals moving from project execution to client-advisory roles (the 1999 "Provisional Selves" study), mid-career changers (Working Identity), about 500 managers in her INSEAD leadership-transition program, and women's advancement (Catalyst sponsorship data and a 360-degree dataset of 2,816 executives). She gives the coach two mechanisms: how people change (from the outside in, through new work and new relationships) and how people get advanced (visible leadership work, networks, sponsors). Her "do-it-yourself transition" fits an AI PM unusually well: job expectations change with the technology and the org long before any title does. Transfer caution: her samples are mostly executive-education participants, MBAs, and professional-services staff, and her prescriptions assume some slack and permission to experiment, which not every role or person has.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Outsight: act your way into new thinking. Change runs from the outside in.
- **Claim:** In transitions, introspection-first development fails because your current thinking is a product of your past experience. Doing new things (new projects, new people, new ways of working) is what changes how you think about the job and who you become. Self-knowledge is an output of leadership work, not an input. Too much introspection anchors people in the past.
- **Source:** Act Like a Leader, ch. 1 (outsight principle; insight vs. outsight table).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach does not open with "define your leadership purpose". It asks what the user will do in the next two weeks that someone at the next level would do, and schedules reflection after the action, on what actually happened and how others reacted.
- **Boundary conditions:** Action without sensemaking produces thrash. Working Identity pairs experiments with making sense of them. When the issue is a values conflict, burnout, or a real choice of path, some reflection first is warranted (see Hughes Johnson).

### D2. Most transitions are now do-it-yourself: the job changes before the title does.
- **Claim:** In her 2013 survey of 173 INSEAD alumni, many reported new stakeholders, more cross-functional responsibility, and a changed business environment. Only 47% of those reporting major changes in expectations had been promoted in the prior two years. The rest were expected to step up without a new title or guidance. Opportunities that aren't created inside the day job may never arrive.
- **Source:** Act Like a Leader, ch. 1 (survey figures and the "do-it-yourself transition").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach treats environmental shifts as transition triggers even without a promotion: a new CEO, an AI platform shift, a reorg, new stakeholders. It runs her ten-item self-assessment (F6). A user who says "nothing has changed, I'm just busier" is often inside an unrecognized transition.
- **Boundary conditions:** When an org does label transitions clearly, Watkins-style entry planning applies alongside this.

### D3. The competency trap: doing more of what you're good at crowds out leadership work.
- **Claim:** We enjoy what we do well, so we do more of it and get better at it. The more time goes to our strengths, the less goes to learning new things, and learning new things gets costlier over time. In her survey, 57% of managers agreed that routine and operational work consumed too much of their time, even though they rated cross-unit collaboration, strategic direction, and buy-in as the most important competencies. Scheduling two hours of daily "thinking time" didn't work for the manager in her lead case. Changing the work did.
- **Source:** Act Like a Leader, ch. 1-2; author site; strategy+business.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach names the user's craft comfort zone (for an AI PM: deep model work, eval tinkering, detailed PRDs, being the technical tiebreaker) and prescribes specific substitutions with outside-facing activities, not "block time for strategy".
- **Boundary conditions:** In AI, technical depth is scarce and a source of credibility. The trap is exclusivity, not depth. Keep the depth that earns a seat and trade away the depth that merely feels good.

### D4. Redefine the job as a platform for leadership: be a bridge, not a hub.
- **Claim:** Leadership work means bridging across groups and organizations, envisioning new possibilities, and engaging people in change. Secondary summaries add a fourth task, embodying the change (attribution less certain). Team leaders who reach outside the team (scouting ideas, coordinating with outsiders, reading shifts in the organization, securing top-management support) outperform those who manage only inward. The shift is from having good technical ideas to getting buy-in from a broad set of stakeholders, and from implementing directives to deciding under ambiguity. Side projects outside core expertise and some slack in the schedule create the platform.
- **Source:** Act Like a Leader, ch. 1-2; author site (hub vs. bridge); strategy+business.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach has the user list current bridging activities, identify where they act as a hub (everything routes through them), and choose one extracurricular platform, such as a cross-org AI governance group, a partner integration, or an internal community of practice, where they can visibly do bridging and envisioning work.
- **Boundary conditions:** Bridging without delivering core results destroys credibility. Charan and Watkins would make on-time delivery the ticket to the bridge work.

### D5. Build all three networks; managers under-invest in strategic networks exactly when they need them.
- **Claim:** Operational networks are internal, present-focused, and mostly prescribed by the task. Personal networks are external and discretionary; they provide referrals, development, and a safe space. Strategic networks are lateral and vertical, reach beyond one's function, and look to the future. Their value is leverage: the ability to marshal information, support, and resources from one part of a network to get results in another. Managers avoid strategic networking because it feels political or insincere and because operational demands eat the time. The remedies are to delegate to free time, reach out before there is a need, and persist through the delayed payoff. The book also argues that left alone, networks become homophilous and convenient (she calls them narcissistic and lazy), and that a good network needs breadth, connectivity across clusters, and dynamism (by reference).
- **Source:** Ibarra & Hunter 2007 (author site); Act Like a Leader, ch. 3 (by reference).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach runs the network audit (F3) and targets missing strategic ties. For an AI PM these are often finance, sales leadership, legal and policy, the CTO's staff, and peers at other AI companies. It sets an outreach cadence and tracks new ties per quarter.
- **Boundary conditions:** In cultures with strong anti-politics norms (Campbell), frame networking as learning and creating value for the other person, not as working the room.

### D6. The authenticity paradox: feeling fake is often a sign of growth, and rigid authenticity is a trap.
- **Claim:** Authenticity crises come when leaders take charge in unfamiliar territory, sell their ideas, or absorb negative feedback. Definitions of authenticity that fix a "true self" become an anchor. High self-monitors ("chameleons") adapt without feeling fake, while "true-to-selfers" can cling to comfortable behavior that no longer meets new requirements. Authentic-leadership advice also carries American norms of self-disclosure that put managers from other cultures in a bind. The recommended moves: borrow selectively from many role models, set learning goals rather than performance goals, and keep revising your story.
- **Source:** "The Authenticity Paradox" (author site); Act Like a Leader, ch. 4.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user says "that's not me" about influence, self-advocacy, executive presence, or vision-setting, the coach treats it as a hypothesis to test, not a verdict. It designs a low-stakes trial and reviews the result.
- **Boundary conditions:** Adaptation is not abandoning values. For people facing double binds (D10), the same experiment carries different costs, so the coach adjusts the risk.

### D7. Identity experiments: observe, try provisional selves, evaluate.
- **Claim:** Professionals moving into senior roles adapt in three steps. They observe role models to see possible identities, try out "provisional selves", and evaluate the results against their own standards and others' feedback. Selective borrowing from several models works better than copying one person wholesale or insisting on staying as you are.
- **Source:** "Provisional Selves" (ASQ 1999, by reference); echoed in "The Authenticity Paradox".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach helps the user pick role models for separate facets, for example one executive for board-level brevity, one for technical credibility with engineers, and one for handling conflict with peers. It extracts specific behaviors and schedules trials (F5).
- **Boundary conditions:** Role-model scarcity is itself a barrier for underrepresented people (Women Rising). The coach may need to widen the search beyond the company.

### D8. A leadership reputation is built socially: others see you do leadership work, confirm it, and you internalize it.
- **Claim:** Acting like a leader (proposing new ideas, contributing outside your expertise, connecting people and resources to a goal) leads others to see and confirm leadership. Repeated demonstrations build a reputation that brings endorsement, such as a bigger job, and the person comes to see themselves as a leader. The cycle then compounds.
- **Source:** Act Like a Leader, ch. 1.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach tracks visibility of leadership work, not just output. Which senior people have seen the user bridge, envision, or engage in the last quarter? This feeds trajectory-model and helps attribution-analysis separate a capability gap from a visibility gap.
- **Boundary conditions:** Visibility without substance backfires. The reputation has to be earned in front of people, not claimed.

### D9. Sponsorship, not mentoring, gets people promoted, and sponsorship is a spectrum you can move people along.
- **Claim:** Catalyst's survey of more than 4,000 high-potential MBA graduates found women had mentors at least as often as men, yet men received about 15% more promotions. Mentoring predicted promotion for men but not for women, partly because women's mentors were less senior and had less clout. In the article's framing, women were over-mentored but under-sponsored. The 2019 piece treats sponsorship as a spectrum of increasing commitment: mentor (private advice), strategizer (insider knowledge about advancing), connector (introductions, talking you up to peers), opportunity giver (high-visibility assignments), and advocate (staking their reputation in promotion and succession decisions). Relationships move along the spectrum as trust and evidence build.
- **Source:** "Why Men Still Get More Promotions Than Women" and "A Lack of Sponsorship..." (author site).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach inventories the user's senior relationships by spectrum position (F4). It asks what each person has personally seen the user deliver and what the next step up the spectrum would require, such as a specific introduction or a visible stretch assignment. It prompts the user to identify the most powerful person who knows their work well.
- **Boundary conditions:** Sponsors are earned through visible, high-stakes performance. Asking for sponsorship before delivering, or treating it as a transaction, backfires.

### D10. Some attribution problems are structural, not about capability: second-generation bias and the vision gap.
- **Claim:** Women Rising describes subtle, structural bias rather than overt discrimination: scarce role models, gendered career paths and assignments, less access to networks and sponsors, and double binds (too tough or not tough enough). These disrupt the identity work of becoming a leader. Over-focusing on perceptions and "presence" drains energy, and anchoring in a leadership purpose helps. Women and the Vision Thing found that in 360 data on 2,816 executives, women outscored men on most dimensions but not on envisioning. Explanations included different, more collaborative ways of building vision, reluctance to go beyond hard data without a presumption of competence, and skepticism about vision as a priority.
- **Source:** Women Rising; Women and the Vision Thing (author site).
- **Changes coaching how:** In attribution-analysis the coach separates four explanations: capability, visibility, perception or bias, and context. It names double binds explicitly when the feedback pattern shows them. For any execution-strong PM, it treats envisioning as a visible behavior to practice (articulating direction beyond the data, in public) rather than a trait someone has or lacks.
- **Boundary conditions:** Don't assume bias without evidence, and don't dismiss it when the pattern is there. Use feedback patterns (contradictory feedback, style critiques with no performance critique) as data.

### D11. Stepping up is nonlinear. Expect setbacks and an in-between period.
- **Claim:** The step-up process moves forward and falls back repeatedly and only builds momentum if the person keeps learning. Success means changes driven by a new clarity about oneself that comes from direct leadership experience. Working Identity describes a between-identities phase in which small experiments beat one big decision.
- **Source:** Act Like a Leader, ch. 1 (preview of ch. 5); Working Identity (author page).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach normalizes regressions, for example a stressful quarter that pulls the user back into the weeds, and judges the trajectory over quarters rather than weeks. It keeps the experiment log going through setbacks.
- **Boundary conditions:** Nonlinearity doesn't excuse stalling. If no leadership experiments have happened in a quarter, the coach says so.

## Frameworks in operational form

### F1. Outsight tripod: a 90-day experiment plan
| Leg | Question | Example actions (AI PM) | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | What will I do differently that the next level does? | Take on one cross-org problem; hand off one craft task each month; open a slack block for outside-facing work | Hours shifted; new problems owned |
| Network | Whom will I connect with who sees the world differently? | Monthly 1:1s with finance, sales, and policy leaders; one external AI peer group | New strategic ties; referrals received |
| Self | Which new behavior will I try, even if it feels unnatural? | Present an unfinished vision to execs; state a point of view beyond the data | Reactions observed; what was kept or dropped |
Review every two weeks: what happened, how others reacted, what I learned about the job and about myself.

### F2. Competency-trap audit
1. List the recurring activities from the last two weeks.
2. Tag each one as craft comfort, leadership platform (bridging, envisioning, engaging), network building, or pure operations.
3. Estimate the time share of each tag. Compare it with the three competencies the user says matter most to their next role.
4. Pick two comfort activities to delegate, reduce, or stop, and one platform activity to add.
5. Recheck in 30 days. Expect the old share to creep back under pressure (D11).

### F3. Network audit
1. List the 20-30 people the user most often turns to for work matters.
2. Code each contact: type (operational, personal, strategic); internal or external; same function or different; senior, peer, or junior; and whether the tie was added in the last 12 months.
3. Score breadth (spread across functions, levels, organizations), connectivity (whether the user links clusters that aren't otherwise connected), and dynamism (share of new ties).
4. Find the gaps: missing strategic ties to decision-makers two levels up, adjacent functions, and outside the company.
5. Set an outreach plan with a reason to connect: a question, an offer of help, or shared work. Give before asking.

### F4. Sponsorship-spectrum inventory
| Senior person | Current role on the spectrum | What they have personally seen me deliver | Next step up | What would earn it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
Positions, in rising commitment: mentor, strategizer, connector, opportunity giver, advocate. Review quarterly. A pipeline with only mentors is a warning sign.

### F5. Identity-experiment protocol
1. Trigger: the situation where current style falls short (for example, selling a vision to executives).
2. Role models: two or three people, each for a different facet. Note specific behaviors, not general styles.
3. Experiment: one behavior, tried first in a low-stakes setting and then escalated.
4. Feedback: set up one observer in advance, plus the user's own read of results against their values.
5. Evaluate: keep, modify, or drop the behavior. Update the story the user tells about who they are becoming.

### F6. Do-it-yourself transition check (paraphrase of her ten-item self-assessment)
Count the yeses. Industry changed a lot recently. Top leadership changed. Company grew or shrank significantly. Major change effort under way. New competitors. Technology changing how the business works. More stakeholders needed to do the job. More than two years in the same job. Sent to leadership training. Business becoming more international. Her scoring: 8-10 means the environment is changing dramatically and leadership must change with it; 4-7 means expectations to step up are growing; 3 or below means prepare for shifts.

### F7. Four-way attribution lens (derived from D8-D10)
- Capability: has the user actually done the next-level work, even in small settings? If not, design experiments.
- Visibility: have decision-makers seen it? If not, work bridging platforms and sponsor exposure.
- Perception or bias: is the feedback about style rather than results, or contradictory? If so, name the double bind and find advocates.
- Context: does the org have the role and budget, and a boss who promotes? If not, consider a lateral move or another company.

## Diagnostic questions
- Is the user in an unlabeled transition? What changed in the environment in the last 12-24 months?
- What is the user's craft comfort zone, and what share of the week does it take?
- Where is the user a hub, and where a bridge?
- What leadership experiment did the user run in the last month, and what did they learn?
- Which "that's not me" statements keep coming up? Are they values, or untested discomfort?
- Who are the user's role models, and for which specific behaviors?
- How many of the user's regular contacts are strategic? How many were added this year?
- Who is the most powerful person who knows the user's work well, and where do they sit on the sponsorship spectrum?
- Which senior people have seen the user bridge, envision, or engage in the last quarter?
- Does the user's feedback show style critiques without performance critiques, or contradictory demands?
- Can the user state a direction for their area that goes beyond what the data proves?

## Observable signals
Positive:
- The user takes on work outside their core expertise that connects groups, with visible results.
- Operational work is delegated so time can go to outside-facing activities, and the calendar shows it.
- New strategic ties are added each quarter across functions, levels, and outside the company.
- The user tries new behaviors in public (for example, presenting a direction without full data), then asks for feedback and adjusts.
- The user talks about identity as evolving ("I'm learning to...") rather than fixed ("I'm not a politics person").
- At least one senior person has moved up the sponsorship spectrum this year, for example from mentor to opportunity giver.
- The user articulates a vision for the area and gets others to engage with it.
- The user recovers from a regression quarter without abandoning the experiments.
Negative:
- Works harder at the same things when expectations shift. Firefights and still reviews every detail.
- Blocks strategy time that gets eaten by operations and never changes the work mix.
- The network is almost entirely internal, same-function, and years old.
- Uses "that's not authentic to me" to decline influence, networking, or visibility work.
- Has many mentors and no one who has created an opportunity for them or advocated for them.
- Is described as solid on execution and weak on vision in feedback.
- Waits for a promotion before acting at the next level.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Taking "act first" as license for impulsive moves or for skipping diagnosis. Outsight means deliberate experiments with reflection afterward.
- Treating networking as a transaction or collecting contacts. Ibarra frames it as learning and value creation, and sponsors notice the difference.
- "Fake it till you make it" without learning goals. The point is to learn from trying on selves, not to perform a persona.
- Pursuing executive presence as appearance and polish. Women Rising warns that over-focus on perceptions drains the energy needed for purposeful leadership.
- Chasing sponsors before delivering visible, high-stakes results.
- A coach that defaults to reflective exercises (values lists, purpose statements) when the user needs new actions and new people.
- Explaining every setback as bias, or none of them. Use F7 and the evidence.
- Encouraging extracurricular platform work while the core job slips.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Ibarra vs. Hughes Johnson.** Hughes Johnson puts self-knowledge first: know your working style, energy, and values as the basis of your operating system. Ibarra says too much introspection anchors you in the past and that self-knowledge comes from action. Hughes Johnson is more right when the user lacks basic self-understanding, is burning out, or is choosing between paths. Ibarra is more right when the next level needs behaviors outside the comfort zone and reflection would just confirm "that's not me".
- **Ibarra vs. Watkins.** Watkins enters a role diagnosis-first with a learning plan. Ibarra acts first. Watkins fits formal moves with a clear boss and a honeymoon. Ibarra fits unlabeled transitions where identity, not information, is the constraint.
- **Ibarra vs. Charan.** Charan assumes a defined pipeline and a developing boss. Ibarra's data show that most people get little help from bosses (her alumni ranked the boss's guidance below external training, family and friends, and peers) and have to build their own transition. Use Charan to define the target and Ibarra to get there when the organization won't.
- **Ibarra vs. Campbell.** Campbell's model disdains politics inside the team. Ibarra says reconciling yourself to the political nature of organizational life is one of the biggest hurdles of stepping up, and that seeing influence as "playing politics" holds people back. Both hold under their conditions: team-first norms inside a trusted team, and strategic networking across the organization.
- **Ibarra vs. Pfeffer.** Both treat networks and power as central. Pfeffer is more instrumental: seek power directly, promote yourself, and don't count on merit. Ibarra frames network-building around learning, purpose, and value creation. Pfeffer is the corrective where the user's purity blocks necessary self-advocacy. Ibarra is the corrective where instrumental networking would read as manipulative.
- **Ibarra vs. Hewlett.** Both separate sponsorship from mentoring. Hewlett presents sponsorship as a reciprocal deal (the protégé delivers performance and loyalty) and treats executive presence as something to cultivate. Ibarra sees sponsorship as a spectrum that grows with trust, and warns that over-focusing on presence and perception diverts energy from purpose. Hewlett's is the more tactical playbook; Ibarra's guards against performative self-management.
- **Ibarra vs. Doshi.** Doshi defends staying true to one's preferred level and self. Ibarra's authenticity paradox says a fixed self-concept blocks growth. The coach tests whether the user's preference is stable and informed by experiments, or untested discomfort.
- **Ibarra vs. Goldsmith.** They are aligned on changing behavior rather than insight. Goldsmith emphasizes stopping habits through stakeholder feedback. Ibarra emphasizes adding new behaviors through experiments. Use both.
- **Ibarra vs. Linda Hill.** They are aligned. Hill's first-time managers also discover the job is an identity change learned through experience, not a set of skills.

## Limits and biases
- Samples: INSEAD executive-education participants (self-selected, international, sponsored by employers), professional-services staff (Provisional Selves), and MBA graduates (Catalyst). They are mostly corporate managers, not product organizations in tech.
- Evidence: much of the doctrine rests on qualitative interviews and self-report surveys. Survey percentages describe perceptions, not measured outcomes.
- Privilege of slack: outsight assumes time and permission for side projects and experiments. People in overloaded or high-scrutiny positions, including the underrepresented people she studies, may find experiments costlier.
- Gender research compares women and men only. Other identity dimensions are less developed in the work cited here.
- Delivery risk: the doctrine underweights sheer execution and results, which senior leaders still screen for first (see Charan, Campbell).
- The 2007 networks article predates remote and hybrid work. How strategic networks form in distributed companies is not addressed in the sources consulted. The 2023 preface may address this but was not reviewed.
- Some framings (narcissistic and lazy networks; breadth, connectivity, dynamism; the fourth task, embodying) come from the book and secondary summaries, not the pages read this session.
