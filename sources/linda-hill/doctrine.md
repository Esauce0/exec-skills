# Linda A. Hill

## Why this source matters here
Hill is a Harvard Business School professor. Her original research followed 19 new managers through their first year (Becoming a Manager, 1992; 2nd ed. 2003). She has studied the star-performer-to-manager transition for decades since. Her subjects were first-line managers, mostly in sales-side roles, not product executives. The pattern she describes still recurs at each step up: a strong individual performer assumes the new role brings authority and control, and instead finds interdependence and a new identity to grow into. For an AI PM moving to Director, whether they are taking on reports for the first time or starting to manage managers, she explains why doing more of what made them successful makes things worse.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. The transition is a change of identity, learned by doing
- **Claim:** Becoming a manager is not an added skill set. It is a psychological transformation in which the person unlearns habits that made them a star and takes on new ways of measuring success and getting satisfaction from work. It is learned mainly on the job, through trial and error, in small steps, often without the person noticing they are learning. Hill's new managers had four tasks: learning what a manager actually does, developing judgment about people, gaining self-knowledge, and coping with stress and emotion. The shift runs from specialist and doer to generalist and agenda-setter, and from individual actor to network builder.
- **Source:** "Becoming the Boss" (the "Why Learning to Manage Is So Hard" section); Becoming a Manager.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach expects discomfort and a dip in satisfaction after a step up, and treats them as normal rather than as evidence the user made the wrong move. It asks, "Where are you still measuring yourself by what you personally produced?" It plans deliberate stretch experiences instead of courses, because this is learned by doing.
- **Boundary conditions:** The research is on first-line managers. For a Director managing managers, the same arc repeats at a higher level of abstraction (see Charan).

### D2. Authority turns out to be interdependence, so managing the context is part of the job
- **Claim:** New managers expect more authority and freedom. They find themselves constrained by relationships with bosses, peers, outside parties and reports, all making conflicting demands. The people most likely to make their lives miserable are outside their formal authority. Until they accept negotiating these dependencies as part of the job, they cannot lead well. Their team will lack resources unless the manager builds relationships with the people it depends on.
- **Source:** "Becoming the Boss" (myth 1); Being the Boss (manage your network).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user complains about "politics" or dependencies blocking their team, the coach reframes that as the core of the job at the next level. It then maps the people the team depends on and plans regular contact with each. Hill's Winona Finch example: biweekly executive notes and a cross-publication board kept superiors and peers engaged.
- **Boundary conditions:** Building a network is not a license to spend all your time on relationships. Larson warns that doing so with no tangible output looks like contributing nothing.

### D3. Power comes from credibility, not position, and showing off expertise can undermine it
- **Claim:** New managers find their power comes from "everything but" formal authority. Authority emerges only as they build credibility with reports, peers and superiors, through character (the intention to do the right thing), competence (knowing how to do the right thing, which for a manager is not mainly technical) and influence (the ability to get the right thing done). The more talented the report, the less likely they are to simply follow orders. Eagerness to prove technical depth can make a manager look like a micromanager. Reports value a boss who can deliver for them; working for a powerless boss is among their worst experiences.
- **Source:** "Becoming the Boss" (myth 2; the Isenberg trading-desk example); Being the Boss (trust rests on competence and character).
- **Changes coaching how:** For an AI PM, the trap is proving model and technical depth in every review. The coach asks whether the user is listening and asking questions, or directing. It also treats influence as something the team is actively judging: "What have you delivered for your team from outside it this quarter?"
- **Boundary conditions:** Technical credibility still matters as a baseline, especially in AI orgs. The failure is relying on it as the main source of authority.

### D4. Control gets compliance; managers need commitment
- **Claim:** Insecure new managers want compliance and fall back on formal authority to get it. Even when it works, it is a false victory. Compliance does not equal commitment: people who aren't committed won't take initiative, so the manager can't delegate, and the team won't take the calculated risks that improvement requires. The more power a manager shares, the more influence they tend to have. Hill's example is a manager who led through a culture of relentless questioning rather than dictating how things should be done.
- **Source:** "Becoming the Boss" (myth 3; the Finch example).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user says "I told them and they didn't do it," the coach looks at how the direction was given, not at the report's attitude. It suggests shifting to shared goals, asking questions, and delegating the "how."
- **Boundary conditions:** Commitment-building is slow. In emergencies or on compliance-critical matters (AI safety, legal), direct instruction is appropriate. Rabois's conviction × consequence grid marks the boundary.

### D5. Leading the team is not the sum of your one-on-one relationships
- **Claim:** New managers treat people management as building the best possible relationship with each report. They handle team-wide issues one-on-one, rely on a few trusted people, and decide with limited information. Every decision about one person affects the team. In Hill's example, a parking-spot favor to one salesman nearly caused a top performer to quit. Shaping team norms and using group forums to solve problems unlocks performance that individual management cannot.
- **Source:** "Becoming the Boss" (myth 4; the Collins example).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks which recent decisions the user made one-on-one that affected the whole team, and whether any exception for one person set a precedent. It recommends taking team-wide problems to group forums and setting explicit team norms.
- **Boundary conditions:** One-on-ones are still necessary for coaching and development. The error is treating them as the whole of leadership.

### D6. Beyond keeping things running: drive change, including outside your formal authority
- **Claim:** Keeping operations running is hard and can use up all of a manager's time. Managers are also responsible for recommending and starting changes that improve their group, which often means challenging processes and structures above their authority. Most new managers see themselves as recipients of change rather than agents of it. They blame flawed systems and wait for others to fix them. In Hill's sidebar, a marketing manager who failed to get budget and then made do inside his team was fired for not being proactive. The CEO's position was that creating the conditions for his success was his own responsibility.
- **Source:** "Becoming the Boss" (myth 5; the "Create the Conditions for Your Success" sidebar).
- **Changes coaching how:** This is one of the most behavior-changing points for a PM moving up. When the user says "the planning process didn't fund us" or "leadership hasn't decided," the coach asks what the user did to change the context: escalations, allies, a proposal to the budget owner or CEO. Accepting a flawed system is treated as a readiness gap, not as bad luck.
- **Boundary conditions:** Pushing beyond your authority needs credibility (D3) and a sponsor. Without them it reads as overreach (see Larson on staying aligned).

### D7. New managers rarely ask for help, and good bosses stop improving
- **Claim:** New managers seldom seek help, partly from the belief that the boss should have all the answers, partly from fear that the boss who evaluates them will hold it against them or step in. Hill found that about half eventually turned to their bosses, often in a crisis, and many found them more tolerant than expected. Hill and Lineback later argue that many bosses reach proficiency and then stop developing. Their remedy is continuing work on three imperatives: manage yourself, manage your network, manage your team.
- **Source:** "Becoming the Boss" (the "New Managers Aren't Alone" section); Being the Boss; "Are You a Good Boss—or a Great One?"
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach helps the user find development relationships outside the chain that evaluates them (peers in other functions or companies). With the boss, the coach has the user bring proposed solutions rather than open questions. After a step up, the coach runs a periodic three-imperative review so the user doesn't plateau.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some bosses do punish help-seeking. Choose the channel based on what the boss has actually done before.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Myth-to-reality check** (run after any step up, and quarterly):
| Myth | Reality | Evidence question for the user |
|---|---|---|
| Authority | Interdependence | Who outside my authority blocked or enabled us this month? |
| Formal authority | "Everything but": character, competence, influence | What did I deliver for my team from outside it? |
| Control | Commitment | Where did I direct the "how" instead of agreeing on the goal? |
| Managing one-on-one | Leading the team | Which one-on-one decision had team-wide effects? |
| Keeping it running | Making changes | What change did I start beyond my formal authority? |

**Dependency map:** list the people and teams the user's team depends on for resources, decisions and inputs. Rate each relationship as strong, neutral or weak, and set a regular touchpoint for the weak but critical ones.

**Three-imperative review:** Self: is my impact matching my intentions? Network: are my peer and boss relationships creating the conditions my team needs? Team: is there a shared purpose, explicit norms, and forums for group problem-solving?

## Diagnostic questions
- Is the user still getting their sense of worth mainly from their personal output?
- When blocked, does the user wait for the system to be fixed or work to change it?
- Is the user leaning on technical expertise or formal title when they need influence?
- What decisions did the user handle one-on-one that should have gone to the group?
- Who outside the evaluation chain does the user use for honest development help?

## Observable signals
- Positive: talks about the team's results rather than their own output; spends regular time on dependencies; asks more than they direct; takes team-wide issues to group forums; proposes changes to processes above their level; asks for help early, with a proposed approach.
- Negative: "I'll just do it myself"; complains about politics and dependencies; wins arguments with technical detail; makes one-off exceptions for favored reports; blames planning or budget systems; hides struggles from the boss until a crisis.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using "commitment over control" to avoid necessary direction or hard performance conversations.
- Reading "network builder" as permission for relationship-building with no output.
- Applying first-line-manager findings to executive roles without adjusting for the larger scope.
- Treating the identity shift as something to reason your way through instead of something learned through action.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Rabois:** Rabois's editor overrides on high-conviction, high-stakes calls. Hill warns that control leads to compliance. Rabois's advice to explain the why and delegate low-stakes decisions reconciles them.
- **Pfeffer:** Both treat power and networks as core to the job. Hill grounds influence in credibility and character. Pfeffer is more willing to endorse self-promotion and rule-breaking.
- **Charan:** Charan specifies the skills, time use and values required at each leadership passage. Hill describes what the passage feels like and how it is learned. Use Charan for level expectations and Hill for the transition experience.
- **Ibarra:** They agree that a new identity comes from action. Ibarra adds that the "authenticity" trap and outside networks speed up the shift.
- **Lencioni:** They agree on team over one-on-one. Lencioni adds peer-team (first team) loyalty, which Hill's first-line research does not address.

## Limits and biases
The foundational sample is small (19 managers) and from the early 1990s, mostly sales-side roles. Evidence is qualitative and longitudinal, from interviews and case studies. Remote and matrixed product organizations are only partly covered (Being the Boss adds some notes on virtual trust). Coverage of executive-level transitions is thinner than coverage of first-line ones.
