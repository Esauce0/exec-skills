# Sylvia Ann Hewlett

## Why this source matters here
Hewlett is an economist who founded the Center for Work-Life Policy. It became the Center for Talent Innovation (CTI) and is now Coqual. From there she ran large survey programs (thousands of college-educated professionals at large corporations, plus focus groups and executive interviews) funded by corporate task-force members in finance, professional services, pharma and tech. Her question was why women and people of color stall below the top even when they perform. Her answers were a missing sponsor and a missing, or differently judged, "executive presence." The mechanisms apply to anyone whose next promotion is decided in a room they are not in, which is exactly the Director and VP transition for a PM. Transfer caution: the evidence is self-reported, cross-sectional, drawn from large hierarchical U.S.-centric companies, and not peer-reviewed. The presence findings record what incumbent senior leaders say they reward, which is not necessarily what should be rewarded.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Mentors advise; sponsors spend capital. Most people overcount their sponsors
- **Claim:** The 2010 report defines a sponsor by behavior. A sponsor uses chips on your behalf and advocates for your next promotion. They also do at least two of the following: expand your sense of what you can do, connect you to senior leaders, connect you outside the company, promote your visibility, connect you to career opportunities, give career advice, and advise you on your appearance. Sponsorship is public, because the sponsor puts their reputation next to your performance. It is oriented to your future and driven by the sponsor. Mentoring can be done privately without reputational risk. In the report's own data, 42% of women and 31% of men at director level and above called themselves sponsors, but only 17% of men and 11% of women reported actually using up chips for a protégé.
- **Source:** The Sponsor Effect (2010), ch. 2 (Figure 2-1) and ch. 10 (Figure 10-1).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach scores every senior relationship the user describes against the rubric. The key questions are whether this person has ever spent capital for the user, and whether they would name the user in a talent review without being prompted. Most PMs who say "my VP is my sponsor" are describing a mentor or a friendly manager. Closing that gap becomes an explicit trajectory-model item.
- **Boundary conditions:** The rubric is a survey definition, not a law. In small companies, the founder's inner circle may be the only possible sponsors. Mentors remain valuable for building skill and for honest feedback.

### D2. Sponsorship changes what people ask for, not only what they get
- **Claim:** In the 2010 data, 57% of unsponsored employees were satisfied with their rate of advancement, against 70% of sponsored men and 68% of sponsored women. The report calls this a 23% (men) and 19% (women) sponsorship effect. Willingness to ask for a stretch assignment rose from 43% to 56% for men and from 36% to 44% for women with a sponsor. Most people without sponsors do not ask for raises; with a sponsor, nearly half of men and 38% of women did. The report puts the benefit at 22–30% depending on the request.
- **Source:** The Sponsor Effect (2010), ch. 3 (Figures 3-2, 3-3).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user is not asking for stretch scope, the coach checks for a missing sponsor before diagnosing "confidence." A sponsor is also the lever that makes an ask land: the coach plans a big ask and the sponsor conversation together.
- **Boundary conditions:** These are cross-sectional associations. High performers may both attract sponsors and ask more. Treat the numbers as directional, not as effect sizes.

### D3. Sponsorship is a two-way alliance: deliver performance, trustworthiness and distinct value
- **Claim:** Protégés must deliver outstanding performance (which Hewlett calls table stakes), loyalty and trustworthiness toward the sponsor and the organization, and a distinctive value-add: a skill, perspective or network the sponsor lacks, which also enhances the sponsor's own standing. In summaries of the 2013 book, loyal performers who never distinguish themselves risk staying permanent lieutenants. Practitioners quoted in the 2010 report add: make the sponsor look good, since they put their reputation on the line; take work off their plate; and visibly act on their feedback, or future requests fall flat.
- **Source:** HBR 2011 (abstract); The Sponsor Effect (2019) as described in the 2023 HBR podcast; The Sponsor Effect (2010) ch. 10; Forget a Mentor (2013, via summaries).
- **Changes coaching how:** For each target sponsor, the coach asks for evidence on all three. What has the sponsor personally seen the user deliver? Has the user been trustworthy: no surprises, discretion, backing decisions after losing the argument, candor in private? What does the user bring that the sponsor lacks? For an AI PM, distinct value is often translating model capability, cost and risk for executives who came up through go-to-market or finance, or reach into research teams the sponsor cannot reach.
- **Boundary conditions:** Loyalty means commitment to the sponsor's success and the mission. It does not mean complicity, silence about problems, or taking sides in feuds. If a sponsor's request conflicts with integrity, the relationship gives way. In unhealthy organizations, "loyalty" is used to extract unpaid work, and the coach watches for that.

### D4. Look for clout and line of sight, usually above and beyond your boss
- **Claim:** In large organizations the ideal sponsor is about two levels above you, with line of sight to your work. In small firms, it is the founder or president or someone in their inner circle. Good candidates already know your strengths, stand to benefit from your help, and have the clout to move you. The 2010 report quotes a practitioner's advice to build relationships with decision-makers outside your line of business and at the level you want to reach.
- **Source:** Forget a Mentor (2013, via summaries); The Sponsor Effect (2010) ch. 10; Business Standard interview headline (2013).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach builds a sponsor map with columns for level, clout (sits in calibration and talent reviews, controls roles and budget), line of sight, what they gain from the user's work, and current relationship state. It flags dependence on one sponsor, especially when that sponsor is the direct manager.
- **Boundary conditions:** Cultivating people above your boss must never look like going around them. The boss should be an ally in the user's visibility, and in most companies the boss's advocacy is still necessary even when it is not sufficient. In flat startups, "two levels up" may be the CEO.

### D5. Hold several sponsors, including outside your chain
- **Claim:** One patron cannot be counted on for a whole career: sponsors leave, lose favor, or get reorganized away. The report's practitioners describe rebuilding from scratch after a sponsor left. Connections outside the company are part of the sponsor definition itself. Summaries of the 2013 book describe a portfolio with at least one internal sponsor in the line, one in another function, and one external. The exact rule name and counts are uncertain, so treat this as the book's general direction.
- **Source:** The Sponsor Effect (2010) chs. 2, 10; Forget a Mentor (2013, via summaries).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach targets two or three active sponsors across different power centers. For example: the user's own VP, a peer VP in engineering or research, and an external senior figure such as a former executive or a board-adjacent advisor. It re-checks the portfolio after every reorg or executive departure.
- **Boundary conditions:** Sponsorship requires depth, so many shallow "sponsors" amount to a network, not advocacy. A portfolio drawn entirely from one faction fails when that faction loses.

### D6. You cannot simply ask someone to sponsor you; get into their line of sight and let them see you work
- **Claim:** Practitioners in the 2010 report say you cannot walk up to someone and ask them to be your sponsor. You put yourself in the "strike zone": work where the potential sponsor can watch you perform, treat every exposure as an interview, and schedule sponsor relationships like key client relationships. Hewlett says the junior person must drive the relationship because senior people lack time. Sponsors vet protégés with smaller, lower-risk assignments before large ones. A pattern of broken trust ends the relationship; she describes a "two strikes" standard.
- **Source:** The Sponsor Effect (2010) ch. 10; 2023 HBR podcast.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach designs specific exposure. Volunteer for the cross-functional review the target chairs, deliver a crisp readout, follow up with results, ask for one piece of feedback, and report back on acting on it. It treats the first small assignment from a potential sponsor as a test, and over-delivers on reliability and discretion.
- **Boundary conditions:** Contrived exposure without substance hurts. What sponsors judge is performance under their own observation.

### D7. Sponsors gain too; frame the alliance around the sponsor's goals
- **Claim:** The 2019 book and the 2023 interview report that senior executives with protégés were 53% more likely to have received a recent promotion, and managers with protégés were 167% more likely to have received stretch assignments. Sponsors also report more career satisfaction. Protégés extend a leader's reach and fill capability gaps. In the 2010 data, sponsors named three benefits: a protégé who goes the extra mile, looking good when the protégé succeeds, and paying it forward (cited by 71% of women and 61% of men).
- **Source:** 2023 HBR podcast; The Sponsor Effect (2010) ch. 10 (Figure 10-2).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user seeks a sponsor, the coach frames every contact around the sponsor's objectives and how the user extends them. When the user becomes a Director, the coach pushes them to actively sponsor two or three people, as managerial leverage and as a way to build their own reach.
- **Boundary conditions:** The numbers are correlational and self-selected. Sponsors also carry the risk that a protégé fails, which is why vetting exists.

### D8. Sponsorship skews toward people like the sponsor, so account for it in diagnosis
- **Claim:** Sponsors gravitate toward protégés who resemble them, which leaves women and people of color under-sponsored. Hewlett's remedy for sponsors is a "one plus two" portfolio: at most one protégé like you, and at least two who differ by gender, ethnicity, generation or geography. The 2010 report finds that the under-sponsored often hold a strong belief in meritocracy (77% of female respondents said hard work, long hours and credentials drive promotion) and are wary of "relationship capital." The report argues that building and using those relationships is itself a demonstration of leadership.
- **Source:** 2023 HBR podcast; The Sponsor Effect (2010) chs. 2, 4.
- **Changes coaching how:** If the user belongs to a group that tends to be under-sponsored in their company, attribution-analysis records the sponsor gap as partly a context problem, not only a capability gap, and the coach compensates with deliberate exposure. When the user sponsors others, the coach checks their slate for similarity bias.
- **Boundary conditions:** The data is U.S., large-company and pre-2020. The coach should not assume a user's identity determines their experience; ask.

### D9. Senior leaders weight gravitas far above communication and appearance
- **Claim:** In CTI's 2012 research (268 senior executives within a sample of nearly 4,000 professionals), senior leaders said executive presence accounts for 26% of what it takes to get the next promotion. 67% named gravitas as the core, 28% communication, and 5% appearance. Gravitas behaviors, with the share saying each contributes to a woman's or man's presence: confidence and grace under fire (79%/76%); acting decisively, or "showing teeth" (70%); integrity and speaking truth to power (64%/63%); emotional intelligence (61%/58%); reputation (56%/57%); vision (50%/54%). Communication comprises speaking skill, commanding a room, and reading an audience. Appearance acts mainly as a filter. Presence alone does not get anyone promoted, but its absence slows them.
- **Source:** CTI Executive Presence key findings (2012 research, 2013 PDF); Executive Presence (2014).
- **Changes coaching how:** For meeting-presence, the coach works on gravitas moments before slide polish or wardrobe. How did the user respond the last time an executive attacked their numbers? Did they make a call or present options without a recommendation? Have they disagreed upward, with evidence, in a room that mattered? The coach rehearses composure under challenge and making a clear recommendation.
- **Boundary conditions:** The weights are votes by incumbents, and the categories are broad. The report itself says presence varies by company culture (Google differs from Goldman Sachs) and by level, and that latitude for personal style grows with seniority.

### D10. Blunders are asymmetric: one visible lapse can outweigh months of presence
- **Claim:** Respondents named blunders that detract from presence. Communication: racially biased comments (70% for women, 72% for men) and sounding uneducated (59%/58%). Appearance: unkempt attire (83%/76%) and, for women, overly tight or provocative clothing (73%). Online: 78% search for new colleagues online first. The book also catalogs gravitas blunders, including sexual impropriety and integrity lapses (per secondary summaries; exact ranking not verified). As seniority rises, the stage gets bigger, which makes blunders more costly.
- **Source:** CTI Executive Presence key findings; Executive Presence (2014, via summaries).
- **Changes coaching how:** Before high-visibility moments (a board demo of an AI feature, an all-hands, an incident review), the coach runs a pre-mortem. What could read as flip-flopping, losing composure, overclaiming, or not knowing your own numbers? It also checks the user's public digital footprint.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some listed "blunders" encode biased norms. The coach separates integrity blunders (non-negotiable) from style norms (contextual and open to deliberate choice).

### D11. Presence standards are stricter, and feedback scarcer, for women and people of color
- **Claim:** 56% of people of color felt held to a stricter presence code, against 31% of white respondents, and 36% against 29% said they deliberately recast how they tell their own stories. Women were less likely to get presence feedback from a male superior (32% against 47% of men). Because the feedback that is given is often contradictory, 81% said they were unclear how to act on it. Hewlett frames this as a built-in tension between conforming to corporate culture and staying true to yourself. Her practical line is to hold values fixed and adapt surface presentation deliberately.
- **Source:** CTI Executive Presence key findings; Executive Presence (2014, via summaries).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach triages any presence feedback the user receives. Is it about a capability signal (composure, decisiveness, clarity) or about conforming to the incumbent style (accent, dress, energy, communication style)? The user then decides consciously what to adapt and what to keep. The coach helps the user ask sponsors for specific presence feedback, since the sponsor definition includes it.
- **Boundary conditions:** The coach must not push identity-erasing conformity. See the critiques in Limits and the Ibarra tension.

### D12. The model is moving toward authenticity, inclusiveness and virtual command
- **Claim:** In new research in 2022, Hewlett reports that leaders now look for confidence combined with authenticity and inclusiveness, plus mastery of virtual leadership (commanding a video meeting, leading remote teams, using social media). Authenticity and inclusiveness now sit inside gravitas.
- **Source:** Executive Presence 2.0 (2023) publisher description; HBR, "The New Rules of Executive Presence" (2024) abstract.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach counts video presence (concise openings, camera, managing chat) and inclusive behaviors (giving credit, drawing out quieter experts, crediting the team in executive forums) as gravitas evidence, not extras.
- **Boundary conditions:** Only descriptions and abstracts were read this session. Detailed 2.0 weights are by reference and should not be quoted as numbers.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Sponsor-or-mentor test (from the 2010 definition)
For each senior relationship, mark yes, no or unknown, with evidence:
- Has spent political capital for the user (required).
- Has advocated, or would advocate, for the user's next promotion (required).
- At least two of: expands what the user thinks they can do; connects the user to senior leaders; connects the user outside the company; promotes the user's visibility; connects the user to opportunities; gives career advice; gives presence or appearance feedback.
Classify as sponsor, mentor, or ally. Anyone with unknown on the two required items is not yet a sponsor.

### Sponsor portfolio map
| Candidate | Level vs. user | Clout (talent review seat, budget, roles) | Line of sight to user's work | What they gain from user | Distinct value user brings | Evidence of trust | Last substantive contact | Next exposure |
Targets: two or three real sponsors across at least two power centers, one ideally external. Flag a single point of failure.

### Protégé value audit (performance, trust, distinct value)
- Performance: list outcomes the sponsor personally observed in the last two quarters. Outcomes they only heard about secondhand count for half.
- Trust: no surprises, discretion kept, commitment after decisions, candor in private, feedback acted on and reported back.
- Distinct value: one sentence on what the user offers that the sponsor lacks, and one instance where it helped the sponsor.
A weak leg is the next development priority.

### Sponsor cultivation cadence
1. Identify two potential sponsors from the map.
2. Find or create a work reason to be in their line of sight (a review they chair, a problem they own).
3. Deliver a crisp result and send a short follow-up tied to their goals.
4. Ask for one specific piece of feedback, act on it, and report back within weeks.
5. Put recurring contact on the calendar, as with a key client.
6. Once trust exists, make a specific ask (a stretch assignment, a nomination) framed in their interest.
7. Reciprocate: take work off their plate, and bring them information and your network.

### Executive presence audit
Score recent high-stakes moments (last two to three) on each item with an observable example:
- Gravitas: composure under challenge; made a decision or a clear recommendation; spoke truth upward with evidence; read the emotional temperature; reputation cited by others; articulated where things are going.
- Communication: clear, concise delivery; held the room; adapted to the audience in real time.
- Appearance and filter: nothing distracted from the content, including the video setup.
- Blunder check: any flip-flop, loss of composure, overclaim, or careless remark.
Use raters: the user's own account plus at least one senior observer's.

### Feedback triage for presence comments
For each piece of feedback, ask: which behavior, in which moment? Is it a capability signal or a conformity signal? What does it cost the user to adapt it? Adapt, experiment with, or decline (and compensate elsewhere).

## Diagnostic questions
- Who, by name, would argue for the user's promotion in a calibration meeting the user is not in? What have they seen firsthand?
- Has any senior person spent capital for the user in the last year? What exactly did they do?
- How many real sponsors does the user have, in how many power centers, and what happens if one leaves?
- What does the user offer each sponsor that the sponsor lacks?
- Has the user ever received feedback from a sponsor and visibly acted on it?
- Is the user relying on the direct manager as the only advocate?
- In the last contentious executive meeting, did the user keep composure and state a recommendation?
- When did the user last disagree upward, with evidence, in a room that mattered?
- What presence feedback has the user received? Is it about capability or conformity? Has any of it been contradictory?
- Is the user's missing sponsorship partly a context problem (similarity bias, few senior people like them)?
- If the user already manages, whom are they sponsoring, and does the slate look like them?

## Observable signals
Positive:
- A senior leader nominates the user for an opportunity the user did not ask for.
- The user is mentioned by name in forums they do not attend, and hears about it afterward.
- More than one senior person from different functions can describe the user's distinct value.
- The user makes and wins asks for stretch scope, with a sponsor pre-aligned.
- Under challenge in executive meetings, the user stays even-tempered, answers with data, and still makes a recommendation.
- The user gets specific presence feedback and changes one behavior visibly within weeks.
- As a manager, the user publicly advocates for their own people and has a diverse sponsorship slate.

Negative:
- The user calls a friendly senior mentor their sponsor, but no one has ever spent capital for them.
- There is a single advocate, the direct manager.
- The user's contact with senior leaders is limited to status meetings.
- The user answers executive challenges with defensiveness, over-explanation, or a retreat to "it depends" without a recommendation.
- The user asks for sponsorship directly and cold, without a performance history the sponsor has observed.
- The user treats loyalty as agreeing in public and undermining in private.
- The user dismisses networking as "not fair" and waits to be discovered.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Mistaking mentorship for sponsorship and feeling covered.
- Treating sponsorship as a one-way ask. Hewlett's model is an exchange, and protégés who only take lose sponsors.
- Loyalty turned into complicity: covering for a sponsor's mistakes, joining their feuds, suppressing bad news.
- Putting all bets on one sponsor, then stalling after a reorg.
- Going around the direct manager in ways that read as disloyal.
- Reading the 67/28/5 weights as "appearance doesn't matter." The report says appearance is a filter that can derail.
- Treating executive presence as costume: polish, voice and wardrobe work while avoiding the gravitas behaviors (decisions, composure, speaking up).
- A coach pushing conformity to the incumbent style as if it were a capability gap.
- Using the sponsorship numbers as causal effect sizes in a promotion case.
- As a sponsor, picking only mini-mes, or offering private encouragement without public advocacy.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Ibarra (authenticity and growth):** Ibarra argues that clinging to a fixed "authentic self" blocks growth, and that leaders develop by trying provisional selves and borrowing from many role models. Hewlett's audience-rated presence model tells you what incumbents reward, which risks teaching people to copy one dominant style. They agree that some stretching beyond comfort is needed. Ibarra is more right for long-term identity development and when norms are shifting. Hewlett is more right for a specific, near-term, high-stakes evaluation where the raters' criteria are known. On sponsorship they agree: Ibarra's 2010 HBR work with Carter and Silva also found women over-mentored and under-sponsored (by reference).
- **Pfeffer (power realism):** They agree that performance is not enough and that relationships with power decide advancement. Hewlett's version is reciprocal and trust-based. Pfeffer's includes tactics (anger displays, rule-breaking) that clash with Hewlett's top gravitas traits: grace under fire, emotional intelligence, and integrity. Rule-breaking also risks embarrassing a sponsor. For a rising executive, Hewlett's composure-first posture is the safer default. Pfeffer's tactics are for the invisible or blocked.
- **Campbell (trust):** Campbell backed people for integrity, loyalty and team-first behavior, and actively spent his own standing for them, a sponsor in Hewlett's terms. They agree on trust as the currency. The tension: Campbell's team-first ethic can leave the user under-credited unless someone with clout sees the work, which is Hewlett's point.
- **Horowitz (minimize politics):** Horowitz wants promotion processes that don't reward lobbying. Sponsorship can look like favoritism if the sponsor advocates without evidence. They reconcile when the user gives sponsors an evidence pack to argue inside a rigorous process. In a well-designed process, a sponsor's evidence-backed advocacy is legitimate. Unsupported patronage is what Horowitz rightly fears.
- **Gabarro and Kotter (managing your boss):** Managing the boss is a separate relationship from cultivating sponsors. The boss may or may not be a sponsor, but a hostile boss can block any sponsor. Both relationships have to be worked.
- **Scott (Radical Candor):** Hewlett's data shows women and people of color get less and more contradictory presence feedback. Scott's guidance on soliciting criticism is the practical fix, and it also tests whether a would-be sponsor cares enough to be candid.
- **Lencioni (vulnerability):** Lencioni's vulnerability-based trust can seem to conflict with gravitas. Summaries of Hewlett's book report that owning mistakes calmly can add gravitas. The line: admit errors with composure and a plan (gravitas), not anxiety or self-deprecation (a blunder).
- **Watkins:** Transitions put sponsorship at risk, because a new boss or a sponsor's departure resets the map. Watkins's early coalition-building is the moment to recruit new sponsors.
- **Minto:** Minto supplies structure for the communication pillar. Answer-first delivery is itself a signal of decisiveness.
- **Doshi:** Doshi's "confidence" and "context" dimensions of perception overlap with presence. His warning that charisma can mask incompetence argues for anchoring presence in content.

## Limits and biases
- Evidence quality: large self-report surveys and focus groups by a research center funded by corporate task-force members, published as reports and trade books rather than peer-reviewed papers. The numbers are cross-sectional associations. They record what respondents say, not what raters do.
- Incumbent lens: the presence weights reflect what current senior executives say they value. Critics argue that "executive presence" is attributed more readily to people who look like existing leaders, so optimizing for it can reproduce bias. Hewlett's own data (stricter codes, contradictory feedback) supports this concern even though her prescriptions lean toward adaptation.
- Critics also note that the 2014 book urges conformity to norms while praising authenticity, and that it acknowledges sexism without challenging the system that produces it.
- Context: large U.S. and global corporations, heavy on finance and professional services. Startups, flat product organizations, and non-Western cultures may define sponsorship and presence differently.
- Era: the core sponsorship data is from 2010 and presence from 2012. The 2023 update shifts toward authenticity and inclusion, and the older weights should not be quoted as current without that caveat.
- Gender focus: most of the analysis is framed around women's and multicultural professionals' advancement. The mechanisms generalize, but the magnitudes may not.
- Ethics of patronage: sponsorship can entrench in-groups and reward loyalty over merit if not paired with transparent evaluation.
