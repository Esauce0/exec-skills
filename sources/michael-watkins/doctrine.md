# Michael D. Watkins

## Why this source matters here
Watkins is a professor at IMD and cofounder of Genesis Advisers, a firm focused on onboarding and transition acceleration. He previously taught at Harvard Business School, the Kennedy School, and INSEAD. His academic roots are in negotiation and corporate diplomacy, and it shows: his doctrine treats a leadership transition as a sequence of diagnoses and negotiations. He studied leaders in promotions, onboarding, and international moves, and for the seven shifts he interviewed more than 40 executives, HR leaders, and newly appointed unit heads about the move from running a function to running an enterprise. For this user, every step (PM to Director to VP) is a transition with a learning curve and a credibility window, and the seven shifts preview the enterprise thinking that VP and GM roles demand. Transfer caution: the lens is executive education in large corporations. The 90-day frame fits formal moves better than the unlabeled transitions Ibarra describes, and the negotiation framing assumes counterparts who are reasonably rational and interest-driven.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Promote yourself: make the mental break from the old role before day one.
- **Claim:** The biggest transition pitfall is assuming that what made you successful so far will keep working. Strengths that won the promotion can become the new role's vulnerabilities. Leaders must consciously let go of the old job's identity and habits and prepare for the new one.
- **Source:** The First 90 Days, ch. 1 ("Promote Yourself" 2003; "Prepare Yourself" 2013).
- **Changes coaching how:** Before a move, the coach has the user write a start/stop/continue list, name the two old-role behaviors most likely to come back under stress, and set a date for handing off old responsibilities. For internal promotions, the coach plans how the user will handle the pull of the old team and old requests.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a do-it-yourself transition with no new title (Ibarra) there is no day one. The coach creates one, such as a new planning cycle, a new charter, or a reorg announcement.

### D2. Diagnose the business situation before choosing a strategy. Most roles are a portfolio of situations.
- **Claim:** The STARS situations are start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, and sustaining success. Each calls for different moves, different early wins, and a different pace. Leaders over-apply the style of their last success. Turnaround veterans push too hard in a realignment, where the core challenge is creating urgency before a visible crisis, and realignment specialists move too slowly in a real crisis. A single job usually holds several situations across its products, projects, and processes.
- **Source:** The First 90 Days, ch. 3; HBR 2009. Accelerated growth was added in the 2013 edition.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach runs STARS per initiative, not per job, and checks that the user and the boss agree on the diagnosis. A typical AI portfolio: a new agent product is a start-up, a legacy ML platform losing ground to foundation models is a realignment, and the core product is sustaining success.
- **Boundary conditions:** The labels are heuristics. A product's lifecycle stage is not the same thing as its business situation. A mature product can be in turnaround.

### D3. Negotiate success with your boss through five conversations, and own the whole relationship.
- **Claim:** The five conversations are situational diagnosis, expectations, resources, style, and personal development. Hold diagnosis, expectations, and style early. Hold resources once you know what you need. Hold development once the relationship is established, around the 90-day mark. Supporting rules: don't stay away, don't surprise the boss, bring bad news early and privately, bring problems with proposed plans, clarify expectations early and often, get early wins in areas the boss cares about, and don't expect to change the boss.
- **Source:** The First 90 Days, ch. 4 (chapter notes consulted).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach turns the five conversations into written agendas and uses them as the managing-up protocol at every level. Two uses are specific. The user confirms a shared diagnosis before proposing strategy. And the development conversation explicitly asks what evidence the boss would need to see before sponsoring the user's next step.
- **Boundary conditions:** Matrixed and dotted-line roles, bosses caught in a reorg, and bosses who are themselves the problem all strain this protocol. Watkins' advice not to try to change the boss implies widening coalitions rather than fighting upward.

### D4. Early wins should build credibility and advance the long-term agenda, not just be easy.
- **Claim:** Early wins create personal credibility and organizational momentum. Pick a small number of focus areas (three or four) that matter to the boss, fit the culture, and are a down payment on the real agenda. Run change in waves: learn, design, build support, implement, then observe and consolidate. Named pitfalls include doing too much, going after the easiest wins first, setting unrealistic expectations, and acting before learning. Credibility comes from how you behave as much as from results; the book describes a balance such as demanding but satisfiable, and decisive but judicious (paraphrase).
- **Source:** The First 90 Days, ch. 5 and the pitfalls list.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks which win the boss, and the boss's boss, will notice. Is it also a step toward the strategic goal? What behavior change in the team is the real win behind the visible one?
- **Boundary conditions:** In sustaining success, the early win may be not breaking anything while learning. In a turnaround, speed beats consensus.

### D5. Accelerate learning with a plan that covers the technical, cultural, and political.
- **Claim:** New leaders fail by learning the wrong things (only technical), arriving with "the" answer, or acting before understanding. A learning plan covers the past, present, and future of the unit. It asks a standard set of questions across many people (biggest challenges, root causes, untapped opportunities, what it takes to capture them, what you'd focus on) and draws on sources beyond the hierarchy: customers, front line, integrators, the unit's historians.
- **Source:** The First 90 Days, ch. 2 and pitfalls.
- **Changes coaching how:** For an AI PM stepping up, the coach makes sure the learning plan includes the political map and culture (who decides AI investments, which past failures still shape attitudes), not only the model stack and roadmap.
- **Boundary conditions:** In very fast orgs 90 days is too long. Compress the plan, but don't skip the political and cultural parts.

### D6. Build coalitions on purpose. Map influence, sort stakeholders into supporters, opponents, and persuadables, and plan winning and blocking coalitions.
- **Claim:** Success depends on people you have no authority over. Map who must say yes, who can say no, and who influences whom. Understand each stakeholder's interests and alternatives. The book's influence techniques are consultation, framing, choice-shaping, social influence (opinion leaders), incremental commitment, sequencing, and action-forcing events. Neglecting horizontal relationships is one of the named pitfalls.
- **Source:** The First 90 Days, ch. 8 ("Create Coalitions"/"Create Alliances").
- **Changes coaching how:** For every initiative that needs cross-org support, the coach builds an influence map (F4) and a sequence of conversations, and names the action-forcing event (planning deadline, exec review, launch gate). The coach treats "I'll present it and they'll see the logic" as a red flag.
- **Boundary conditions:** In cultures that prize directness and "no politics" (Campbell), overt maneuvering damages trust. Do coalition work transparently and in the company's interest, and consult rather than manipulate.

### D7. Specialist to generalist: learn every function's mental models well enough to decide for the whole business and to judge leaders in functions you've never run.
- **Claim:** Business functions are distinct managerial subcultures, each with its own language and tools. "Enough" knowledge means making good decisions for the business as a whole and evaluating the talent that runs each function. Build a template for evaluating each function's leader; the article's sales example lists core performance metrics, people metrics, customer metrics, and warning signs. The classic trap is overmanaging the function you know and undermanaging the others. Watkins' example executive was told by HR that he was smothering his sales VP.
- **Source:** HBR 2012, "Specialist to Generalist" and the sidebar "How Do I Evaluate a Sales Executive?"
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach has the user draft evaluation templates for Engineering, Design, Data Science, Product Marketing, and Sales leadership, and watches whether a newly promoted user over-directs PM craft. Preparation: trade insight with peers in other functions to learn their models.
- **Boundary conditions:** This is sharpest at the VP or GM move, but a Director who manages a cross-functional pod faces a smaller version.

### D8. Analyst to integrator and tactician to strategist: make cross-functional trade-offs, explain them, and practice three strategic skills.
- **Claim:** The enterprise leader's job is integrating what the functional teams know and making trade-offs: supply against demand, this quarter against the future, execution against innovation. Explaining the rationale matters as much as the call. The strategic mindset rests on level shifting (moving between detail and big picture), pattern recognition (separating signal from noise), and mental simulation (anticipating how competitors, regulators, and the media will react). Watkins notes strategic talent can be weeded out early because promotions reward blocking and tackling.
- **Source:** HBR 2012, "Analyst to Integrator" and "Tactician to Strategist". The 2024 book extends this into six disciplines.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks for trade-off memos that state what is being given up and why. Before AI launches, the user runs mental simulations: how would a competitor, a regulator, or a large customer respond? For an execution-strong PM, the coach tracks whether time spent in the weeds is shrinking.
- **Boundary conditions:** Watkins holds that strategic thinking is partly innate. The coach can develop it but shouldn't promise a transformation.

### D9. Bricklayer to architect: treat the organization as a system and don't restructure before you understand the interdependencies.
- **Claim:** New enterprise leaders reach for the easy levers, structure and strategy, without understanding how those connect to processes, skill bases, and incentives. Watkins' example leader proposed a customer-focused restructuring that would have required salespeople to master far more products, and the team rejected it. Enterprise leaders need the basics of organizational design and change management, or at least need to be educated consumers of that expertise. The alignment chapter describes misfits between strategy, structure, processes, and skills.
- **Source:** HBR 2012, "Bricklayer to Architect"; The First 90 Days, ch. 6.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user proposes a reorg, for example from product-line teams to customer-segment teams, the coach asks which processes, skills, incentives, and interfaces must also change, and what the transition will cost. A Director candidate should diagnose alignment before redesigning anything.
- **Boundary conditions:** Sometimes structure really is the binding constraint and fast action is right, especially in a turnaround. The point is analysis first, not paralysis.

### D10. Problem solver to agenda setter: decide which problems the organization works on, including the white spaces no function owns.
- **Claim:** Many managers are promoted for fixing problems. Enterprise leaders must instead choose which problems the organization tackles, including "white spaces" that fall outside any single function. They must also communicate priorities in a form the organization can act on. In Watkins' case, the leader's team and the annual planning process helped him build his agenda.
- **Source:** HBR 2012, "Problem Solver to Agenda Setter".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks: "What are the three issues your org should work on in the next 12 months that nobody owns?" For AI orgs these are often evaluation infrastructure, AI governance, data rights, and cost-to-serve. The coach also checks whether the user mostly solves problems on other people's agendas.
- **Boundary conditions:** At Director level the agenda has to sit inside the boss's agenda. Setting it without alignment reads as overreach (see D3).

### D11. Warrior to diplomat: shape the external environment through negotiation, persuasion, conflict management, and alliances, sometimes working with competitors.
- **Claim:** Enterprise leaders spend surprising time influencing regulators, NGOs, media, and investors. They must look for aligned interests, understand how other kinds of organizations decide, and manage staff whose initiatives (government relations, communications) play out over years rather than quarters.
- **Source:** HBR 2012, "Warrior to Diplomat".
- **Changes coaching how:** For AI product leaders, external constituencies matter unusually early: regulators, standards bodies, model providers, strategic partners, enterprise customers' risk teams. The coach looks for chances to practice external representation now, such as partner negotiations, industry working groups, and customer advisory boards. Internally, the diplomat skill shows up in dealings with peer orgs that compete for the same resources.
- **Boundary conditions:** Most PMs have a narrow external mandate. Practice inside it and don't freelance on company positions.

### D12. Supporting cast to lead role: your words and moods get amplified, so communicate through layers and channels and build ways to hear the front line.
- **Claim:** At enterprise level people read cues into everything the leader says and does. In Watkins' example, a passing musing about packaging turned into a feasibility report on the leader's desk two weeks later. Leaders must become more self-aware, remember what the view from below feels like, spread messages through their direct reports and other channels, and create ways to hear the front line directly.
- **Source:** HBR 2012, "Supporting Cast Member to Lead Role".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach reviews the user's offhand comments in leadership forums for unintended directives, asks how a key message reaches people two levels down, and checks whether the user has any unfiltered channel to front-line reality.
- **Boundary conditions:** This applies at Director level too, with less intensity. Over-correcting into guarded blandness costs authenticity (Ibarra).

### D13. Every transition has a breakeven point. The goal is to reach it sooner and avoid vicious cycles.
- **Claim:** A new leader consumes value before creating it. Executives surveyed estimated about six months for a midlevel manager to reach breakeven. Early actions set off virtuous or vicious cycles in credibility. Managing yourself means planning discipline, personal discipline, and an advice network that covers technical, cultural, and political counsel.
- **Source:** The First 90 Days, introduction and ch. 9.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach sets a 30/60/90 plan with checkpoints and has the user map their advisers into the three types, filling the political slot first, since it is the one most often empty.
- **Boundary conditions:** The six-month figure is a survey estimate, not a law. "90 days" is a planning heuristic.

### D14. Promotion is a different problem from onboarding: breadth, delegation, influence, communication, presence. Leading former peers is its own case.
- **Claim:** In a promotion the leader faces wider scope, has to delegate more deeply, relies more on influence than on authority, communicates more formally from further away from the work, and is watched more closely. Leading former peers requires rebuilding trust and exerting influence without heavy control.
- **Source:** Your Next Move / Master Your Next Move (IMD page and summaries).
- **Changes coaching how:** For an internal promotion from PM to lead over former peers, the coach scripts an explicit renegotiation of each relationship. It also plans for presence: what the user is now seen to model, and how their communication has to change in formality and channel.
- **Boundary conditions:** Onboarding into a new company adds culture and network deficits. Promotions carry the baggage of old reputation. Diagnose which challenge dominates.

## Frameworks in operational form

### F1. STARS diagnosis per initiative
| Situation | Core challenge | Early-win focus | Typical trap | AI product example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Assemble people, funding, technology for something new | A few bets; build the team; basic operating rhythm | Over-planning, or no structure at all | New agentic product line |
| Turnaround | Rescue a unit in visible trouble | Fast, decisive cuts to the core; visible action | Too much consultation, too slowly | AI feature with runaway cost and poor quality |
| Accelerated growth | Scale systems, hiring, discipline | Processes and structure before they break | Systems lag demand; heroics as the operating model | Feature with usage surging and inference costs spiking |
| Realignment | Revive a drifting but successful unit | Build shared awareness of the problem; urgency | Moving too aggressively, or failing to convince | Legacy ML platform losing to foundation models |
| Sustaining success | Keep a high performer high and find the next growth | Learn deeply; protect what works; no gratuitous marks | Breaking what works to make a mark | Core product line with stable economics |
Procedure: list the initiatives, tag each with a situation, compare with the boss's view (conversation 1), and set the pace and early wins per tag.

### F2. The five conversations as agendas
- Situation: How do you see the state of each area? What's working and what isn't? What is the history behind that?
- Expectations: What must be true at 90 days and at 12 months? How will success be measured, and by whom? What is off the table?
- Style: How do you want updates (form, frequency)? Which decisions do you want in on, and which should I just make? How do you like bad news delivered?
- Resources: What people, budget, and executive support will the agreed expectations need? What is the trade-off if we don't get them?
- Development: Where am I strong or weak for this role? What would you need to see before backing me for the next scope? Which assignments would build that?

### F3. 90-day scaffold
- Before start: a hand-off plan for the old role; a learning plan; an initial STARS read; a list of people to meet.
- Days 1-30: learn (technical, cultural, political), the first three conversations, and credibility behaviors.
- Days 31-60: a shared diagnosis with the boss, early wins launched, priorities drafted, and a first read on the team.
- Days 61-90: agenda and priorities set, team decisions made, coalitions in place for the first big initiative, and the development conversation held.
- Throughout: a weekly self-review of what the user is excited about, which connections to make, which initiatives are in trouble, and what they missed.

### F4. Influence map
1. List the decisions the initiative needs, and who formally decides each one.
2. For each stakeholder, record their current position (support, oppose, persuadable), interests, and alternatives, and who influences them.
3. Identify the winning coalition (enough support for a yes) and any possible blocking coalition (enough to stop it).
4. Choose techniques per person: consult them, reframe the proposal, reshape the choice, bring in an opinion leader, or ask for an incremental step.
5. Sequence the conversations: persuade the opinion leaders first, then the persuadables, and meet the opponents' interests where you can.
6. Name the action-forcing event that ends the deliberation.

### F5. Seven-shifts readiness rubric (score 0-2 per shift, with evidence)
| Shift | Functional-level behavior | Enterprise-level behavior | Practice now (Director-scale) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specialist to generalist | Deep in PM craft; judges only PMs well | Evaluates Eng, Design, Data, and GTM leaders with templates | Build evaluation templates; swap knowledge with peers |
| Analyst to integrator | Optimizes own area | Makes and explains cross-functional trade-offs | Write trade-off memos for shared decisions |
| Tactician to strategist | Lives in the weekly details | Shifts levels; sees patterns; simulates reactions | Run competitor and regulator simulations before launches |
| Bricklayer to architect | Changes structure as the easy lever | Diagnoses fit among strategy, structure, process, skills | Alignment diagnosis before any reorg proposal |
| Problem solver to agenda setter | Solves what arrives | Chooses problems, including white spaces | Propose owners for the unowned issues |
| Warrior to diplomat | Fights competitors and peers | Shapes the environment; allies where interests align | Partner negotiations, industry groups |
| Supporting cast to lead role | Messages go directly | Knows the shadow; uses channels; hears the front line | Audit offhand comments; open a front-line channel |
The HBR sidebar's development moves double as stretch-assignment ideas: cross-functional project leadership, an international posting, exposure to several STARS situations, a seat on a senior team, work with external stakeholders, a chief-of-staff role, and leading an acquisition integration or restructuring.

### F6. Team assessment (when the user inherits a team)
Assess each person on competence, judgment, energy, focus, relationships, and trust, and separate brilliance from judgment. Common pitfalls: criticizing the predecessor, keeping the wrong people too long, and unsettling good people through prolonged uncertainty (per chapter notes).

## Diagnostic questions
- What kind of transition is this: promotion, onboarding, leading former peers, a do-it-yourself scope change, or a mix?
- Which STARS situations are in the portfolio, and do the user and the boss agree?
- Which of the five conversations has actually happened, and what was agreed?
- What does the boss's boss think success looks like?
- What are the three early wins, and does each advance the long-term agenda?
- Who can block the user's top initiative, and who influences them?
- Which horizontal relationships has the user neglected?
- Which old-role behaviors are reappearing under pressure?
- Where is the user on the breakeven curve, and what would speed it up?
- On the seven shifts, where is the user weakest, and what low-risk practice exists now?
- Who is in the user's advice network for technical, cultural, and political counsel?

## Observable signals
Positive:
- The user holds structured early conversations with the boss and can state the agreed expectations and measures.
- Initiatives are tagged by situation, and pace and tactics differ between them.
- The first wins are few, visible to the boss, and tied to the strategic agenda.
- The user brings bad news early and privately, with a plan attached.
- Influence maps exist for major initiatives, and conversations are sequenced before the big meeting.
- The user makes cross-functional trade-offs and explains them in writing.
- The user asks sharp questions of functions they never ran, and evaluates those leaders with explicit criteria.
- Reorg proposals come with a process, skills, and incentive analysis.
- The user sets agenda items no one else owns and gets them staffed.
- The user builds front-line listening channels and is careful with offhand remarks.
Negative:
- The user is still doing the old job, or the old team routes work to them.
- Arrives with "the answer" in week one, or launches many initiatives at once.
- Chooses easy wins that don't matter to the boss, or wins that ignore the culture.
- Surprises the boss publicly; stays away because "no news is good news".
- Relies on logic alone to win cross-org decisions; is caught out by opposition.
- Over-directs the function they came from and under-manages the rest.
- Restructures early in order to make a mark.
- Takes on every problem thrown at them, with no agenda of their own.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Treating 90 days as a deadline for big moves rather than a learning window. The book warns against the action imperative.
- Pursuing the easiest early wins, then looking busy but strategically irrelevant.
- Running the five conversations as a one-time checklist instead of a recurring alignment practice.
- Turning coalition-building into manipulation or back-channeling. It destroys trust in cultures like Campbell's, and it corrodes the user's reputation.
- Applying one STARS label to a whole role, which mis-sets the pace for half the portfolio.
- Treating the seven shifts as a Director-level bar. They describe the function-to-enterprise move. At Director level, use them as practice targets.
- The coach overlooking the negotiation premise: when the boss is misaligned or unstable, the protocol needs the coalition and sponsorship skills alongside it.
- Mechanical onboarding with no sense of how this org actually works. The political learning is the part people skip.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Watkins vs. Ibarra.** Watkins prescribes a planned, diagnosis-first entry (learning plan, then early wins in waves). Ibarra says act first and let thinking follow, because the new identity only forms through action. Waves of early wins are experiments too, so the gap is narrower than it looks. Watkins is more right when entering a formal role with a honeymoon and a clear boss. Ibarra is more right in an unlabeled transition where the self-concept, not the plan, is the constraint.
- **Watkins vs. Campbell.** Watkins treats coalition-building and influence mapping as core leadership work. Campbell's teams prize directness, team-first behavior, and an aversion to politics. Campbell's norm is more right inside a well-run team. Watkins is more right for cross-org initiatives where the user has no authority. Reconcile by building coalitions in the open, on the merits and in the company's interest.
- **Watkins vs. Gabarro and Kotter.** Gabarro's study of managers taking charge found the process takes two to three years, moving through stages of learning and reshaping. Watkins compresses the first phase into 90 days. Both agree that learning precedes reshaping. Watkins governs the credibility window and Gabarro sets expectations for deep change. Kotter and Gabarro's "Managing Your Boss" supports the five conversations.
- **Watkins vs. Slootman and Horowitz.** Slootman argues for raising urgency immediately. Watkins warns about the action imperative. STARS reconciles them: urgency is right in turnaround and accelerated growth and costly in sustaining success.
- **Watkins vs. Charan.** Charan prepares people for passages over years. Watkins lands them in months. The seven shifts roughly match Charan's Passage Three-to-Four move. Watkins puts more weight on diplomacy and influence.
- **Watkins vs. Pfeffer.** Both take power seriously. Pfeffer is more explicit that power-seeking and self-promotion drive careers, while Watkins frames influence as principled negotiation. Pfeffer is the corrective when a user's influence map ignores self-interest.
- **Watkins vs. Lencioni.** Watkins assesses and reshapes an inherited team quickly. Lencioni builds trust before conflict and commitment. When the team is broadly competent, Lencioni's sequence protects performance. When it is misaligned, Watkins' faster assessment wins.
- **Watkins vs. Larson.** Larson's advice for new engineering executives also stresses learning the company's existing processes before changing them. That supports Watkins' warning in D9 against early restructuring.

## Limits and biases
- The evidence is interviews (40+ for the seven shifts), consulting cases, and executive surveys. It is qualitative, and the case subjects are often composites.
- The model fits large corporations. It is less tuned to startups, where roles form and change monthly, and to flat product orgs where a "transition" may be a scope creep with no date.
- The negotiation-scholar heritage assumes rational, interest-based counterparts. It says less about irrational bosses, toxic cultures, or identity-based resistance.
- Presence and credibility advice has no gender or identity analysis. Behaviors read as "decisive" in one person may be penalized in another (see Ibarra, Women Rising).
- The seven shifts are about function-to-enterprise moves. Applying them at the Director level needs deliberate scaling down.
- The 90-day and 6.2-month figures are heuristics and survey estimates, not measured causal effects.
- Genesis Advisers sells transition programs, a commercial interest in treating transitions as high-risk events.
