# Patrick Lencioni

## Why this source matters here
Lencioni is a consultant (founder of The Table Group), not an operator. His models come from facilitating CEO staffs and their leadership teams, and are delivered as business fables. What he supplies here is the peer-team side of promotion: a new Director or VP is judged by the boss and by peers on whether they act like a member of the leadership team or like a lobbyist for their own function. The user can practice this behavior now, in any cross-functional leadership forum they sit in. Treat the models as diagnostic lenses, not validated science.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. Your first team is the peer leadership team you sit on, not the function you lead
- **Claim:** Leaders belong to two teams: the one they run and the one they are a member of. Healthy organizations expect members of a leadership team to put that team first. In decisions they set aside their functional role and argue for the enterprise. Leaders who put their own department first turn leadership meetings into contests over resources, and the people below them are left with conflicting priorities they cannot resolve.
- **Source:** The Advantage, Discipline 1; Table Group "What is Your First Team?"
- **Changes coaching how:** Readiness for Director or VP is partly measured by first-team behavior. The coach checks the user's recent tradeoff debates. Did they ever argue for something that cost their own product line (headcount, roadmap slot, launch credit) because the company needed it? If every position they took helped their own area, the coach calls it functional lobbying and the diagnosis is below-level behavior, however good the arguments were.
- **Boundary conditions:** First-team loyalty only pays off when the peer group is a real team with shared goals and a leader who referees. In a hub-and-spoke staff where the boss deals with each report separately, first-team behavior may not be returned, so the user should push for explicit norms before giving things up. Putting the first team first does not mean abandoning your own team. You still bring your team's reality into the room, and then you decide as an executive.

### D2. Build vulnerability-based trust, and go first
- **Claim:** Lencioni distinguishes two kinds of trust. Predictive trust means knowing how someone will behave. Vulnerability-based trust means confidence that peers mean well, so no one needs to be guarded. The second kind requires admitting mistakes, weaknesses and needs for help, and without it the rest of the pyramid fails.
- **Source:** Five Dysfunctions (dysfunction 1); Table Group summary PDF; The Advantage.
- **Changes coaching how:** Ambitious PMs often try to look flawless to peers and seniors. The coach treats that as a trust cost, not as polish. It asks for concrete, bounded disclosures to peers: a call the user got wrong, a skill gap, a request for help.
- **Boundary conditions:** Disclose to peers who have a baseline of good faith, not to evaluators who are hostile. Disclosure is about work (mistakes, gaps), not personal oversharing. The penalty for showing vulnerability is not equal for everyone (a coach caveat, not Lencioni's claim), so calibrate the pace and the audience.

### D3. Artificial harmony is the failure mode; mine for conflict about ideas
- **Claim:** Teams that lack trust avoid open debate. Disagreement moves into back channels and veiled comments, and decisions get worse. Leaders should draw out buried disagreement and give explicit permission for it in the moment.
- **Source:** Five Dysfunctions (dysfunction 2); Table Group summary PDF.
- **Changes coaching how:** A user who stays quiet in the executive meeting and then reopens the decision in one-on-ones is showing the dysfunction, and the coach names it that way. It asks: "Where did you disagree in the room this month, and how did you frame it?"
- **Boundary conditions:** The conflict has to be about ideas, not people. It works best when members already have trust (D2). Norms about open disagreement vary by culture and org, so the user may need to set it up in advance ("I'm going to push on this").

### D4. Commit without consensus, then cascade
- **Claim:** Commitment needs clarity and buy-in, not unanimity. People can commit to a decision they argued against if their view was heard. Commitment fails when the decision is vague or opinions were never aired. Healthy teams end meetings by stating what was decided and what each person will tell their teams. They then pass that message down consistently and quickly. Table Group consultants suggest brief, unscripted conversations with teams within about a day.
- **Source:** Five Dysfunctions (dysfunction 3); The Advantage (overcommunicate clarity, cascading messages); Table Group "Peril" article.
- **Changes coaching how:** A strong executive-readiness signal is carrying a decision you lost to your own team without undermining it, and without "they made me do this." The coach has the user rehearse that cascade. It also recommends the user volunteer to run the closing commitment check in meetings they attend.
- **Boundary conditions:** If the user was not in the debate, what they can offer is compliance, not commitment (see Hill). In that case they should first ask to be heard.

### D5. Peer-to-peer accountability beats leader-policed accountability
- **Claim:** Once a team has committed to a clear plan, members should hold each other to both behaviors and results, even though it is uncomfortable. If only the boss enforces standards, accountability is weak and slow.
- **Source:** Five Dysfunctions (dysfunction 4); The Advantage (Discipline 1).
- **Changes coaching how:** Most PMs send peer problems up to the boss. The coach asks whether the user raised the issue directly with the peer first, referring to the agreement they both committed to. Escalating without doing that reads as political.
- **Boundary conditions:** Only possible after explicit commitments exist (D4). Without positional authority, point to the shared agreement rather than your own preference.

### D6. Collective results over status, ego and functional goals
- **Claim:** When individuals are not held accountable, they put ego, career and recognition ahead of team results. Healthy leadership teams agree on "what is most important, right now" and judge themselves on shared results.
- **Source:** Five Dysfunctions (dysfunction 5); The Advantage (six critical questions).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach tests whether the user's updates and asks start from the company's top priority or from their own product's metrics. It rewrites updates so the product's contribution to that shared priority comes first.
- **Boundary conditions:** Where compensation and promotion reward only functional results, the user will have to manage both. They should name the tension to their boss rather than quietly optimizing locally.

### D7. Humble, hungry and people-smart, and beware the "skillful politician"
- **Claim:** Ideal team players combine humility (share credit, define success collectively), hunger (self-motivated, seek more responsibility) and people-smarts (read group dynamics and their own impact). Missing any one causes problems. Hungry and smart without humble is the most dangerous combination: the person appears well-meaning while serving themselves. The book calls this type the skillful politician.
- **Source:** The Ideal Team Player; Table Group model PDF.
- **Changes coaching how:** An ambitious user risks being hungry and smart but seen as short on humility. The coach audits credit-sharing and attention-seeking behavior before any visibility push. The same model can be used as a quick screen when the user hires or promotes.
- **Boundary conditions:** It is a team-player screen, not a full executive hiring bar: it leaves out judgment, strategy and domain depth. "Smart" means people-smart, not intellect.

### D8. Create clarity and overcommunicate it
- **Claim:** Leadership teams must agree on six questions: why do we exist, how do we behave, what do we do, how will we succeed, what is most important right now, and who must do what. They then repeat the answers much more than feels necessary and reinforce them through hiring, performance management, recognition and meetings.
- **Source:** The Advantage, Disciplines 2 to 4; Table Group model PDF.
- **Changes coaching how:** For a user leading a product area, the coach has them write short answers to the six questions for their scope and check whether their peers would give the same answers. Gaps point to misalignment before it shows up as conflict.
- **Boundary conditions:** Repeating an answer the peer team never agreed on spreads confusion. Behavioral alignment (D1 to D5) has to come first.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Leadership-team dysfunction scan** (run on each leadership forum the user belongs to):
| Layer | Symptom to look for | What the user can do without authority |
|---|---|---|
| Trust | People guard information; no one admits errors | Go first with one bounded admission |
| Conflict | Quiet meetings, lively hallways | Name the unspoken disagreement; ask for dissent explicitly |
| Commitment | Same topic reopened; teams hear different versions | Run "what did we decide / what do we tell our teams" at close |
| Accountability | Issues go to the boss, not peer-to-peer | Raise it with the peer directly, referring to the agreement |
| Results | Updates list function wins, not shared goals | Report against the shared top priority |

**First-team audit** (monthly): (1) List the last three resource or priority debates. (2) Mark each position you took as enterprise-first or function-first. (3) Name one thing you gave up for the enterprise. (4) Did you pass the last lost decision to your team cleanly? (5) Did you raise a peer issue directly before escalating it?

**Humble/hungry/smart self-check:** For each virtue, write one piece of evidence from the last month and one piece of counter-evidence. Ask a trusted peer which virtue they would rate lowest.

## Diagnostic questions
- Which team does the user treat as their first team, going by their calendar and their positions rather than what they say?
- When did the user last argue against their own function's interest in a leadership forum?
- Is the peer group a real team (shared goal, a leader who referees) or a set of one-on-one relationships with the boss?
- Where does disagreement show up: in the room, or afterwards?
- How do the user's reports describe decisions the user lost?
- Would a peer describe the user as hungry and smart but not humble?

## Observable signals
- Positive: gives up resources for a company priority and says so plainly; names disagreement in the meeting; passes decisions down consistently within a day; confronts peers directly and fairly; credits peers in public; reports against shared goals.
- Negative: argues every position from their function's benefit; stays quiet in the room, then vetoes in private; "leadership decided" framing to their team; escalates peer friction to the boss first; takes credit visibly; keeps a polished front that shuts down peer candor.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using "first team" to justify dropping advocacy for your own team, or to justify an insular leadership clique. Table Group itself warns that cohesion can look like groupthink if it is not communicated downward.
- Performing vulnerability in a scripted way, or in front of people who will use it against you.
- Treating "conflict" as permission to be abrasive (the bulldozer failure mode).
- Invoking "disagree and commit" to shut down dissent before it has been heard.
- Using humble/hungry/smart as a personality label instead of as observed behavior.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Pfeffer:** Lencioni says healthy organizations have all but eliminated politics. Pfeffer says power is unavoidable and must be built deliberately. Lencioni is more right inside a working peer team with a strong referee. Pfeffer is more right when incentives are zero-sum and the "team" is nominal, which is common at Director level.
- **Horowitz and Slootman:** They model projecting strength and decisiveness under pressure. Lencioni asks the leader to show vulnerability first. Vulnerability works among peers who already have some trust. Projecting strength matters more in a crisis or with outside audiences.
- **Larson:** They agree on first team (Larson's "make your peers your first team"). Larson adds that each executive's narrative is incomplete but valid. He also notes that executives who are only tolerated or resented get no return on first-team behavior.
- **Hill:** They agree on commitment versus compliance and on team versus one-on-one. Hill focuses on the manager's own team and network, Lencioni on the peer team. A new Director needs both.
- **Bezos/Amazon:** Both endorse disagree-and-commit. Amazon puts more weight on written mechanisms and less on relationships.

## Limits and biases
Fables and consulting frameworks with little peer-reviewed validation. The assessment built on the model is a commercial product. The work centers on the CEO's staff and assumes a leader who will referee. It is US corporate in tone. It says little about power asymmetries, identity-based costs of vulnerability, or remote teams. The evidence is practitioner experience and client stories.
