# Shreyas Doshi

## Why this source matters here
Doshi was a PM and PM leader at Yahoo, Google (about six years, largely ads), Twitter and Stripe, where he was an early PM, became the first PM manager and helped grow the function from about 5 to more than 50 people. He led Stripe Connect and Stripe Terminal. Since leaving operating roles he teaches (Maven courses on product sense, product strategy and PM careers) and writes aphoristic threads on X. His material is unusually direct about the parts of senior product work that formal ladders leave out: perception, optics, busyness and the gap between a PM's level of thinking and an executive's. Two cautions shape transfer. His diagnoses are explicitly bounded to high-performing, well-intentioned environments. And by his own account he stepped off the traditional climb when his org fan-out reached about 50 people, because the optics load did not suit him. That makes him an excellent source on the cost of the executive path and a weaker source on how to thrive at VP scale.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. Before litigating a disagreement, check whether the parties are working at different levels
- **Claim:** Product work happens at three levels: impact (what difference this makes to customers, the business, the brand), execution (what it takes to get it done and hit the next milestone), and optics (awareness of the impact and execution). People have a default level. When a PM and a CEO, or a PM and their team, sit at different levels, they argue over details without noticing the real mismatch. His recurring example: a PM explains, correctly, why customer support will be thin at launch (execution level), while the CEO is reacting to the damage to customer experience and brand (impact level). Which level an organization promotes people for also shapes its culture.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast 2022; X thread Mar 2021; Amplitude conversation 2021.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user reports a product review that "went badly for no reason," the coach first asks what level each party was operating at. It rehearses the user naming the mismatch out loud, as Doshi suggests: acknowledge that the work is strong at the execution level, say plainly where it falls short at the impact level, and state what would change that. In pre-reads, the coach checks that the first paragraph speaks to impact before execution detail.
- **Boundary conditions:** Level language is a diagnostic, not an excuse. Sometimes the executive is simply wrong on the facts, and sometimes the user's plan really is weak at the impact level.

### D2. Optics is legitimate work; the danger is an organization that rewards it as the goal
- **Claim:** Doshi defines internal optics as creating awareness of the impact and the execution you or your team are delivering. Some optics is necessary and good: it creates energy, excitement, feedback and opportunities. The balance breaks when an organization signals, through who it hires, fires and promotes and what the CEO praises at all-hands (a great status update rather than a result), that doing optics well is enough. He says one mistake of his own career was downplaying optics.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast 2022; X post Apr 2024.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach treats a user who dismisses internal communication as "politics" as having a blind spot, not a virtue. It also checks the opposite: if most of the user's recognized work is updates, decks and forums rather than outcomes, the user is being shaped by an optics-rewarding culture. Allocation should be mostly execution, a good share on impact, and adequate optics.
- **Boundary conditions:** "Adequate" is context-specific. A platform team that caused outages owes its dependents more optics; an early-stage team should spend very little.

### D3. Optics load grows with scope; decide deliberately whether that job suits you
- **Claim:** As scope grows, a leader in almost any company must spend much more time at the optics level. Being forced to operate outside your default level most of the week produces frustration that willpower cannot fix indefinitely. Doshi's default was impact; at Stripe, when his org fan-out reached about 50 people, he chose to move back to earlier-stage products rather than keep climbing. His advice is to identify your strengths and make career choices on that basis, not out of expectation or envy of peers' titles. He also describes the PM leader's job as lonelier than the PM's.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast live, Oct 2024.
- **Changes coaching how:** For a user targeting VP, the coach makes the optics tax explicit early: a large share of the calendar will be communication, alignment and representation. It asks the user to estimate their default level from recent weeks and to test, with evidence from stretch assignments, whether they can sustain heavy optics work without burning out. This is a trajectory-model input, not a reason to stop.
- **Boundary conditions:** Doshi describes his own preference and choice; other sources (Ibarra, Charan) argue that people grow into new work values by doing the work. The coach should not let "stay true to my default level" become a way to avoid the next level's real demands.

### D4. Most execution problems are strategy, interpersonal or culture problems
- **Claim:** In high-performing, well-intentioned organizations, leaders stay overwhelmed because they keep solving recurring "execution" problems with new meetings and processes. Doshi's worked example is two misaligned teams. The misalignment usually comes from missing or conflicting strategy (teams wrote OKRs without one), from a culture that punishes teams for helping others at the cost of their own OKRs, or from two managers who do not get along. Real execution problems exist too: aging infrastructure, and skill gaps (for example a zero-to-one PM put on a scaled, mature product). The reliable tell of a fake execution problem is a band-aid that has been applied before and fell off, with short organizational memory hiding the repetition.
- **Source:** X post, Oct 2020; Lenny's Podcast 2022.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user proposes a new sync, review process or tracker, the coach asks whether a similar fix has been tried and how long it lasted. If it failed before, the coach redirects to the strategy question (is there a written strategy both teams accept?), the culture question (what does each team get rewarded for?), or the interpersonal question (which two people need a direct conversation, possibly mediated?).
- **Boundary conditions:** Doshi scopes the claim to high-performing environments with good intent. In low-capability or low-trust environments, the problem may really be execution, capacity or bad faith.

### D5. Sort work by leverage and spend perfectionism only where it pays
- **Claim:** In a high-leverage role, tasks come in three kinds. Leverage tasks return 10x to 100x the effort. Neutral tasks return roughly what you put in. Overhead tasks return less than the effort. Ambitious perfectionists treat all three the same, which is the problem. The same activity can fall in any bucket: notes from a routine meeting are overhead (send the three action items immediately), while notes from a contentious CEO product review that settled a decision are leverage and deserve an hour or two of careful wording. The leverage tasks people avoid are usually the ones they fear, strategy above all, because they doubt they have anything good to say. His tactics: fill the preceding days deliberately with neutral and overhead work, then change location on the day reserved for the leverage task.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast 2022; Amplitude conversation 2021.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user to label this week's list L, N or O and to name the L task they have been postponing. A strategy document that has been "next month" for a quarter is treated as a fear signal, not a time problem. The coach also checks for over-investment in neutral tasks that build a reputation for polish (beautiful notes, exhaustive bug reports) at the expense of leverage.
- **Boundary conditions:** Some overhead tasks carry high downside if done badly (compliance filings, anything a regulator or auditor reads); "overhead" means low upside, not zero risk.

### D6. In high-leverage roles, minimize opportunity cost instead of chasing positive ROI
- **Claim:** A PM or leader can find hundreds of positive-ROI tasks. Because time sits in the denominator of ROI, optimizing it pushes teams toward quick wins and low-hanging fruit. The better question is whether something is the best use of time, not whether it is a good use. Doshi does not recommend quantifying opportunity cost. The leader's lever is permission: in planning he gives teams rough allocation guidance (his example was about 60% incremental improvements, 30% one or two big new initiatives, 10% stability and infrastructure, adapted from Google's 70-20-10), so teams have explicit room for ambiguous, larger bets. His Stripe Connect example: he moved a team off a list of positive-ROI items onto a larger marketplace-payments bet.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast 2022.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user presents a quarterly plan of well-defined, positive-ROI items, the coach asks which one or two ambiguous opportunities could change the trajectory of the business and are absent. For a user leading a team, the coach suggests setting allocation guidance before teams plan, rather than approving bottom-up lists.
- **Boundary conditions:** Where the business is in survival mode or deeply behind on reliability, the allocation should shift heavily toward the near term; the percentages were his example, not a rule.

### D7. Run pre-mortems that make it safe to name the real risks
- **Claim:** Borrowed from Gary Klein's HBR pre-mortem: at the start of an important initiative, ask the team to imagine it has failed badly and work backward to what went wrong. Doshi's version has each person log three kinds of risk privately during quiet time: tigers (real threats that could kill the effort), paper tigers (threats others worry about but the author does not), and elephants (things nobody is discussing). Everyone who will do the work attends (all engineers; separate go-to-market sessions for large launches). People then vote on the scariest tiger raised by someone else. The leader owns a prioritized action plan and is accountable for progress. The episode page also describes classifying tigers as launch-blocking, fast-follow or track; those labels come from his Coda template and are not in the transcript. The long-term payoff is shared vocabulary: teams start raising tigers in meetings he does not attend.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast 2022 (transcript and episode page).
- **Changes coaching how:** Before any high-visibility launch, the coach asks whether the user has run a pre-mortem and what the elephants were. When a launch draws avoidable backlash, the coach asks who on the team saw it coming and why it was not said. This is a cheap, visible way for the user to show executive-level risk judgment.
- **Boundary conditions:** In low-trust teams, even anonymous logging may not surface elephants; the user may need a trusted third party to collect them. The ritual only works if the leader acts on it.

### D8. Chronic busyness at senior levels signals missing strategy and weak product decisions
- **Claim:** Eventually scope outgrows every productivity system. Doshi describes 16 or 17 stressed years before he found the fix. Annual planning is the illustration: four to six weeks of templates and debates, a plan that is abandoned by late February. With a real product strategy that stakeholders had already aligned on, his Stripe Connect planning took about three days, and he skipped templates that did not make sense. His other admission: he was busy because he was not making good enough product decisions, since each weak feature decision creates follow-on work.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast live, Oct 2024.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user reports overload, the coach does not start with time management. It asks whether a written, pre-aligned strategy exists for the user's area and how many current work items trace to decisions the user would not make again. Planning season is a good test: a long planning cycle suggests the strategy is missing or not agreed.
- **Boundary conditions:** Bending planning rules worked for a senior leader with a credible strategy at a company that tolerated it. A less-established user should secure their manager's cover before skipping mandated artifacts.

### D9. At your level, most two-way doors are one-way doors
- **Claim:** Teams invoke "two-way door" to decide quickly on a feature without thinking through customer motivation, differentiation and distribution. In practice the decision does not reverse: the feature ships, adoption is weak, the leader reaches for favorable anecdotes at the quarterly business review, sales says it lacks table stakes, and the team signs up for more work on a feature it should not have built. Doshi argues decisions that are reversible at Bezos's level are often irreversible for a PM leader, and that pausing for two minutes, two days or two weeks is often right. His summary: thinking is cheap, so do more of it.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast live, Oct 2024.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user describes a quick "reversible" feature decision, the coach asks what reversal would actually cost them (sales commitments, customer expectations, QBR narrative, team morale) and whether motivation, differentiation and distribution were examined. The coach flags the use of anecdotes to defend weak adoption as a symptom of earlier decision debt.
- **Boundary conditions:** Where a change is truly reversible (internal experiment, flag-gated test with no commitments), speed still wins. The claim is about what happens in practice to features that reach customers.

### D10. High agency is the trait that separates PMs who exceed their paper credentials
- **Claim:** Crediting the term to Eric Weinstein, Doshi defines high agency as finding a way to get what you want without waiting for perfect conditions or blaming circumstances. For PMs it has three parts: ownership, creative execution through obstacles, and resilience. He saw it as the main difference between PMs who outperformed their résumés and talented PMs who never reached their potential.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast 2022.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach listens for the ratio of constraint-description to constraint-reversal in the user's accounts ("we couldn't because legal..." versus "legal blocked it, so I..."). In promotion evidence, the coach favors stories where the user changed the conditions (secured resources, redrew scope, fixed a dependency) over stories of performing well within them.
- **Boundary conditions:** Agency that ignores decision rights or bypasses a manager reads as insubordination at senior levels; see Larson and Watkins on staying aligned with authority.

### D11. Becoming a PM leader adds representation, editing and audience-tailored communication, and fails through identity lag
- **Claim:** From the Amplitude conversation (primary): newly promoted PMs often keep an IC identity, attend meetings to show they still have it, and drift into micromanagement; Doshi says he did this himself. He argues a leader's job is to help people know when to use their non-default behaviors, and that leaders should protect time for thinking rather than a packed calendar. From a secondary write-up of a 2023 talk (attribution through Shyvee Shi's notes): PMs need execution ability and product judgment, and PM leaders additionally need the trust to represent organizational priorities, the ability to align teams to a vision, the skill of editing (evaluating others' proposals, often by asking whether the team has the right problem and clear metrics rather than prescribing answers), strategic thinking, and communication tailored to what each executive values.
- **Source:** Amplitude conversation, July 2021; Shyvee Shi's write-up of a July 2023 talk (secondary).
- **Changes coaching how:** For a new manager, the coach watches for the "prove I still have it" pattern: rewriting PRDs, dominating reviews, jumping into execution. The coach develops editing as a distinct skill, measured by the quality of questions the user asks in their team's reviews rather than the answers they supply.
- **Boundary conditions:** Player-coach roles (group PM) legitimately mix creating and editing; the identity risk is highest in pure manager roles.

### D12. Perception at review time runs on content, confidence and context; find out how people with power see you
- **Claim:** In mid-sized and large companies, your work is perceived along three dimensions: content (ideas, proposals, problem-solving, what you ship, metrics moved, business impact), confidence (whether you seem in control, able to take on hard work, and as leadership material, and how you communicate), and context (your feel for the company's unwritten culture and power structure, and how you adapt to it). He is explicit that these are about perception, not the true measure of impact. Separately, he calls the most important and rarest feedback from a manager an honest account of how you are perceived by people in positions of power. In Deb Liu's write-up of their conversation, he also notes that promotion systems favor articulate presenters, which disadvantages quieter people.
- **Source:** X thread, Sep 2024; X post, Nov 2022; Deb Liu interview, Nov 2022.
- **Changes coaching how:** This is a direct input to attribution analysis. When the user's results are strong but reviews are lukewarm, the coach scores confidence and context separately from content and asks the user to request specific perception feedback from their manager ("how do the VP and the CPO describe my work?").
- **Boundary conditions:** The model describes how evaluation often works, not how it should. It should be used to close perception gaps, not to swap content for performance of confidence (see D15).

### D13. Influence stakeholders by making the idea theirs, and manage expectations rather than tasks
- **Claim:** Doshi's advice on influence is to help stakeholders arrive at the idea themselves through good questions. It appears slower than pitching a finished idea and asking for feedback, but produces real buy-in and avoids months of escalations and half-hearted support. In the Coda piece he describes management as setting, aligning on and managing expectations rather than managing tasks and schedules, and says how others perceive your communication largely decides your career trajectory.
- **Source:** LinkedIn post (c. 2023); Coda blog with Lane Shackleton.
- **Changes coaching how:** Before a high-stakes alignment conversation, the coach helps the user draft the questions that lead a stakeholder to the conclusion, not just the pitch. When a stakeholder repeatedly escalates, the coach asks whether expectations were ever explicitly set and agreed.
- **Boundary conditions:** For executive decisions under time pressure, lead with the answer (Minto). Question-led influence suits contested changes where ownership matters more than speed.

### D14. Build taste in what you believe, not only in pixels
- **Claim:** Doshi says taste is the ability to recognize what is good before results prove it (his image is "game recognize game" in the practice session, not after the championship). He criticizes senior people who become harsh graders, since calling everything bad takes no taste. He lists ways people adopt ideas for the wrong reasons: catchy metaphors, alliteration, authority (because a famous executive said it), and charts or math they do not understand, which some leaders use deliberately. He credits his own shift to evaluating ideas apart from their social and authority proof. At Google he absorbed a belief that strategy did not matter, and Twitter's struggles later convinced him it did. He defines product sense as the ability to usually make correct product decisions, macro and micro, even under major ambiguity.
- **Source:** Lenny's Podcast live, Oct 2024; Maven course page (product sense definition).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user justifies a position by citing a framework, a famous executive or a slick chart, the coach asks for the argument stripped of its source. The coach also applies this to its own use of frameworks, including Doshi's.
- **Boundary conditions:** Authority is sometimes a reasonable prior; the point is not to dismiss experts but to check the argument separately from who made it.

### D15. Learn the incompetent leader's playbook so you neither fall for it nor copy it
- **Claim:** Doshi describes a savvy, charismatic incompetent leader who is good at feigning competence, creating confusion, buying time and failing up. Typical moves: first claiming the team is wrong and execution is impossible without new hires (buying six to nine months), then claiming the org structure is wrong (buying a reorg and time for it to settle), and so on until a lateral or upward exit. His warning is not to confuse charisma with competence. He also suggests trying to build more charisma, as long as it is not used to mislead.
- **Source:** X thread, Dec 2020 (text via search results and thread fragments).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user reports to or competes with a leader whose narrative outpaces results, the coach helps the user track commitments and dates rather than impressions. It also runs the pattern against the user: does the user's plan ask for more people or a reorg before showing any output?
- **Boundary conditions:** Hiring and reorganizing are sometimes the right first moves for a new leader. The signal is repetition without output, not any request for resources.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Level-mismatch triage (for any recurring conflict or bad review)
| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Name each party's default level: impact, execution or optics. Use their questions as evidence (CEOs asking about brand or customer experience are at impact). |
| 2 | Identify which level the decision actually needs in this context (early-stage: execution; platform after incidents: more optics; strategy review: impact). |
| 3 | Restate the user's position at the other party's level before arguing details. |
| 4 | If the user's work is weak at the needed level, say so explicitly and propose what would change it. |

### Fake-execution-problem test
1. Has a fix for this problem been tried before? When, and how long did it hold?
2. Is there a written strategy that both parties accept? If not, it is probably a strategy problem.
3. What is each team rewarded for? If helping the other hurts their own review, it is a culture problem.
4. Do the two leads get along? If not, it is interpersonal; coach them or mediate.
5. Only if all three are clear: is it aging infrastructure or a skill gap? Then it is a real execution problem.

### Weekly LNO pass
- Label every item L, N or O. Expect few L items.
- Schedule L items in peak-energy blocks; set a deliberately low bar for N and O.
- Name the L item postponed longest and what the user fears about it.
- Re-label activities by context (the same meeting notes can be O or L).

### Pre-mortem run sheet
- Timing: early in an important initiative, and before large launches.
- Invite: everyone who will build it; a separate session for go-to-market functions on large launches.
- Prompt: "Imagine this failed badly six months from now. What went wrong?"
- Quiet time for private entries: tigers, paper tigers, elephants.
- Round-robin sharing; each person votes for the scariest tiger raised by someone else.
- Leader publishes a prioritized action plan (optionally launch-blocking, fast-follow or track) and reports progress.

### Planning allocation guidance
Before teams plan, the leader states a rough split (Doshi's example: 60% incremental, 30% one or two big initiatives, 10% stability) tied to the strategy, and asks teams to propose within it. The coach checks whether the "big" bucket actually contains an ambiguous, trajectory-changing bet.

### Perception audit (Content, Confidence, Context)
For the last review cycle, have the user rate themselves and, separately, collect their manager's view on: content (outcomes and ideas), confidence (seen as in control and as "leadership material"), context (fit with how decisions really get made). The largest gap between self-view and manager's view becomes the development goal.

### Door-type check at the user's level
For a proposed "quick" decision: what does reversal cost the user specifically (customer promises, sales narrative, QBR credibility, team morale)? Have customer motivation, differentiation and distribution been examined? If reversal is expensive at the user's level, treat it as a one-way door and slow down.

## Diagnostic questions
- In the last contentious review, what level was the user speaking at, and what level was the most senior person at?
- Which recurring problem has the user "fixed" more than once? What was the band-aid each time?
- What is the leverage task the user has postponed longest, and what do they fear it will reveal?
- How many items in the user's current plan are quick wins? What large, ambiguous opportunity is missing?
- Did the user run a pre-mortem before their last major launch? What elephant was named, and what happened to it?
- How long did the user's last planning cycle take, and how much of the plan survived the first quarter?
- Which current work traces to a feature decision the user would now reverse?
- How do the user's manager and skip-level describe the user's work to others? Has the user asked?
- Is the user's recognized work mostly outcomes or mostly visibility artifacts?
- Where in recent stories did the user change conditions rather than work within them?
- As a new manager, when did the user last redo a direct report's work instead of asking a question that improved it?
- Honestly, how much optics-level work can the user sustain, and is the VP path they are pursuing the right one for them?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating at the next level, per Doshi):
- Opens executive conversations with impact, then execution; names level mismatches explicitly and without blame.
- Ends a recurring problem by addressing strategy, incentives or a relationship, and the problem stays fixed.
- Has a written strategy that makes planning short and escalations easier to adjudicate.
- Protects blocks for leverage work; does visibly lower-polish work on overhead tasks.
- Runs pre-mortems whose action plans are tracked; the team uses the vocabulary without prompting.
- Proposes at least one large, ambiguous bet per planning cycle, with a stated allocation.
- Knows, from direct feedback, how senior leaders perceive them, and acts on the gap.
- Converts obstacles into changed conditions (secured headcount, renegotiated scope) rather than explanations.

Negative (current-level or below):
- Responds to executive impact concerns with more execution detail.
- Creates new syncs and processes for problems that have been "solved" before.
- Postpones strategy work for months, citing firefighting.
- Fills plans with positive-ROI quick wins; no bet the team is nervous about.
- Defends weak feature adoption with anecdotes at business reviews.
- Treats all internal communication as politics and is surprised by weak perception; or the opposite, is praised mainly for updates and decks.
- New manager rewrites reports' specs and dominates their reviews.
- Asks for more people or a reorg before showing results.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Using "optics is legitimate" to justify visibility-seeking that is not tied to real impact or execution. Doshi's definition requires something real to create awareness of.
- Using "I'm an impact-level person" as a reason to skip execution rigor or the optics that senior roles require.
- Applying the fake-execution-problem lens in a low-capability team, where the problem really is execution.
- Treating LNO as a license to do sloppy work on anything the user finds boring, including risky overhead.
- Running pre-mortems as theater: collecting tigers and never publishing an action plan.
- Citing "most two-way doors are one-way" to delay decisions that really are cheap to reverse.
- Treating Doshi's allocation percentages or level balance as fixed rules; he offers them as examples.
- Coach misuse: adopting Doshi's catchy frameworks uncritically. His own warning about metaphors, alliteration and authority applies to his frameworks too.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Horowitz (anti-politics).** Horowitz defines politics as advancing careers or agendas by means other than merit and contribution, and treats it as a design failure the CEO must remove. Doshi treats optics as necessary work and describes how perception (confidence, context) shapes reviews. The two are compatible when optics means making real merit visible; they collide when an organization rewards optics as the goal, which Doshi describes and Horowitz would call politics. For a user inside a company, Doshi's description is the operating reality; Horowitz's is the standard the user should apply once they run an org.
- **Pfeffer (power).** Pfeffer argues individuals must deliberately build power and that performance alone rarely suffices. Doshi largely agrees on perception but chose to step off the path when optics dominated. Pfeffer would call that leaving power on the table; Doshi would call it choosing your strengths. The coach should make the user choose consciously.
- **Bezos/Amazon.** Amazon's doctrine favors fast decisions on reversible (two-way door) choices. Doshi argues that at a PM leader's level, many such doors are one-way in practice. Bezos is more right for internal experiments and CEO-level optionality; Doshi is more right for customer-facing features that create sales and roadmap commitments.
- **Duke.** Both value pre-mortems and separating decision quality from outcome. Doshi's "slow down" and Duke's advice to spend less time on low-impact, reversible decisions differ mostly in how they classify reversibility.
- **Minto.** Minto says lead with the answer. Doshi's influence tactic is to lead with questions so the stakeholder owns the idea. Minto fits executive updates and decision requests; Doshi fits contested changes where buy-in is the bottleneck.
- **Ibarra and Charan.** Doshi's "to thine own self be true" and choice to stop at a certain scope sit against Ibarra's authenticity paradox (clinging to a fixed self-concept blocks growth) and Charan's claim that each leadership passage requires learning to value new kinds of work. The coach should treat Doshi's choice as legitimate but test whether the user's reluctance is a stable preference or an untested discomfort.
- **Hewlett (executive presence).** Doshi's confidence dimension overlaps with presence; his incompetent-leader thread warns that charisma masks incompetence. Build presence, but anchor it in content.
- **Cagan.** Both say missing strategy causes most planning and alignment pain. Cagan barely treats internal visibility as work; Doshi says it becomes a large part of the senior job. Cagan's leverage runs through coaching; Doshi's LNO is personal leverage. They are complementary, but a user reading only Cagan will underestimate the optics load at VP scale.

## Limits and biases
- Evidence is personal experience at Google, Twitter, Stripe and Yahoo, plus advising: elite, high-talent environments. He bounds the execution-problems claim to high-performing, well-intentioned organizations himself.
- Much of the material is in aphoristic threads optimized for memorability, and some is available only through course marketing or third-party write-ups. Attribution through secondary summaries (D11 in part, some of D12) is less certain.
- He left operating roles and now sells courses on careers and product sense; his account of executive life is partly from the choice not to pursue the largest roles.
- Several frameworks (levels, LNO) are heuristics without published evidence beyond his observation.
- On identity: in the Deb Liu conversation he notes that presentation-weighted evaluation disadvantages quieter and introverted people; beyond that, I found no documented identity critiques of his work. The perception model is especially likely to encode bias, since confidence is read differently by gender and culture.
