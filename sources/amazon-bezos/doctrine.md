# Jeff Bezos / Amazon

## Why this source matters here
Bezos wrote the shareholder letters as founder-CEO while Amazon grew from a startup into one of the largest companies in the world. The letters state his operating philosophy in plain language. The Leadership Principles are used in Amazon's hiring and promotion. The mechanisms (door types, narratives, PR/FAQ, single-threaded ownership) can be carried elsewhere. At Amazon, though, they work because the top of the company enforces them. A PM who brings a six-pager into a slide-deck culture pays an adoption cost and must manage it. Amazon's culture is also publicly criticized as harsh, which limits how far its norms should be copied.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. Match the decision process to how reversible the decision is
- **Claim:** Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly so ("one-way doors"). They deserve a slow, careful, consultative process. Most decisions can be reversed ("two-way doors"). They should be made quickly by individuals with good judgment or by small groups. As organizations grow they tend to apply the heavy process to everything, which causes slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, too little experimentation, and less invention. Bezos notes the opposite mistake exists too (light process on irreversible decisions), but companies that make it tend to die before they get large.
- **Source:** 2015 letter, "Invention Machine".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach has the user classify each pending decision by door type. For a two-way door, the user should decide within their own scope, tell the boss rather than ask, and state how the decision would be reversed. Naming the reversal cost makes it easier for the boss to delegate. A user who escalates two-way doors is operating below level. A user who rushes a one-way door without consultation is taking a trust risk.
- **Boundary conditions:** Reversibility is usually a matter of degree, and the cost of undoing a decision rises over time (Duke's cost to quit). Customer-facing changes that affect trust, such as pricing, data use or AI model behavior, are often less reversible than they look. And the decision may not be the user's to make.

### D2. Decide with about 70% of the information you wish you had, when you can correct course
- **Claim:** Most decisions should be made with around 70% of the information you would like. Waiting for 90% usually means being slow. You need to be good at noticing and fixing bad decisions quickly. If you are, being wrong costs less than people expect, while being slow is always expensive.
- **Source:** 2016 letter, "High-Velocity Decision Making".
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user is waiting for more data, the coach asks three things: roughly how much of the needed information they have, what specific finding would change the decision, and how they would detect a mistake and reverse it. The recommendation then comes with a named monitoring signal.
- **Boundary conditions:** The rule depends on being able to correct course. Where feedback is slow or noisy (enterprise contracts, regulated or safety-relevant AI), deciding at 70% can be reckless. "70%" is a rule of thumb, not a calibrated number.

### D3. Have backbone, then disagree and commit, and it applies to the boss too
- **Claim:** Leaders are obliged to challenge decisions respectfully, even when it is uncomfortable or exhausting, and not to compromise just to keep the peace. Once the decision is made, they commit fully. Bezos says the boss should do the same, and gives an example of committing to an Amazon Studios show he doubted. When a disagreement comes from different objectives, more discussion will not settle it. Escalate quickly, because a decision reached by wearing the other side down is slow and drains energy.
- **Source:** Leadership Principle "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit"; 2016 letter.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks both halves. Before the decision: did the user state the disagreement clearly, with reasoning, ideally in writing? After it: did they commit visibly, without relitigating and without telling their team "leadership made us"? When the user is the decider, disagree-and-commit is something they can give their team by letting the team's call go ahead. When the user is stuck with a peer over objectives, the coach recommends a joint escalation early, with a shared framing of the disagreement.
- **Boundary conditions:** It is misused when it cuts off dissent before it has been heard. It does not cover unethical or unsafe decisions. Commitment does not rule out reopening the decision when material new information arrives.

### D4. For decisions that matter, write a narrative, not slides
- **Claim:** In 2004 Bezos banned PowerPoint at senior-team meetings. His reasoning was that a good narrative memo forces better thinking about what matters more than what and how ideas connect, while slides let people skip over ideas, flatten relative importance and ignore how ideas depend on each other. Meetings open with silent reading of the memo. In the 2017 letter he says weak memos usually come from underestimating the work involved, not from a writer who can't recognize good work. A great memo takes a week or more, with drafts, feedback and fresh-eyed editing.
- **Source:** 2004 S-team email; 2017 letter.
- **Changes coaching how:** For any major recommendation, the coach has the user write the narrative (one to six pages) before building slides. The review checks whether the memo shows relative importance and dependencies, and states what would change the recommendation. The coach also plans the calendar so the memo gets a week, not an evening.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a slide culture, or with a listener boss (Gabarro and Kotter), the memo can still be the thinking tool even if it is delivered verbally. Six pages is a maximum, not a target. Many executive asks need one paragraph with the answer first (Minto).

### D5. Work backwards from the customer, with the PR/FAQ as a gate before building
- **Claim:** Before building, write the launch press release and FAQ. The press release covers headline, subhead, summary, customer problem, solution, quotes and call to action. The external FAQ answers customers' questions. The internal FAQ answers the hard questions: market size, economics, feasibility, risks and dependencies. Documents are reviewed with silent reading and then discussion, and revised over several drafts. Most are never approved, and that is intended: it kills weak ideas cheaply.
- **Source:** Working Backwards; workingbackwards.com PR/FAQ page.
- **Changes coaching how:** For a new product or AI capability proposal, the coach asks for a PR/FAQ. It checks whether the internal FAQ answers "why us, why now, what must be true, what does it cost," or skips them. A proposal that starts from the solution or the technology gets sent back to state the customer problem first.
- **Boundary conditions:** It suits products with an identifiable customer. It fits platform, infrastructure and compliance work less well. A PR/FAQ aligns thinking. It is not evidence of demand, so discovery is still needed (Cagan).

### D6. High standards are domain-specific and teachable, and are mostly a problem of expected scope
- **Claim:** High standards are learned separately in each domain. They need two things: recognizing what good looks like there, and a realistic sense of how much effort good takes. Bezos's example is a handstand, which takes about six months to learn, not the two weeks people expect. Unrealistic, unspoken beliefs about scope destroy standards. You do not need to be able to do the work yourself to recognize and teach the standard.
- **Source:** 2017 letter.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user's work is judged not executive quality, the coach separates two possible gaps. A recognition gap means the user hasn't seen what good looks like, so the coach has them collect strong examples from that audience. A scope gap means the user knows what good looks like but under-budgets time, so the coach schedules drafts and review time. As a leader, the user should set explicit scope expectations for the artifacts their team produces.
- **Boundary conditions:** "Unreasonably high standards" can become cover for harshness. Standards need to be paired with realistic scope and time.

### D7. Earn trust by being self-critical and close to the details, and don't let proxies replace the goal
- **Claim:** The Earn Trust principle asks leaders to listen, speak candidly and be vocally self-critical even when it is awkward. The Dive Deep principle asks them to stay connected to details, audit frequently, and be skeptical when metrics and anecdotes disagree. The 2016 letter warns against letting a process or a metric stand in for the result it was meant to serve.
- **Source:** Leadership Principles; 2016 letter ("Resist Proxies").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach looks for the user volunteering their own misses in senior forums before anyone asks. It tests whether the user can explain a metric movement down to specific customer cases. When a metric and customer anecdotes disagree, the coach asks the user to investigate the anecdotes rather than dismiss them.
- **Boundary conditions:** Being self-critical in front of hostile evaluators needs care about framing (see Lencioni on vulnerability). Diving deep is not micromanaging the team's work.

### D8. Give one leader full-time ownership of an initiative, with few dependencies
- **Claim:** A single-threaded leader is the most senior person working full-time on one initiative, with the organizational skill and control of resources to deliver it, leading a team that can operate largely on its own. Reducing dependencies on other teams is a main design goal. The Ownership principle asks leaders to think long term, act for the whole company, and never say "that's not my job."
- **Source:** Working Backwards (single-threaded leadership); Leadership Principle "Ownership".
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user owns a strategic initiative part-time, alongside other work, the coach names that as a delivery risk and helps them propose dedicated ownership or a smaller scope. For scope expansion, the clearest marker of readiness is owning an initiative end to end, including its resources.
- **Boundary conditions:** This needs initiatives that can be separated. AI and platform work often depends heavily on shared infrastructure, and forcing separation can duplicate that infrastructure.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Decision triage** (for each pending decision): door type (one-way or two-way) → who decides (user, boss, higher) → information level (about 70%? what would change the decision?) → how to reverse it → the signal to monitor → communication (inform or ask).

**Narrative checklist:** recommendation stated plainly; context the reader lacks; options considered and rejected, with reasons; what matters most and why; dependencies; risks and what would change the recommendation; the ask. Budget a week, including one cold reread.

**PR/FAQ gate:** customer problem in one sentence; why existing alternatives fail; internal FAQ covering market size, economics, feasibility, risks, dependencies and a kill signal. If the internal FAQ is thin, the idea is not ready.

## Diagnostic questions
- Which of the user's pending decisions are two-way doors they are escalating anyway?
- For the last major recommendation, what would have changed it, and was that written down?
- After the user's last lost argument, what did their team hear?
- Is the user's weak artifact a recognition gap or a scope gap?
- Has the user volunteered a mistake in a senior forum this quarter?
- Is the user part-time on something the company calls strategic?

## Observable signals
- Positive: decides two-way doors and informs; writes before presenting; states "this would change my mind"; disagrees on the record, then carries the decision cleanly; escalates stalemates about objectives early and jointly; explains metrics down to customer cases; names their own misses first.
- Negative: brings every choice to the boss; slides with no narrative logic; revisits decisions after committing; lets disagreements drag on to avoid escalating; defends a metric even when the customer evidence contradicts it; treats "backbone" as permission to be combative.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Calling everything a two-way door to avoid consultation.
- Using "disagree and commit" to shut down dissent, or committing publicly while undermining in private.
- Mandating six-pagers without the reading time and discussion norms that make them work.
- Treating the PR/FAQ as marketing copy rather than a way to kill weak ideas.
- Importing "unreasonably high standards" as a license for harshness.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Gabarro and Kotter:** they say adapt to the boss's reader or listener preference, while Amazon imposes one institutional format. Follow the institution's norm where one exists and the individual's preference where it doesn't.
- **Duke:** they largely agree on speed and reversibility. Duke adds that the cost of quitting varies continuously, and suggests stacking cheap decisions before an irreversible one. Duke's question "what information would change my mind?" makes the 70% rule usable.
- **Goldsmith:** "Are Right, A Lot" and "Have Backbone" can reward winning too much. Backbone is justified on consequential calls and costly on small ones.
- **Cagan:** a PR/FAQ is a document. Cagan would require evidence from prototypes and customers. Use the PR/FAQ for alignment and a first kill decision, and discovery for proof.
- **Horowitz:** a wartime CEO makes the calls personally, while Amazon pushes Type 2 decisions down. Centralize in an existential crisis; delegate otherwise.

## Limits and biases
The letters were written by a very successful founder-CEO looking back, and survivorship bias applies; Bezos acknowledges it in the 2015 footnote. The mechanisms rely on enforcement from the top and a strong writing culture. Press accounts, notably the New York Times in 2015, have described Amazon's workplace as harsh, and some principles can be used to justify that. Bezos's own examples from the letters are illustrations, not evidence. Amazon's principles have been revised over time (two were added in 2021).
