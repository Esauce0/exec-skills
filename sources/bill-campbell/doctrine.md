# Bill Campbell

## Why this source matters here
Campbell (1940-2016) went from a losing record as Columbia's football coach to Apple sales and marketing, CEO of Claris and GO Corp, CEO then chairman of Intuit, a long Apple board seat, and unpaid coach to Steve Jobs, Eric Schmidt, Larry Page, Ben Horowitz and dozens of others. From 2001 he sat in Google's weekly executive meeting, and Schmidt credits him with shaping Google's organizational structure, including a product management group that did not step on engineering. His doctrine is a view from inside the room where executives decide who gets scope: what they notice, what disqualifies someone, and how they want disagreement brought to them. Transfer caution: almost everything recorded about him comes from people he coached, it concerns CEO-level teams at outlier companies, and his standing (board seats, founder friendships, no salary, no agenda) let him act candidly without the political exposure a PM carries.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Trust is the precondition for everything else, and it is built from specific behaviors
- **Claim:** Campbell treated trust as the first thing to establish in any working relationship; the book breaks it into keeping your word, loyalty, integrity, and discretion. Coaching and candor only worked inside that envelope. Fortune reports he knew for a year that a key Google employee was seriously ill and did not tell the CEO, because it was the employee's confidence; Schmidt considered that the right call.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach, trust chapter ("Build an envelope of trust"); Fortune 2008.
- **Changes coaching how:** Before the coach helps the user ask for more scope, it audits trust with that executive component by component: commitments kept, what the user says about the executive when the executive is absent, whether any status report has been shaded, and whether the user has kept private remarks private. PMs commonly spend trust without noticing by forwarding an executive's offhand comment to their team to create urgency. Repair trust gaps before asking for scope; a scope request on a thin trust base gets deferred with vague reasons.
- **Boundary conditions:** Trust compounds only across repeated interactions. With a short-tenure executive or in a reorg-heavy company, the relationship may end before it pays out. Loyalty to one executive becomes a liability if that executive falls (see Pfeffer tension).

### D2. Executives screen for a small set of traits, and coachability comes first
- **Claim:** Campbell only coached the coachable: people who were honest (including with themselves), humble, willing to persevere and work hard, and constantly open to learning. For people he would back or hire he looked for intelligence in the sense of learning fast and making "far analogies" across domains, hard work, integrity, grit, empathy, and a team-first attitude, and he watched whether people celebrated others' success. Character could veto brilliance: Schmidt recalled him rejecting a candidate with a flat judgment that something was wrong with the man's character.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach ("Only coach the coachable," "Pick the right players"); Fortune 2008.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach treats coachability as the user's first test with any new senior leader. Evidence: the user visibly acts on feedback within weeks and reports back that they did. The coach flags defensiveness in the user's own retelling of feedback ("they didn't have the context") as the signal an executive will read. On the intelligence trait, at executive level it shows as synthesis across functions rather than depth in one; coach the user to bring analogies from finance, operations, or go-to-market into product conversations.
- **Boundary conditions:** This is a filter for who gets invested in, not a promotion rubric; results still decide scope. Unstructured character judgments by a trusted insider are also where bias hides.

### D3. Work the team, then the problem
- **Claim:** When a problem surfaced, Campbell first checked whether the right people were on it and working well together, then turned to the problem. His own diagnostic split an issue into how much is people, how much technology, how much process, and he assessed a leader by watching how they actually ran 1:1s and staff meetings.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach; Schmidt on Tim Ferriss #367; Kleiner Perkins piece.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user brings a stuck initiative, the coach asks team questions before solution questions: who owns it, is that the right owner, which two leads are not talking, does the meeting cadence produce decisions? Upward, executives trust the person who arrives with a people diagnosis ("the blocker is disputed ownership between data platform and my team; here is what I have done about it") and not only a technical one.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a genuine technical or market crisis the problem can dominate; "working the team" can become a way to avoid a hard technical call.

### D4. Peer relationships are a formal performance dimension
- **Claim:** The review system Campbell taught, and many of his coachees adopted, weighted four areas: performance against the job's requirements, relationships with peers, management and leadership of one's own people, and innovation or best practices. Fortune summarizes the rule as: weak in any of these means not good. Horowitz's gloss: you can hit your goals and still be so difficult that the people depending on you miss theirs. Campbell also paired peers on projects and decisions to build cross-silo relationships deliberately.
- **Source:** Fortune 2008 (four areas; Horowitz); Trillion Dollar Coach (1:1 framework; "Pair people").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach scores the user on all four and asks specifically what the user's peers (other PMs, engineering, design, data, GTM leads) would say if the user's boss asked them. A user strong on delivery and weak on peers will read "not ready" from above while experiencing it as politics. Recommend a standing peer 1:1 cadence and at least one co-owned win per half with a peer the boss respects.
- **Boundary conditions:** The research brief mentioned a "top two" peer-relationship practice; I could not verify a Campbell practice by that name. Verified: peers as one of four 1:1 dimensions, and the "pair people" principle. Secondary summaries describe a "rule of two" (send the two people closest to a disagreement off to resolve it together); treat that label as attribution uncertain.

### D5. 1:1s get structure and preparation
- **Claim:** Campbell expected 1:1s to be prepared: a short list of one-word topics in priority order ("five words on a whiteboard"), compared with the other person's list, opening with a personal check-in, and cycling through the four review areas. Staff meetings opened with "trip reports," brief personal updates, before business.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach (1:1 framework; "Start with trip reports").
- **Changes coaching how:** For managing up, the user brings their own ranked five topics to every boss 1:1 and covers each of the four areas at least monthly, especially peers and innovation, which PMs tend to omit because they are not on the roadmap. For managerial leverage, the coach audits the user's 1:1s with reports against the same structure.
- **Boundary conditions:** With a boss who runs loose, conversational 1:1s, a rigid agenda can read as managing the boss. Keep the preparation and soften the format.

### D6. In decisions, get to the best idea, then someone makes the call
- **Claim:** Campbell disliked consensus and hated politics. He wanted every view surfaced, the leader ideally speaking last, and held that failing to decide can be as damaging as deciding wrongly, so the manager must break ties. Chris Sacca described the process Campbell built for Schmidt, Page, and Brin: voice dissent, lobby each other, hear everyone, decide, then present a united front; Sacca stressed it was not consensus-building. Campbell also pushed leaders to name the first principles of a situation before deciding. His own line to Fortune was that CEOs must make decisions and own them.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach ("The throne behind the round table," "Lead based on first principles"); Fortune 2008.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach separates voice from vote: the user argues fully before a decision and commits publicly after, never relitigating in hallways or in their team's hearing. That pattern is one of the fastest ways to earn executive trust. When the user is the decider, coach them to state the principles up front, hear all positions, and break the tie on a stated date. Consensus-seeking language in the user's updates ("still aligning stakeholders") is a below-level signal when the user holds the decision right.
- **Boundary conditions:** Depends on clear decision rights. In a matrix where the user lacks authority, "breaking ties" means escalating crisp options with a recommendation, not deciding.

### D7. Candor with care, and no gap between what you report and what is true
- **Claim:** Campbell was relentlessly honest, delivered negative feedback privately and quickly, and made his care evident at the same time. He would tell an executive plainly that a peer was not a fan, then help the two work through it. The book explicitly connects this to Kim Scott's Radical Candor; Scott was at Google in the same period.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach ("No gap between statements and fact"); Fortune 2008 (Sacca episode).
- **Changes coaching how:** Two applications. First, status: no gap between the status the user reports and the status the user knows; the first time an executive discovers shaded bad news, trust resets to zero. Second, politics: when a peer conflict exists, the Campbell move is to name it with the peer directly and propose resolving it together, not to route around them or lobby the boss.
- **Boundary conditions:** Campbell's bluntness (profanity, what one coachee called "management by insult") ran on decades of goodwill and unusual status. The style copied without the relationship reads as aggression.

### D8. Team first, with self-interest visibly subordinated
- **Claim:** Team-first people give things up for the larger cause and celebrate others' wins. Campbell steered executives toward the role that served the company over the one that served their pride, and insisted on winning right, with integrity. People who persistently put themselves first had to go, with a narrow exception for the "aberrant genius" whose value clearly exceeded the disruption and whose conduct stayed ethical.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach ("Team first," "Winning right," "Manage the aberrant genius").
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks for evidence the user has given up something visible for the team (headcount, credit for a launch, a favored roadmap item) and whether the executive saw it. It flags credit-seeking tells: first-person-singular narratives in executive updates, partner teams unnamed. Recommend naming partner contributions specifically in executive forums; it reads as confidence, not modesty.
- **Boundary conditions:** Team-first without visibility becomes invisibility (Hewlett, Pfeffer). Pair it with deliberate visibility of results the user led.

### D9. Your title makes you a manager; your people make you a leader
- **Claim:** Positional authority is thin. Leadership is conferred by people who choose to follow, and the manager's primary job is to help people become more effective and grow.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach, chapter 1 and "Bill's manifesto."
- **Changes coaching how:** For a PM leading without authority, this is the working condition. The coach looks for evidence that engineers, designers, and peers follow the user when they do not have to: people asking to join the user's work, partner leads defending the user's plan when the user is absent, strong people staying. For next-level readiness, ask what the user's team would say to the user's skip-level unprompted.
- **Boundary conditions:** In a turnaround or restructuring, directive authority matters and the user may need to use it.

### D10. Believe in people more than they believe in themselves
- **Claim:** Campbell pushed leaders toward bold bets and expressed belief in them ahead of their own confidence; the book frames him as an evangelist for courage.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach ("Be the evangelist for courage").
- **Changes coaching how:** This is the observable behavior of a sponsor. The coach helps the user identify which senior leader has expressed belief beyond the user's current evidence and what exactly that leader is betting on, so the user can deliver against the bet. As a manager, the user should do the same for reports and say so explicitly.
- **Boundary conditions:** Belief without candor is flattery; Campbell always paired the two.

### D11. Fill the gaps between people and put the biggest problem on the table first
- **Claim:** Campbell watched for tension and misunderstanding between executives and addressed it in side conversations. In meetings he pushed teams to surface the "elephant in the room" first and take the politics out of it, and he kept complaint sessions short before turning to solutions.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach ("Fill the gaps between people," "Solve the biggest problem").
- **Changes coaching how:** For meeting presence, coach the user to name the unspoken biggest issue early, framed as a shared problem rather than an accusation. Executive trust accrues to the person who makes a meeting more honest without making it more heated. Coach the user to act as connective tissue between leaders who are not talking.
- **Boundary conditions:** Naming the elephant requires standing. A junior person doing it cold in a large forum can look like grandstanding; pre-wire with the most affected leader.

### D12. Care for the whole person, including on the way out
- **Claim:** Campbell led the whole person, knowing families and lives outside work, and handled exits generously: people could not keep the job but could keep their respect. During Loudcloud's restructuring he warned Horowitz that once the deal was announced, employees would care only about where they stood; Horowitz cancelled his New York press trip, and Campbell told him to be there all day and help people carry their things to the car.
- **Source:** Trillion Dollar Coach ("Heads held high"); Fortune 2008 (Horowitz).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user must manage someone out, reorganize, or deliver bad news, the coach checks how the people affected will be treated, because everyone watching calibrates trust from it. Senior leaders watch how a rising leader handles the unpleasant parts of the job more closely than the pleasant ones.
- **Boundary conditions:** Personal care must not turn into delaying the decision (Scott's ruinous empathy).

## Frameworks in operational form

### Four-area mirror: how a Campbell-minded executive reads the user
| Area | What the executive looks at | Evidence the user should be able to show | Typical PM gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Performance vs. job requirements | Metrics the role is judged on; commitments met | Outcome metrics moved, dated commitments kept | Output reported instead of outcomes |
| Peer relationships | Whether peers make the user's work easier or harder | Peers who would volunteer praise; co-owned wins | Friction with eng/data/GTM leads treated as their problem |
| Management/leadership | Whether the user's people grow, stay, and follow | Retention, promotions of reports, people seeking to join | Talent work deferred behind roadmap work |
| Innovation/best practices | Whether the user improves how work gets done | A practice the user introduced that others adopted | "We shipped" with no change in method |
Run it quarterly. Any area rated weak on evidence is the coaching priority regardless of the user's own ranking.

### Trust audit for one executive relationship
For each key executive, record evidence (not feelings) for:
1. Word: every commitment made to them in the last quarter, kept or renegotiated before it was missed?
2. Loyalty: what has the user said about this executive to others when they were absent?
3. Integrity: any update where the reported status was rosier than the known status?
4. Discretion: has the user repeated anything the executive said privately?
5. Candor: has the user told them something unwelcome, privately, with care?
6. Team-first: has the executive seen the user give something up for the larger goal?
Any "no" or "unknown" becomes a concrete action before the next scope conversation.

### Five-word boss 1:1
1. Before the meeting, write up to five one-word topics in priority order.
2. Across a month, cover all four review areas, not only delivery.
3. Open with a short personal check-in; do not skip it to save time.
4. Ask for the boss's list; if the lists conflict, take the boss's first item first.
5. Close with what the user will do and by when; open the next 1:1 by reporting it (this is how coachability becomes visible).

### Voice-then-commit protocol for decisions
- Before: send a short pre-read that names the first principles the user thinks should govern the decision.
- During: state dissent once, clearly, with evidence; ask what would change the decider's mind.
- After: commit publicly; describe the decision to the user's team as the user's own; never relitigate outside the room.
- If the user decides: hear every position, speak last, name the principle that broke the tie, set a date.

### Coachability evidence check
- Last three pieces of critical feedback the user received: what changed, how fast, and did the giver see the change?
- Does the user's retelling of feedback contain justification before acknowledgment?
- Has the user asked for feedback from anyone senior without being prompted in the last month?

## Diagnostic questions
- Which executive currently trusts the user most, and what specifically did the user do to earn it?
- Has the user ever reported a status rosier than they knew it to be? Did the executive find out?
- What would the user's three most important peers say about them to the user's boss?
- Where has the user argued against a decision and then fully committed? Where have they relitigated?
- Is the current stuck problem a people problem, a technology problem, or a process problem, and what does the user's answer leave out?
- Which two leaders around the user's work are not talking, and has the user tried to close that gap?
- When did the user last give up something visible for the larger team, and who saw it?
- Who has expressed belief in the user ahead of the evidence? What are they betting the user can do?
- How does the user run their own 1:1s and staff meetings? Would Campbell's inspection of them pass?
- What private confidences has the user been given by executives, and have all of them been kept?
- When the user delivered bad news to their team last, how were the affected people treated?

## Observable signals
Positive (operating at the next level, per this source):
- Arrives at boss 1:1s with a ranked agenda that includes peer and people topics, not only delivery.
- Reports bad news early, in the same terms they would use privately.
- Names partner teams' contributions specifically in executive updates.
- Argues hard in the decision meeting; afterwards, is indistinguishable from the decision's author.
- Peers seek the user out to resolve cross-team conflicts.
- Acts on feedback within weeks and tells the giver what changed.
- Surfaces the uncomfortable topic early in a meeting and keeps the temperature down.
- Reports ask to stay on the user's team; strong people ask to join.

Negative (current-level or below):
- Executive updates in first person singular; partners unnamed.
- "Still aligning stakeholders" on a decision the user owns.
- Private disagreement with a decision voiced to the team after it was made.
- Routing around a conflicting peer to the boss instead of addressing it with the peer.
- Forwarding an executive's private remark to create urgency.
- 1:1s with no agenda, or agendas that are only status.
- Defensive explanation as the first response to feedback.
- Treating peer friction as "politics" rather than as a performance dimension.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Imitating the persona: profanity, bear hugs, blunt verdicts. The style was earned over decades; borrowed, it reads as aggression or theater.
- Reading "hate politics" as "ignore power." Campbell operated from immense structural power (boards, founders' trust, no dependence on anyone's approval). A PM who ignores power dynamics is not being Campbell; they are being naive.
- Loyalty as unconditional allegiance to a boss. Campbell's loyalty sat alongside candor and integrity; loyalty that covers for a boss's bad judgment breaks his integrity principle.
- Using "work the team first" to avoid owning the technical or product call.
- Treating "no consensus" as permission to override. The leader still owes everyone a full hearing and a stated reason.
- Coaches misusing it: treating the trait list as a fixed personality verdict instead of a set of behaviors the user can show; ignoring the invisibility cost of team-first behavior.
- Assuming the user has a Campbell. Most PMs do not have a trusted outside arbiter smoothing executive conflict; the coach should not recommend moves that only work when one exists.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Pfeffer (power realism):** Pfeffer argues performance and likability are overrated, that self-promotion and building power matter more than being trusted by one's boss, and that loyalty to a boss is a weak bet. Campbell argues trust, integrity, and team-first behavior are what executives reward. Campbell is more right where leadership is stable, performance is legible, and senior leaders actively coach (his own environments). Pfeffer is more right in politicized organizations, during leadership turnover, and where credit is contested. The coach should diagnose which environment the user is in before choosing, and note that Campbell himself held great structural power.
- **McCord (keeper test):** McCord's test keeps people if the business needs them for what comes next, regardless of past contribution; Campbell's loyalty attached to people and survived their departures. They agree on honesty and dignity at exit (her generous severance, his "heads held high"). McCord's lens is more predictive of how a fast-changing company will actually treat the user; Campbell's describes what earns trust from the individual executive. The user needs both: be trustworthy to the person and indispensable to the future plan.
- **Scott (candor):** Largely convergent; the Trillion Dollar Coach authors cite Radical Candor. The tension is style: Scott warns that directness without demonstrated care is obnoxious aggression regardless of intent, which is what Campbell's blunt manner becomes in someone without his track record.
- **Bezos/Amazon:** "Disagree and commit" matches Campbell's voice-then-commit. Amazon routes disagreement through written narratives; Campbell's mode was verbal and relational. The user should learn which mode their executives trust.
- **Hewlett (sponsorship):** Campbell's team-first ethic can leave the user under-credited; Hewlett argues sponsorship must be actively secured and paid back with visible performance. Combine: team-first behavior, deliberately made visible to the sponsor.
- **Lencioni:** Convergent on trust as the base of a team and on the executive's "first team" being their peers, which matches Campbell's peer dimension.
- **Grove:** Grove's output-per-manager lens and task-relevant maturity are more mechanical than Campbell's person-first coaching; Grove is more useful for designing the user's operating cadence, Campbell for reading how executives judge the user.

## Limits and biases
- Evidence quality: the record is hagiographic, written largely by people he coached and who owe him their careers. There is no systematic evidence that his practices, rather than the companies' extraordinary markets, drove outcomes.
- Survivorship: the book tells the Google and Apple stories. GO Corp failed under him; the lessons drawn there are about treating people well in failure, which is a different claim.
- Governance critique: Fortune (2008) questioned whether his confidential, cross-company relationships created a clubby opacity toward shareholders; he sat on Apple's board while advising Google's CEO. Court filings in the no-poach antitrust litigation of the early 2010s (Apple, Google, Intuit, and others) discussed his cross-company role (by reference; not re-verified this session). The trust networks he built can also exclude and collude.
- Access: his trust was built in bars, on football fields, and in long-standing male friendships. People outside those networks, including many women and people from non-traditional backgrounds, cannot build trust through the same channels; the book's "get women at the table" principle acknowledges the problem without solving it.
- Level transfer: he coached CEOs and founders. A PM earning executive trust is on the other side of the relationship, with less power and more exposure; the coach must translate his manager doctrine into what the user should show upward.
- Era: 1980s to 2016, in-person, pre-remote Silicon Valley.
