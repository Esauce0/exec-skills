# Frank Slootman

## Why this source matters here
Slootman was CEO of Data Domain (acquired by EMC in 2009 after a bidding contest with NetApp), ServiceNow (2011-2017, through its IPO) and Snowflake (2019 to February 2024, through its 2020 IPO). He stepped down as Snowflake CEO in February 2024 and remained chairman. Each time he arrived as a hired CEO with a board mandate to scale a company that had product traction. His doctrine is a playbook for injecting standards, urgency and focus from the top and changing the people who resist. For an aspiring product executive, it gives a useful bar: what hard-driving CEOs expect of the executives around them (ownership, candor about metrics, strategy owned by operators, no "good enough"). It is dangerous as a style to imitate without authority. The coach should use him to sharpen standards and candor, and check the user's mandate before recommending any of his interpersonal moves.

## Operational doctrine

### D1. Fix execution before touching strategy: standards, alignment, focus, pace, then strategy
- **Claim:** His sequence is: raise standards, align people, sharpen (narrow) focus, pick up the pace, and only then transform strategy. Execution comes first because strategy cannot be transformed on top of poor execution. Great execution can carry an average strategy a long way. Poor execution sinks a great one.
- **Source:** LEADERS interview (2022); Amp It Up; Tape Sucks (execution over strategy).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user proposes a new strategy for a struggling area, the coach first asks whether current execution is sound: are standards, focus and pace the problem? Leaders often read a strategy pitch from someone whose area misses its commitments as avoiding the real problem. (enterprise-thinking, decision-making)
- **Boundary conditions:** Some situations are strategy failures (a Grove-style inflection point, a product without differentiation). Slootman himself warns against brute-forcing growth before the underlying conditions exist (D12). Executing harder on a dead strategy speeds the decline (see Tensions: Rumelt, Grove).

### D2. Reject "good enough"; the bar is work people are excited about
- **Claim:** Organizations drift into box-checking where good enough becomes the standard, and that drains them. Raising the bar takes little more mental energy. Ask whether people are excited about what they are delivering, and send work back until they are. He borrows Steve Jobs's "insanely great" as the standard.
- **Source:** LEADERS interview (2022); "Amp It Up!" (2018).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user to rate their last three deliverables (a spec, a launch, an exec update) against "would I be proud to show this to the CEO unannounced?" It pushes the user to set explicit quality bars for their area, for AI features in particular (eval thresholds, failure-mode reviews), and to send work back rather than shipping and apologizing. Raising the bar on your own work needs no authority. (executive-posture, managerial-leverage)
- **Boundary conditions:** High standards applied everywhere become perfectionism and slow learning in discovery work. Decide where "insanely great" matters (customer-facing quality, trust, executive narrative) and where fast and rough is correct (experiments, internal prototypes).

### D3. Compress timelines by questioning them
- **Claim:** Left alone, organizations slow down. Most timelines are set for comfort rather than purpose, and people often cannot say why a date is what it is. Leaders set the tempo by challenging timelines at every turn, asking why a date cannot be sooner. A faster pace forces people to rethink how the work is done.
- **Source:** LEADERS interview (2022); "Amp It Up!" (2018).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user, for each major date in their plan, what the date is anchored to (a dependency, a real risk, a habit). Tempo is a visible executive signal: people who move the cycle time of their area earn scope. The coach steers the user toward compressing timelines they own (decision latency, review turnaround, their own response times) before challenging other teams' dates. (managerial-leverage, executive-posture)
- **Boundary conditions:** Pace compression without authority or trust reads as pushiness and can damage relationships with engineering. In safety-, compliance- or model-risk-heavy work, some timelines exist for reasons the challenger has not understood. Ask the question to learn, not as a demand.

### D4. Narrow the focus: sequence priorities, take work off people's plates
- **Claim:** Most organizations spread themselves too thin across too many initiatives. Narrow the plane of attack and do things in sequence rather than in parallel. Take things off people's plates instead of adding. He has said that not choosing is the worst option because it compromises everything. At ServiceNow he paid executives on a single metric, and he is openly skeptical of balanced scorecards.
- **Source:** LEADERS interview (2022); "Amp It Up!" (2018); Knowledge Project show page; Tape Sucks (via summary).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks the user to name the one thing their area must accomplish this half, and what they have stopped to make room for it. The most senior-sounding thing a PM can bring to a planning meeting is often a proposed stop list with its cost. The coach checks that the user's updates rank priorities rather than list them. (decision-making, executive-communication, enterprise-thinking)
- **Boundary conditions:** A single metric invites gaming and hides damage (see Grove's paired indicators). Sequencing works when work can wait. Platform or regulatory obligations sometimes cannot be parked.

### D5. Alignment does not happen by osmosis; use the mission and the incentives
- **Claim:** Misalignment is everywhere because people do not align on their own. Leaders align them through a clear, compelling, credible mission that pulls on every meeting and message all the time, and through incentives tied to the same few measures. Without it, organizations suffer mission fog, mission creep and chasing shiny objects.
- **Source:** LEADERS interview (2022); "Being Mission-Driven"; Amp It Up (alignment via compensation).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach asks whether the user can state their area's mission in one sentence that would settle a real trade-off, and whether their team's goals and the partner teams' goals point the same way. Where incentives conflict (sales paid on bookings, product on adoption, support on handle time), the user should name the conflict to leadership. That is enterprise-thinking the executive layer values. (enterprise-thinking, executive-communication)
- **Boundary conditions:** A PM rarely controls compensation. Mission statements without resource decisions behind them are slogans.

### D6. Report what is broken; diagnose before prescribing; no happy talk
- **Claim:** Slootman wants the problem in front of leaders, not a sanitized story. Colleagues recount that at his first Snowflake board meeting he set the agenda around what was broken and how it would be fixed. He treats good news as mostly already known. His Data Domain values defined integrity as never knowingly saying anything but the unvarnished truth, including no lies of omission (as summarized from Tape Sucks). He criticizes presentations that jump to solutions without diagnosing the problem (as summarized from Amp It Up).
- **Source:** Sequoia spotlight; Tape Sucks and Amp It Up (via secondary summaries); LEADERS interview (his stated intent to share experience bluntly).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach restructures the user's executive and board-facing updates to lead with the most important problem, its cause, the fix, and what the user needs. Metrics are shown against plan with misses named first. It flags "green-washing" (status colors that hide risk) and solution decks without a diagnosis section. For hard-driving executives, candor about bad news is the fastest route to trust. (executive-communication, board-communication, executive-trust)
- **Boundary conditions:** Some cultures punish the messenger. The user should frame bad news with ownership and a plan, and should tell their own manager before any broader audience. Leading with problems every time can also read as negativity. Name what is working in a line and move on.

### D7. Change the leaders to change the culture, and do not wait when in doubt
- **Claim:** Culture is the persistent pattern of behavior that consequences produce. People learn what matters from what is rewarded and what is removed. Slootman moves fast on leaders who do not fit. At Snowflake he quickly consolidated the leadership team, and his rule (as summarized from Amp It Up) is that doubt about a leader is itself the answer. He treats behavior and performance differently: people get time to improve performance, but not to change behavior, because behavior is a choice (Knowledge Project). He runs peer calibration sessions where executives test each other's judgments about people.
- **Source:** Amp It Up (via summaries); Knowledge Project show page; Cove Street interview notes.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach uses this lens to explain what the user's leadership sees, not to prescribe firing. When leaders are replaced above or around the user, the new leader is changing the culture, and the user should work out quickly which behaviors are now rewarded. When the user takes on a team, the coach asks how long they have tolerated a behavior problem and what that tolerance is teaching everyone else. (talent, organizational-politics, attribution-analysis)
- **Boundary conditions:** Fast removal suits a CEO with a board mandate in a stall. For a new Director inheriting a team, acting on doubt too early risks losing institutional knowledge and trust, and legal and HR processes apply. Duke and Larson would ask for more evidence (see Tensions).

### D8. Be a driver, not a passenger
- **Claim:** Drivers take ownership, argue clear positions, set high standards for themselves and others, and make things happen. Passengers ride along, avoid positions to stay safe, and diagnose problems without solving them. He treats passengers as dead weight in a high-intensity culture.
- **Source:** Amp It Up (via summaries); "Amp It Up!" (2018).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks the user's behavior in senior meetings. Do they take a position with a recommendation, or offer balanced analysis and wait? Do they propose fixes or only name problems? Executives across the roster read driver behavior as readiness. Slootman makes the passenger behaviors unusually concrete. (executive-posture, meeting-presence, next-level-readiness)
- **Boundary conditions:** Driver behavior without the relationship base or context can look like overreach, especially for someone new to a forum. The labels can be used to dismiss careful dissenters as passengers. In Grove's model, dissent before a decision is the driver's job.

### D9. Operators own strategy; do not outsource thinking to consultants or staff
- **Claim:** The CEO must act as chief strategist, and the operators running each business unit must also be the strategists for their business. If an executive cannot be trusted to set strategy for their area, consultants will not fix that. Strategy set by people who do not live with its consequences is disconnected from reality.
- **Source:** Amp It Up (via summaries).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach expects the user to own the strategy for their product area, written by them, not handed down or delegated to a strategy team or consultants. A PM who waits for strategy to arrive from above is operating below Director level. The coach asks, "What is your strategy for this area, in one page, and who would disagree with it?" (enterprise-thinking, product-leadership)
- **Boundary conditions:** In large companies, product strategy nests inside company and portfolio strategy the user does not set. Owning strategy for your area means proposing and defending it within those constraints, not declaring it.

### D10. Work backward from the end state instead of adding increments
- **Claim:** Incrementalism builds cautiously on what exists, because risk-taking is punished more than stagnation. Companies decline slowly this way. Instead, envision the ideal end state, ask what you would build on a clean sheet, and work backward. Measuring progress by looking at the wake does not steer the ship.
- **Source:** "The War Against Incrementalism"; Amp It Up (via summaries); Cove Street interview notes.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user's roadmap is a list of increments on the current product, the coach asks for the end-state picture first. For AI products, the question is what this workflow looks like when models are much more capable, and which current investments become waste. (enterprise-thinking, ai-product-leadership)
- **Boundary conditions:** Clean-sheet thinking without delivery produces decks, not results. In regulated or high-trust products, increments may be required for good reasons. Pair the end state with near-term, measurable steps.

### D11. Approval-seeking makes marginal leaders; the job involves confrontation
- **Claim:** Leaders need conviction and courage. Trying to please and appease produces marginal leadership, and popularity comes from winning, not from being liked. He has described the CEO job as intensely confrontational, against human nature, and has said leaders cannot be conflict-avoidant. He does not manage for employee approval scores.
- **Source:** LEADERS interview (2022); Fortune coverage of his step-down (2024); Cove Street interview notes.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach watches for the user's conflict avoidance: softening a no, delaying a hard message to a peer, letting a weak plan through to keep the peace. It names the cost in executive terms. It then routes the fix through method (Hughes Johnson's joint unblocking doc, Grove's free discussion, a direct conversation in private) rather than through a louder style. (executive-posture, organizational-politics)
- **Boundary conditions:** He speaks as a CEO whose position protects his bluntness. An aspiring leader without that authority relies on peers and sponsors who will not tolerate the same style. Blunt styles are also judged differently depending on who uses them (see Limits).

### D12. Intensity cannot replace preconditions: you cannot brute-force a market
- **Claim:** Amping up works on top of a differentiated product and a repeatable model. Premature scaling fails. He warns against adding salespeople before lead generation, productivity metrics and a repeatable sales process exist. He admits that in hindsight he could have pushed for more ambitious goals, and that he made hiring mistakes in areas he knew less well.
- **Source:** Amp It Up (via summaries).
- **Changes coaching how:** Before recommending urgency, the coach asks whether the conditions exist: is there real product-market fit, a working funnel, a model that repeats? For AI products, is the capability reliable enough that pushing adoption will not produce a trust incident? Pushing harder on a product that does not work well enough is a common way ambitious PMs lose credibility. (decision-making, enterprise-thinking)
- **Boundary conditions:** The preconditions are judgment calls, and waiting for certainty is its own failure. Slootman's later regret was being too conservative.

### D13. Go direct: talk to whoever can help, regardless of hierarchy
- **Claim:** Everyone may contact anyone in the company about anything, without asking permission through reporting lines. Managers who shield their teams or require permission let silos harden into fiefdoms. The CEO models it by answering employee email directly.
- **Source:** Amp It Up (via summaries); Cove Street interview notes.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach encourages the user to go straight to the person who can unblock something (a counterpart two levels over, a finance partner, a model-platform lead) rather than routing through managers. It also asks the user to tell their manager afterward so going direct does not look like going around them. (organizational-politics, managing-up)
- **Boundary conditions:** Only safe where the culture endorses it. In hierarchical organizations, going direct to a senior person without warning your manager can cost more trust than the time saved.

## Frameworks in operational form

### Authority check (run before recommending any Slootman move)
| Move | Needs authority over | Can the user do it now? | Safe version for a PM without the mandate |
| Raise standards | Own work; team output | Yes, for own work and artifacts | Publish explicit quality bars; send own team's work back |
| Compress timelines | The team's plan | Partly | Cut own decision and review latency; ask what other teams' dates are anchored to, to learn |
| Narrow focus | Roadmap and resources | Partly | Propose a stop list with costs to the decision owner |
| Align incentives | Goals and pay | Rarely | Name misaligned incentives across teams in writing to leadership |
| Change leaders | People decisions | No | Observe which behaviors new leaders reward; address behavior issues on own team through HR process |
| Transform strategy | Direction of the area | Partly | Write an end-state strategy for own area and get it ratified |
- If the user lacks the authority, recommend the safe version. Doing the full move without authority is executive theater.

### Amp-It-Up diagnostic for the user's area (score 1-5, with evidence)
- Standards: would the team show its last three deliverables to the CEO unannounced?
- Alignment: do partner teams' goals and incentives point the same way? One sentence of mission that settles trade-offs?
- Focus: how many parallel priorities? What was stopped this quarter?
- Pace: median time from question to decision; from decision to ship; which dates are anchored to nothing?
- Strategy: is there a written end state that the current roadmap moves toward?
- Preconditions: is there evidence of product-market fit, repeatability, and reliability?
- The lowest score with preconditions met is where to push first.

### Candor-first update format (executive or board facing)
1. The most important problem, stated plainly, with the metric against plan.
2. Diagnosis: cause, with evidence. What we do not yet know.
3. The fix: actions, owners, dates, and the leading indicator that will show it is working.
4. What we need from you (decision, resources, air cover).
5. What is working (one or two lines).
- Test: could a skeptical executive find a material problem in the appendix that is not on page one? If so, rewrite.

### Driver behavior checklist (observe in the next three senior meetings)
- Stated a position with a recommendation before being asked.
- Brought a proposed fix, not only a diagnosis.
- Named a trade-off and who pays for it.
- Took an action item with a date and closed it.
- Challenged a timeline or assumption with a question about what it is anchored to.

### Stop list
For each current initiative: what it contributes to the one priority, what happens if it is paused for a quarter, who objects, and what a pause frees up. Bring the top three pauses to the decision owner with a recommendation.

## Diagnostic questions
- Is the area's problem execution or strategy? What evidence separates the two?
- Which of your last three deliverables were "good enough" rather than excellent, and did anyone send them back?
- What is each major date in your plan anchored to?
- What is the one thing your area must do this half, and what did you stop to make room for it?
- Which incentives across your partner teams pull against your goal? Have you named it to leadership?
- Does your last executive update lead with the most important problem? Where is the diagnosis?
- In your last three senior meetings, where did you take a position, and where did you wait?
- What is your one-page strategy for your area, and who would disagree with it?
- Which increments on your roadmap would a clean-sheet design skip?
- What hard message are you delaying, and what is the delay costing?
- For any urgency you want to create: do you own the team, the resources, the plan? If not, what is the safe version?
- Are the preconditions for pushing harder actually met?

## Observable signals
Positive (the executive behaviors Slootman expects):
- Updates lead with problems and misses, then diagnosis, fix and ask.
- The user shortens cycle times in their own scope and can show the before and after.
- The user proposes stop lists and a single priority, and accepts the cost.
- In meetings, the user takes positions and brings fixes.
- The user writes and defends the strategy for their area rather than waiting for one.
- Quality bars are explicit, and work gets sent back before it reaches executives.
- The user contacts the right person across the org directly and keeps their manager informed.

Negative (passenger or theater behaviors):
- Balanced analysis with no recommendation. Diagnosing without proposing.
- Status reports that are uniformly green until a sudden red.
- Accepting timelines without asking what anchors them, or, the reverse, demanding other teams move faster with no authority or context.
- Many parallel priorities with nothing stopped.
- Borrowing Slootman's language ("amp it up", "drivers vs. passengers", "popularity is optional") without changing any results.
- Bluntness used on peers and reports while deferring upward.
- Waiting for strategy from above.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Executive theater: imitating intensity, urgency and bluntness without the authority, results or relationships that make them work. Senior leaders see it quickly and read it as immaturity.
- Using "popularity is optional" to excuse damaging relationships the user depends on, especially with engineering and peer PMs. Slootman had a board mandate. The user needs a coalition.
- Compressing other teams' timelines by pressure rather than by removing their constraints.
- Applying his act-on-doubt rule to people decisions as a new manager, without evidence, process, or understanding what the previous leader tolerated and why.
- Treating single-metric focus as permission to ignore counter-metrics such as quality, safety and trust, which is especially risky in AI products.
- Leading every update with problems in a way that reads as doom rather than ownership.
- Using the drivers-and-passengers label to dismiss careful dissent or people with caregiving constraints.
- Assuming execution intensity will save a product without differentiation or reliability.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Grove (urgency vs. process discipline):** Grove gets output from calibrated process (TRM-matched supervision, meeting disciplines, paired indicators, a decision cycle that includes revisiting). Slootman gets it from leader-set standards and tempo, and is skeptical of balanced measurement. Grove is more right in large, interdependent organizations where coordination failures are costly and people need development. Slootman is more right when an organization has become comfortable and needs a jolt. They share paranoia about competitors and contempt for reporting that hides reality.
- **Hughes Johnson (leader-driven intensity vs. systems others run):** She scales through explicit operating systems, documented decision rights and joint escalation, so that many people can decide without the leader. Slootman scales through the leader's direct push and fast changes of people. Hers holds up better after the intense leader leaves. His moves faster from a stall. Both demand candid business reviews and clear priorities.
- **Campbell and Scott (care vs. popularity optional):** Campbell's coaching and Scott's Radical Candor both pair challenge with personal care. Slootman's public posture puts results ahead of being liked and says little about care. When imitated without authority it tends toward what Scott calls obnoxious aggression. The coach should take Slootman's standards and Scott's delivery.
- **Rumelt:** Both insist on diagnosis before action. Rumelt treats strategy as central (bad strategy cannot be rescued by execution). Slootman ranks execution first and strategy as a multiplier. Rumelt is more right when the diagnosis shows a structural problem. Slootman is more right when the strategy is sound and execution is soft.
- **Duke and Larson:** Slootman's rule of acting on doubt about people and his fast pace favor speed. Duke would ask for decision hygiene (separating decision quality from outcome, stating what would change your mind). Larson emphasizes learning an organization before changing it and sustainable pace for engineering. Slootman's approach fits a CEO turnaround. Theirs fits a new leader inheriting a functioning team.
- **Watkins:** Watkins's STARS situations (start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success) explain where Slootman's playbook fits: turnaround and accelerated growth. In sustaining-success situations, his moves can destroy what works.
- **Horowitz:** Slootman is a textbook "wartime" CEO in Horowitz's terms. Horowitz's point that wartime tactics fail in peacetime applies directly.
- **Cagan:** Slootman's top-down focus and single-metric incentives sit uneasily with Cagan's empowered product teams owning outcomes. They agree on outcomes over output and on the importance of product differentiation.
- **Bezos/Amazon:** Amazon's "insist on the highest standards" and single-threaded ownership match Slootman's standards and focus. Amazon puts more mechanism around decisions (two-way doors, disagree and commit, written narratives). Slootman relies more on the leader's personal tempo.
- **Pfeffer:** Slootman's career supports Pfeffer's argument that being liked is overrated in getting power. Pfeffer would add that the user needs allies and a power base before behaving like someone who already has power.
- **Goldsmith:** For a rising leader, intensity can become a "what got you here" habit: winning every argument, adding pressure to everything. Goldsmith would coach the user to drop those habits at the next level.
- **Lencioni:** Both value open conflict. Lencioni grounds productive conflict in trust among peers. Slootman's insistence on seeking confrontation assumes the authority to absorb the relationship cost.

## Limits and biases
- Context: three hired-CEO roles at enterprise software companies with product traction, sales-led motions and board mandates for growth. Transfer to a PM in a mid-size company is limited to standards, candor, focus and strategy ownership. Most interpersonal tactics do not transfer without authority.
- Survivorship: Data Domain, ServiceNow and Snowflake are told as successes by the person who ran them. The Snowflake chapter ended with growth slowing, a sharp share drop when his step-down was announced, and his stated view that the AI era needed a technologist CEO. That suggests the playbook's edge narrows when the problem shifts from go-to-market to product and technology.
- Style: widely described as blunt to the point of brusque, and by admirers as confrontational. Accounts of what this costs people come mostly from outsiders and critics rather than from his own writing.
- Identity: in 2021 he said diversity goals should not override merit, drew criticism, and apologized, saying he did not mean the two were mutually exclusive. Research on executive presence (Hewlett) shows that bluntness and "popularity is optional" are rewarded in some leaders and penalized in others. That limits how safely the user can adopt the style, depending on who they are and where they work.
- Evidence: memoir and manifesto rather than research. Several items here are known only through secondary summaries of his books (flagged in Source lines). The Cove Street notes are from an unattributed interview.
- Era: formed in the 2000s and 2010s, in cheap-capital hypergrowth and enterprise-software expansion. His growth-over-profit framing has aged unevenly.
