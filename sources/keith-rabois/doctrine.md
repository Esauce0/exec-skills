# Keith Rabois

## Why this source matters here
Rabois was an executive at PayPal and LinkedIn and COO of Square, then became an investor (Founders Fund, Khosla Ventures). His best-known operating doctrine comes from one lecture: "How to Operate," Lecture 14 of Stanford CS183B "How to Start a Startup" (2014). He was speaking to startup founders about hypergrowth companies. For this user, the lecture works two ways. It shows how senior leaders decide who gets more scope (barrels), and it describes what the job becomes once you have that scope (editing, triage, allocating time). Both views are written for startups and for a founder or CEO audience, so they need translating for a larger company.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. A company's output is limited by its barrels, not its headcount
- **Claim:** Most people, even great people, are "ammunition." What limits how fast a company moves is the number of "barrels": people who can take an idea from conception all the way through shipping and bring people with them. Adding ammunition without barrels often reduces how much gets done. Barrels are rare, hard to replace and specific to a culture: a barrel at one company may not be one at another. Keep the ones you have, with equity, promotion and attention.
- **Source:** CS183B Lecture 14 (transcript). In the 2026 Lenny's episode Rabois reportedly said PayPal had roughly 12 to 17 barrels among about 254 people. That figure comes from a secondary summary, so its attribution is uncertain.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach reframes the user's promotion case. The question senior leaders are asking is "can we fire an initiative through this person?", not "is this person excellent?" It gathers barrel evidence: initiatives the user took from ambiguous idea to shipped outcome, with people from other functions who joined without being told to.
- **Boundary conditions:** Being a barrel at the current company does not carry over automatically. In large companies, "bringing people with you" depends much more on persuading peers and navigating process (see Hill, Larson) than it did in early Square or PayPal.

### D2. Leaders find barrels by expanding scope until it breaks, and by watching who others go to
- **Claim:** Give people a small, even trivial, task. If they deliver cleanly, give them something more consequential, and keep going until you find where it breaks. It breaks for everyone, and that point shows the level of complexity the person can handle. Some people with unexpected backgrounds handle a great deal. A second signal: notice who colleagues go to for help, especially people who don't report to them. Promote those people and give them more opportunity quickly.
- **Source:** CS183B Lecture 14 (smoothie story from Square; the "who goes to whose desk" signal).
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach tells the user to make this test easy to run on them. Deliver small asks completely and without follow-up, then ask for the next, larger scope explicitly. It also checks the second signal: do people outside the user's reporting line bring them problems? If not, the coach works on becoming a resource across teams, not on more visibility.
- **Boundary conditions:** In large companies scope usually expands through formal planning cycles, so the user may need a sponsor to create the next increment. And "it breaks for everybody": hitting a ceiling is a data point, not a verdict, unless the ceiling stays put.

### D3. The executive's job is editing: simplify, clarify, keep one voice
- **Claim:** An editor is the best metaphor for the job. The main task is to simplify, which usually means cutting things. Reduce the team's agenda to one, two or three things in a framework people can repeat without thinking. Reject the excuse that the business is too complex. Editors also ask clarifying questions, often basic ones, so everyone understands the goal. And they keep a consistent voice across everything the company says and ships.
- **Source:** CS183B Lecture 14.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user's strategy, roadmap or update lists many priorities, the coach cuts it to three or fewer and tests whether a team member could repeat it from memory. In meetings, the coach encourages the user to ask the simple clarifying question others avoid. That is a mark of editorial presence, not of ignorance.
- **Boundary conditions:** Taking things out needs the standing to do so. Before the user has the authority, simplifying someone else's plan should be offered as a proposal to its owner.

### D4. Delegate by conviction and consequence; overruling spends social capital
- **Claim:** Place each decision on two axes: how sure you are, and how much it matters if it goes wrong. If you are unsure and the stakes are low, delegate fully and let people learn from mistakes. If you are very sure and the stakes are catastrophic, don't let a junior colleague make the mistake. Explain your reasoning. Rabois describes a talented LinkedIn colleague whose line "okay, you're the boss" told him each override was costing social capital.
- **Source:** CS183B Lecture 14.
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user is tempted to override a report or a peer-led team, the coach asks for their conviction level and the consequence level, and treats an override as spending a limited budget. It also warns the user to watch for "you're the boss" responses as a sign of that cost.
- **Boundary conditions:** The middle cases (moderate conviction, moderate stakes) call for judgment. The grid does not settle them. For a PM without reports, the same grid applies to how hard to push on decisions that belong to peers.

### D5. Triage like an ER; audit your calendar against your stated priorities
- **Claim:** An operator's day is triage: tell apart the "colds" that will go away from the problems that look like colds but can be fatal. Measure outputs, not inputs, and pair each metric with a counter-metric (fraud rate with false-positive rate) so teams don't optimize one at the expense of another. Write down your top priorities, then check them against your calendar. They almost never match. Recruiting, for example, is often named the top priority but rarely gets the most time.
- **Source:** CS183B Lecture 14.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach runs a calendar audit on the user's stated priorities (including "build relationships with VP X" or "hire senior PMs") and treats a mismatch as the first finding. For AI product metrics, it adds a paired counter-metric, for example adoption paired with a quality or safety rate.
- **Boundary conditions:** In large companies much of the calendar is imposed. The audit then shows where the user's discretionary time goes.

### D6. People keep their roles only while their learning curve matches the company's growth
- **Claim:** Every company and every individual has its own growth rate. Track each person's personal slope against the company's. People can stay in roles only while their learning keeps pace.
- **Source:** CS183B Lecture 14.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach frames the user's development as a race between their learning slope and the growth of the scope they want. It asks what the user learned this quarter that they could not have done last quarter.
- **Boundary conditions:** Hypergrowth framing. In slower-growing companies the more common constraint is openings, not learning.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Barrel evidence file** (for the user's promotion case): for each initiative, record (1) where it started (whose idea, how ambiguous); (2) what the user did to get it shipped; (3) who joined without being assigned; (4) the outcome; (5) what broke and how the user recovered.

**Scope ladder request:** close the current scope completely, then ask the sponsor for the next, larger increment, naming a specific larger initiative. Repeat.

**Editing pass:** cut the plan to three or fewer priorities; remove or merge anything a team member couldn't repeat; ask one basic clarifying question in each review.

**Conviction × consequence grid:** low/low means delegate fully; high/high means decide and explain why; the middle cases get an explicit note on the call and the social capital spent.

**Calendar audit:** rank priorities, total the last four weeks of hours by priority, and close the biggest gap first.

## Diagnostic questions
- Which initiatives can the user show that went from idea to shipped with people who weren't assigned to them?
- Do people outside the user's reporting line bring them problems?
- When was the user's scope last increased, and did they ask for it?
- Could the user's team recite the user's top three priorities?
- Does the user's calendar match their stated priorities?

## Observable signals
- Positive: small asks closed completely without follow-up; explicitly asks for larger scope; colleagues from other teams seek the user out; plans cut to a few items people can repeat; explains reasoning when overruling; pairs metrics with counter-metrics.
- Negative: many initiatives started and few shipped; needs other people to carry things over the line; long priority lists; overrules without explaining; calendar dominated by other people's agendas.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Treating "barrel" as a personality type instead of a demonstrated track record in a specific context.
- Pushing scope expansion onto reports without support, then reading the breaking point as a moral failure.
- Using "simplify" to erase complexity that matters, such as AI safety, compliance or data constraints.
- Taking Rabois's investor-era positions (for example, that the PM role is dying, or that talking to customers harms consumer products, both from the 2026 podcast topic list) as operating doctrine for a PM inside a company.

## Tensions with other sources
- **Cagan:** Cagan puts empowered product teams and discovery at the center. Rabois's later investor commentary downgrades the PM role and customer conversations for consumer products. Cagan fits product organizations inside a company; Rabois fits founder-led consumer bets.
- **Lencioni:** A barrel who is hungry but not humble becomes Lencioni's bulldozer or politician. Use Lencioni's model to check how the user's drive comes across to peers.
- **Hill:** Rabois's editor overrides decisions when conviction is high and stakes are high. Hill stresses commitment over compliance and shared power. They reconcile through Rabois's own advice to explain your reasoning and to delegate low-stakes calls.
- **Grove:** His idea of task-relevant maturity lines up with the conviction × consequence grid. Both set how involved a manager should be by the situation, not by personal style.
- **McCord:** They agree on talent density and on keeping your best people.

## Limits and biases
The source is one 2014 lecture, supplemented by later podcast material (only partly accessible). It reflects PayPal and Square in hypergrowth and speaks to founders and CEOs. The quotes come from auto-transcribed video, so wording is approximate. Rabois is a polarizing public figure, and his later views are investor opinions, not operating research. Survivorship bias: the lessons come from outlier successes.
