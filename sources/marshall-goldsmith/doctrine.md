# Marshall Goldsmith

## Why this source matters here
Goldsmith is an executive coach who mostly works with CEOs and senior executives of large companies. In his model he is paid only if the leader's stakeholders report that the leader has improved. His subject is the opposite of a capability gap. The leader is already good, and behaviors that success has reinforced (winning arguments, improving every idea, being the smartest person in the room) become the limit. That fits a strong PM whose reputation rests on being right. His habit list was built from senior-executive feedback, so at PM and Director level it needs calibrating: some "adding value" is still the job.

## Operational doctrine
### D1. The higher you go, the more your problems are behavioral, and success hides them from you
- **Claim:** Near the top, everyone is technically competent, so interpersonal behavior is what separates people. Success produces beliefs that resist change: I have succeeded, I can succeed, I will succeed, I choose to succeed. Goldsmith reports that 80-85% of his program participants rate themselves in the top 20% of their peers. Leaders also fall into a "superstition trap": they confuse behaviors they succeeded because of with behaviors they succeeded in spite of. And the higher people rise, the more their staff tell them how wonderful they are.
- **Source:** What Got You Here, Part 1; "The Success Delusion".
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user credits a trait for their success ("I'm blunt, that's why we ship"), the coach runs an in-spite-of test: which outcomes would have come faster or at lower cost without that behavior? For attribution analysis, the coach considers a behavior or perception problem before assuming a capability gap, especially when the user's feedback is about how they get results rather than whether they get them.
- **Boundary conditions:** At PM and new-Director level, real skill gaps are common (strategy, finance, managing managers). Not every stall is behavioral.

### D2. Screen for the twenty habits, most of which are strengths overused
- **Claim:** The habits are: winning too much; adding too much value; passing judgment; making destructive comments; starting with "no," "but," or "however"; telling the world how smart we are; speaking when angry; negativity ("let me explain why that won't work"); withholding information; failing to give proper recognition; claiming credit we don't deserve; making excuses; clinging to the past; playing favorites; refusing to express regret; not listening; failing to express gratitude; punishing the messenger; passing the buck; and an excessive need to be "me". Goal obsession is treated as a 21st habit because it is not a flaw in itself but produces flaws: people pursue a target at the expense of the larger purpose. Goldsmith frames these as everyday interpersonal behaviors, not personality defects, and so they can be changed.
- **Source:** What Got You Here, Part 2.
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach checks the user's meeting behavior and written comments against the list, requiring evidence for each habit, and picks one or two to work on, not twenty. For a high-performing PM the usual cluster is winning too much, adding too much value, starting with "but", telling the world how smart we are, not listening, and claiming credit. Goal obsession shows up as hitting the launch date while burning peer trust.
- **Boundary conditions:** The list comes from coaching experience, not a validated instrument. Several habits (withholding information, claiming credit) overlap with political behavior that Pfeffer treats as rational. Judge them by their effect on trust, not by the label.

### D3. Adding value has a commitment cost, and the cost rises with rank
- **Claim:** When a leader improves someone else's idea, they may raise its quality a little while sharply lowering the owner's commitment to carrying it out. Goldsmith's example is roughly 5% better content for 50% less commitment. Seniority makes this worse: a senior person's casual suggestion is heard as an order. His practice is to pause before speaking and ask whether the comment is worth it.
- **Source:** "Stop Adding Too Much Value"; What Got You Here (habit 2).
- **Changes coaching how:** In reviews of the team's PRDs, designs and plans, the coach tells the user to raise only material points, to ask questions instead of rewriting, and to leave ownership where it is. In executive meetings, the user's "one more idea" on a peer's proposal is read as meeting-presence cost, not contribution.
- **Boundary conditions:** When a flaw is material (customer harm, legal or safety exposure, a wrong decision), add the value. Junior teams may need more direct input than senior ones.

### D4. Changing behavior is not enough; you also have to change perception
- **Claim:** People's perception lags behind real behavior change. Goldsmith is often quoted as saying you have to improve 100% to get 10% of the credit (secondary attribution). His process is: collect feedback; apologize briefly ("I'm sorry, I'll try to do better") and then stop talking; advertise the specific behavior you are working on; listen; thank people; follow up monthly for 12 to 18 months; and use feedforward. The evidence he cites comes from a study of 8 companies, 11,480 managers and 86,000+ co-worker responses. Leaders who followed up regularly with co-workers were seen as improving much more. Those who did little follow-up showed improvement barely above chance. Whether the leader had a coach, and which program they attended, mattered much less than follow-up.
- **Source:** What Got You Here, Part 3; "Leadership Is a Contact Sport" (2004).
- **Changes coaching how:** When the user has changed but their reputation has not, the coach does not declare success. It prescribes naming the change to specific stakeholders and running short monthly check-ins. The trajectory model tracks behavior and perception as separate series.
- **Boundary conditions:** The study is correlational, with self-selected follow-up and co-worker ratings. Advertising a flaw requires a boss and peers who will not use it against you. In a hostile political environment, ask a few trusted stakeholders privately instead.

### D5. Feedforward: ask for ideas about the future, and reply only "thank you"
- **Claim:** Pick one behavior. Ask many people for one or two ideas for the future. No feedback about the past, and no judging or debating the ideas. The only reply is "thank you." Goldsmith gives these reasons it works: the future can be changed and the past cannot; successful people resist negative judgment but welcome ideas; anyone can give suggestions without knowing you well; it is taken less personally; it is fast; it works with bosses and peers because it does not imply superior judgment; and people listen better when they do not have to compose a clever reply.
- **Source:** "Try Feedforward Instead of Feedback"; "Using Feed Forward for Development".
- **Changes coaching how:** The coach makes feedforward the user's default way of asking a boss, skip-level or peer for development input ("I'm working on X; what are two ideas for me?"). It is an easier and safer request upward than "give me feedback." The user can also offer input to senior people as feedforward.
- **Boundary conditions:** Feedforward is for changing behavior. It does not replace diagnosing a decision after the fact (see Duke) or formal performance feedback.

### D6. Stakeholders define success, and some people should not be coached
- **Claim:** In Stakeholder Centered Coaching, the leader chooses one or two behaviors with input from the boss, names the stakeholders, checks in with them briefly each month, and is measured by stakeholder mini-surveys rating perceived change. The coach is paid only if stakeholders report improvement. Goldsmith declines leaders with integrity violations, leaders who do not want to change, and leaders the organization has already written off.
- **Source:** Stakeholder Centered Coaching materials; "Coaching for Behavioral Change" (by reference).
- **Changes coaching how:** For any behavioral goal, the coach's measure of success is the change named stakeholders perceive over months, not the user's own report. The coach also triages: if the organization has written the user off, behavior coaching is not the lever, and the conversation turns to sponsorship or moving on.
- **Boundary conditions:** This depends on stakeholders who will answer honestly. Small or highly political groups can skew the mini-survey.

## Frameworks in operational form
**Habit screen for a senior PM** (pick at most two):
| Habit | What it looks like in PM work | Replacement behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Winning too much | Relitigates small decisions to be right | Concede non-material points out loud |
| Adding too much value | Rewrites the team's docs; tops up every peer idea | "Is it worth it?"; ask a question instead |
| Starting with no/but/however | "Great, but..." in reviews | "And..." or a clarifying question |
| Telling the world how smart we are | "I already knew that"; long displays of context | Say only what moves the decision |
| Withholding information | Hoards exec context to stay central | Share context with the team by default |
| Claiming credit / failing to recognize | "My launch"; no named credit for contributors | Name the contributors in exec updates |
| Not listening | Composes a rebuttal while others talk | Paraphrase before replying |
| Goal obsession | Hits the date, burns peer trust | Check the goal against the larger purpose |

**Change cycle** (12-18 months): choose the behavior with the boss, apologize once, advertise it to 5-10 stakeholders, ask each monthly for one or two feedforward ideas, say thank you, act on something visible, and run a short perception check at about months 5 and 11.

**Before speaking in a meeting:** Is it worth it? Does it change the decision? Whose ownership does it take away?

## Diagnostic questions
- Which of the user's current behaviors got them promoted, and which of those are now costing them?
- In the last three meetings, how often did the user add to a peer's idea compared with asking about it?
- Who would say the user takes credit or holds back context?
- Has the user told any stakeholder what they are working on changing?
- Is the reputation problem current behavior, or perception lagging behind a change already made?
- Has the organization already written the user off? If so, stop coaching behavior and address sponsorship or exit.

## Observable signals
- Positive: lets a subordinate's plan go ahead with small flaws; names contributors in senior forums; asks one question where they used to give three opinions; tells stakeholders what they are working on and checks back monthly; says "thank you" to input without arguing.
- Negative: every review comes back heavily edited; "yes, but" as a reflex; restates others' points as their own; debates feedback on the spot; believes one good quarter has reset their reputation.

## Anti-patterns and misuse
- Turning the list into a vague "be nicer" instruction instead of choosing one observable behavior.
- Using "don't add value" as an excuse to disengage from quality.
- Advertising a change but never following up, which reads as performance.
- Using feedforward to avoid post-mortems on real decisions.
- Applying "don't claim credit" to a user who already under-claims. Helgesen and Goldsmith note that women's derailers often run the other way (reluctance to claim achievements).

## Tensions with other sources
- **Pfeffer:** Pfeffer says self-promotion and visible credit build power, and that modesty is overrated. Goldsmith treats claiming credit and showing off as derailers. Make contributions visible upward (Pfeffer) and give credit to peers and the team (Goldsmith). The problem is taking credit that isn't yours, not visibility.
- **Scott (Radical Candor):** Scott advocates direct criticism. Goldsmith says criticism is almost always taken personally and prefers future-oriented ideas. Scott fits a manager giving work feedback to a report. Goldsmith fits development input to successful people and upward.
- **Duke:** Duke requires honest analysis of past decisions, while feedforward bans discussing the past. They apply to different things: decisions need post-mortems, and behaviors need suggestions about the future.
- **Bezos/Amazon:** "Are Right, A Lot" and "Have Backbone" can reward winning debates. Amazon's "Earn Trust" also asks leaders to be vocally self-critical. Winning is justified on one-way-door decisions and costly on everything else.
- **Horowitz, Slootman:** They model forceful, decisive leaders, which can look like "winning too much." Forcefulness is right in a crisis. Goldsmith's habits matter most in steady-state peer settings where commitment has to be voluntary.

## Limits and biases
Practitioner evidence: coaching anecdotes, plus a correlational follow-up study in which co-workers did the rating. His clients are mainly senior leaders in large US corporations. "Pay for results" is measured by stakeholders' perception, which rewards change that is visible over change that is structural. The framing assumes the problem is in the individual and says little about incentives or power. The original habit list does not account for gender or identity; How Women Rise partly corrects this.
