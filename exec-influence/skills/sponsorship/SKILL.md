# Sponsorship

## Purpose

People get promoted into bigger roles because someone with power argues for them in rooms they aren't in. That person is a sponsor, and what separates a sponsor from a mentor who gives advice is that the sponsor spends political capital. Most people overcount their sponsors, and most promotion surprises trace back to that miscount.

This skill makes the sponsor portfolio honest and designs how to build it legitimately: by delivering for people with power, being trustworthy, bringing something they lack, and making ambitions explicit.

## Use when

- "Who's going to advocate for me?" / "My VP is my sponsor."
- Before a promotion cycle or an open role.
- A sponsor leaves, loses standing, or gets a new boss.
- The user wants a senior leader's attention.
- `attribution-analysis` names a sponsorship gap.

## Don't use when

- The question is how to work with the direct manager day to day (use `managing-up`), though the manager is usually the first sponsor candidate.

## The ladder

Each level requires the evidence named. Friendliness and praise are not evidence of anything above ally.

| Level | What they do | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Contact | Knows who the user is | Has interacted with the user's work |
| Ally | Helps when asked; positive in shared forums | Observed help or support |
| Mentor | Private advice and insider guidance on how to advance | Advice given, especially about how things really work here |
| Connector | Introduces the user; talks them up to peers | A dated introduction or reported praise to others |
| Opportunity-giver | Gives high-visibility assignments or rooms | A dated assignment, invitation, or seat |
| Sponsor | Stakes their own reputation in promotion, role, or succession decisions; spends capital; provides air cover | At least one dated act of advocacy in a decision, or defense of the user when it cost them something |

The ladder is this system's synthesis of Hewlett's sponsor/mentor distinction and Ibarra's spectrum (mentor, strategizer, connector, opportunity giver, advocate). Strategizer is folded into mentor, and advocate is called sponsor.

## Standards

Everything this skill produces follows the three exec-core standards. If exec-core isn't loaded, apply them as stated here:
- **Evidence:** separate what happened from inference; label any claim about another person's view or motive as a hypothesis with confidence and a falsifier; never state it as fact.
- **Posture:** when the user's behavior is part of the problem, fix the behavior before the wording.
- **Prose:** answer first, recommendation over options, owners and dates, no filler.

## Core doctrine

1. **Mentors advise; sponsors spend capital** [Hewlett]. In Hewlett's research, many more senior people describe themselves as sponsors than have actually spent capital for anyone. Assume the user has fewer real sponsors than they think.
2. **Sponsorship drives promotion** [Ibarra; Hewlett]. Mentoring from people without clout does little. The question is what the most powerful person who knows the user's work has actually done for them.
3. **It's a two-way alliance: performance, trustworthiness, distinct value** [Hewlett]. Sponsors back people who deliver outcomes they've personally seen, who are reliable and discreet, and who bring something the sponsor lacks. A weak leg is the next development priority.
4. **Look for clout and line of sight, usually beyond the boss** [Hewlett]. Clout means a seat in talent reviews, control of roles or budget. Line of sight means they can see the user's work directly.
5. **Hold several sponsors across power centers** [Hewlett]. Aim for two or three, in different chains, one ideally outside the company. A single sponsor is a single point of failure, especially if it's the manager.
6. **You can't simply ask; get into their line of sight** [Hewlett; Ibarra]. Volunteer for the review they chair, deliver a crisp result, follow up tied to their goals, ask for one piece of feedback, act on it, report back. Treat the first small assignment as a test.
7. **Frame everything around the sponsor's goals** [Hewlett; Maister]. Sponsors gain too: results they can point to, loyalty, information, problems solved. Visible self-orientation kills sponsorship.
8. **Make the ambition explicit, once trust exists** [Scott; Hewlett; Pfeffer]. Nobody sponsors a direction they don't know. After delivering, say what you want next and ask what they'd need to see.
9. **Sponsors believe in people beyond current evidence** [Campbell]. When a senior leader expresses belief beyond what the user has shown, find out exactly what they're betting on, and deliver against it.
10. **Sponsorship skews toward people like the sponsor** [Hewlett; Ibarra]. If the user belongs to a group under-sponsored in their company, record part of the gap as context, not capability, and compensate with deliberate exposure.
11. **Would your manager fight to keep you?** [McCord, inverted]. Look for behavior: do they put the user's name forward for high-stakes work, fight for their compensation, protect their time?

## Evidence to gather

- `models/sponsorship.md` and the Trust signals section of each senior person's file (dated acts).
- Evidence entries of type `allocation`, and any reported advocacy (`reported-by` tags).
- `models/visibility.md`: who has actually seen the user's work.
- `self/level-model.md § Revealed ladder`: who sponsored the people recently promoted.

## Reasoning procedure

1. **Inventory** every senior relationship from `people/` and `models/sponsorship.md`. Place each on the ladder from evidence only. Anyone with "unknown" on the advocacy evidence is not yet a sponsor.
2. **Map the portfolio** (`references/sponsor-portfolio.md`): clout, line of sight, what they gain from the user's work, the user's distinct value to them, trust evidence, last substantive contact.
3. **Check concentration:** how many real sponsors, in how many chains? What happens if the main one leaves?
4. **Audit the three legs** for the top two candidates: performance they've personally seen, trust evidence, distinct value.
5. **Pick the next step up the ladder** for each target and what would earn it.
6. **Design exposure** that's legitimate: a real work reason to be in their line of sight, around their goals.
7. **Plan the explicit conversation** for anyone at opportunity-giver or above who doesn't yet know the user's goal.
8. **Reciprocate:** what the user will give (results, information, taking work off their plate, loyalty in conduct).

## Diagnostic questions

- Who is the most powerful person who knows your work well, and what have they done (not said) for you?
- Who would name you in a talent review without being prompted?
- What has each potential sponsor personally seen you deliver in the last two quarters?
- What do you bring that they lack?
- Do they know what you want next?
- If your main sponsor left tomorrow, who else would speak for you?

## Failure modes

- Counting mentors and friendly executives as sponsors.
- Asking for sponsorship before delivering anything the sponsor cares about.
- A single-sponsor portfolio, especially the manager alone.
- Being seen as someone's "person" in a factional organization, then losing when the faction does.
- Treating sponsors as transactions; sponsorship follows trust.
- Never stating what the user wants.

## Output

```
Real sponsors: <names, with the act of advocacy that qualifies them> (or "none yet").

Next moves: <1-3 specific exposures or conversations, framed around their goals>.

```

## Tensions in the doctrine

- **Loyalty vs portfolio.** Campbell's loyalty ethic can read as "stay with your sponsor." Hewlett and McCord favor multiple sponsors and owning your own career. Loyalty in conduct toward each sponsor is compatible with having several.
- **Earn vs ask.** Hewlett: you can't ask someone cold to sponsor you. Pfeffer: people comply with explicit asks more than you'd predict. Sequence: earn first through delivery in their line of sight, then ask explicitly.
- **Merit vs affinity.** Sponsorship follows performance and trust, and also follows similarity. The coach states both when diagnosing a gap.
