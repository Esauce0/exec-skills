# The boss relationship as a system

## Six-dimension diagnosis

| Dimension | Healthy | Unhealthy signal |
|---|---|---|
| Shared diagnosis | You and the manager describe the situation the same way | They're solving a different problem than you are |
| Explicit expectations | You could write down what success looks like this half, and they'd agree | Surprises at review time |
| Information flow | They hear bad news from you first, early | They hear things from others; they ask "why didn't I know?" |
| Style fit | Format and cadence match how they take in information | Long docs to a listener; hallway updates to a reader |
| Dependability | They don't add buffer to your dates or chase follow-ups | They double-check your work or your claims |
| Development | They know your goal and what evidence they'd need to back it | They've never discussed your next step |

## The five conversations (after Watkins)

Run them explicitly with a new manager or in a new role, and revisit twice a year.

1. **Situation:** "Here's how I read the state of my area. Where do you see it differently?" Agree on the diagnosis before proposing strategy.
2. **Expectations:** "What does success look like at six months? What would disappoint you? What's non-negotiable?"
3. **Style:** "How do you want to hear from me, how often, and about what? What should I decide without you? What do you want to be consulted on?"
4. **Resources:** "Here's what I need to deliver that. Here's what I'd trade if I can't have it."
5. **Development:** "I'd like to be operating as a Director within a year. What would you need to see to back that? Who else would need to see it?"

## Expectations memo

One page, written by the user, reviewed with the manager:

```
What I believe is expected of me this half: <3-5 outcomes>.
What I'll decide on my own: <list>.
What I'll bring to you for a decision: <list>.
What I'll keep you informed on, and how: <cadence and format>.
What I'm working toward next, and the evidence we agreed would show it: <...>.
```

## 1:1 structure (after Campbell, Grove, Horowitz)

1. Before: up to five topics, ranked. Across a month, cover performance, peers, people, and improvements alongside delivery.
2. Brief personal check-in. Don't skip it.
3. Ask for their list. If the lists conflict, their first item goes first.
4. Bring problems early, with a proposed response.
5. Close with what you'll do by when.
6. Open the next 1:1 by reporting on it.

## Upward candor sequence (after Scott)

1. Solicit feedback on yourself first, about a specific recent event. Tolerate the silence and listen to understand, then act on it visibly or explain why not.
2. Give specific, sincere praise when it's deserved.
3. Ask permission: "Can I give you a thought about how the planning meeting landed?"
4. Raise one small, specific item, privately.
5. Watch what happens. Escalate to bigger items only if the first landed well.

## Difficult-manager time box (after McCord)

1. Ask for a 1:1 framed around being a more effective team.
2. Speak for yourself, and propose one fix per problem.
3. Write down what's working and what isn't. Set a checkpoint about a month out.
4. At the checkpoint: improving, continue; unchanged, start a quiet external or internal search, without drama.

Record each step as evidence entries so the observer can tell whether the relationship is actually improving.

## Skip-level protocol

- Tell the manager you're meeting their boss, and share the topics.
- Bring the skip-level something useful: an insight from the front line, a clear read on a risk, a question about direction.
- Never use it to complain about the manager or relitigate their decisions.
- Afterwards, tell the manager what you learned that's relevant to them.
