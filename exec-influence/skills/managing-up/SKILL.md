# Managing Up

## Purpose

The manager is the single most consequential relationship in most careers: they allocate much of the user's scope, shape how the user is described in calibration, and control access to their own boss. The relationship is one of mutual dependence between two fallible people, and the subordinate carries the burden of managing it, because the subordinate pays the cost when it fails.

This skill treats the boss relationship as a system the user designs and maintains.

## Use when

- Preparing a 1:1 or a significant conversation with the manager.
- The relationship feels off: friction, silence, surprises, micromanagement.
- A new manager arrives, or the user starts a new role.
- Before asking the manager for scope, a promotion conversation, resources, or support.
- Engaging the skip-level or other executives without undermining the manager.

## Don't use when

- The question is about a specific executive's trust tier (use `executive-trust`; this skill uses it).

## Standards

Everything this skill produces follows the three exec-core standards. If exec-core isn't loaded, apply them as stated here:
- **Evidence:** separate what happened from inference; label any claim about another person's view or motive as a hypothesis with confidence and a falsifier; never state it as fact.
- **Posture:** when the user's behavior is part of the problem, fix the behavior before the wording.
- **Prose:** answer first, recommendation over options, owners and dates, no filler.

## Core doctrine

1. **Mutual dependence; the subordinate manages it** [Gabarro & Kotter]. "My boss is bad at X" can be true and still leaves the user responsible for compensating, because the cost lands on the user.
2. **Model the boss explicitly** [Gabarro & Kotter; Pfeffer]. Write down their goals this quarter, what their own boss and peers are pressing them on, what they personally have at stake, their strengths and blind spots. A plan that's right on the merits but wrong on the boss's time horizon gets rejected.
3. **Fit their intake style** [Gabarro & Kotter, crediting Drucker]. Give a reader the memo before discussing it; give a listener the conversation first and the memo after. Also find out whether they're high-involvement or a delegator, and design the cadence and format from the answers.
4. **Negotiate expectations explicitly** [Watkins; Gabarro & Kotter]. Five conversations: situation (do we agree on the diagnosis?), expectations (what does success look like, by when?), style (how and how often do we communicate?), resources (what do I need?), and personal development (what evidence would you need to see to back my next step?). The burden of surfacing expectations sits with the subordinate.
5. **The 1:1 is the subordinate's meeting** [Grove; Horowitz; Campbell]. Bring a ranked agenda. Across a month, cover performance, peers, people, and improvements as well as delivery. Close with what you'll do by when, and open the next one by reporting it; that's how coachability becomes visible.
6. **Authority is on loan; no surprises** [Larson; Gabarro & Kotter]. Treat any surprise to the manager as an incident. Assume you under-inform, and bring problems early. A long run of all-green updates is a risk signal.
7. **Can your manager state your top contributions in their own words?** [Pfeffer]. The manager's perception is what gets carried into rooms the user isn't in. Frame updates in the manager's priorities, not the user's task list.
8. **Make ambitions explicit** [Scott; Hewlett]. A manager can't sponsor a direction they don't know. Volunteer the 18-month goal and ask what evidence they'd need.
9. **Candor upward is sequenced** [Scott]. Solicit feedback first, praise specifically, ask permission, raise one small item privately, watch the reaction, escalate only if it landed well.
10. **Disagree well, escalate cleanly** [Hughes Johnson; Campbell]. Disagree in private, with evidence, once. Co-write the disagreement with the other party when escalating. Then commit.
11. **Spend the boss's time and capital selectively** [Gabarro & Kotter]. Every request should serve the important goals of the user, the boss, and the company.
12. **When it isn't working, time-box it** [McCord]. Have one direct conversation framed around team effectiveness, keep written notes, set a dated checkpoint, and agree beforehand on what gets decided there.

## Evidence to gather

- The manager's `people/` file: accountabilities, pressures, intake style, open loops.
- `self/current-role.md`: mandate, decision rights, how the user is measured, what the manager is measured on.
- The last four to six weeks of 1:1 notes and evidence entries naming the manager.
- `self/ambitions.md` and `commitments.md`.
- The skip-level's person file, if skip-level contact is in play.

## Reasoning procedure

1. **Load the boss model:** their `people/` file (accountabilities, stated and observed priorities, pressures, intake style, trust signals, open loops) and `self/current-role.md § What my manager is measured on`. Mark every field as evidenced or assumed.
2. **Diagnose the relationship** on the six dimensions in `references/boss-system.md`: shared diagnosis, explicit expectations, information flow, style fit, dependability, and development.
3. **Identify the user's authority reflex** from evidence (Gabarro & Kotter): counterdependent (fights the boss, wins arguments, loses outcomes) or overdependent (defers, never dissents). Each has a different fix.
4. **Find the highest-leverage gap.** Usually one of: no shared diagnosis, implicit expectations, the manager being surprised, updates framed in the user's terms, ambitions never stated.
5. **Design the move:** the 1:1 agenda, the expectations memo, the development conversation, or the repair.
6. **Skip-level and exec contact:** plan it so the manager is never surprised and ideally benefits (share the skip-level agenda with the manager; bring back what you learned; never use it to route around them).

## Diagnostic questions

- What is your manager's top goal this quarter, and what is their boss pressing them on?
- Could you write the expectations memo today and have them sign it without edits?
- When did they last hear about a problem in your area from someone else first?
- Reader or listener? Delegator or high-involvement? What's the evidence?
- Do they know what role you want next, and what evidence they'd need to back it?
- Who brings the agenda to your 1:1s?

## Failure modes

- Treating the manager as the audience for problems rather than for decisions and results.
- Waiting for the manager to define expectations or offer development.
- Flattery or telling the manager what they want to hear (Scott's manipulative insincerity; Pfeffer notes it can work short term, but it fails the legitimacy filter and corrodes over time).
- Going around the manager to their boss.
- Letting a bad relationship run for a year without a direct conversation.
- Venting about the manager to peers (it always travels).

## Output

```
Relationship read: <state in one or two sentences, with confidence>.
Weakest dimension: <dimension>. Evidence: <...>.
What your manager needs from you right now: <based on their pressures, with evidence>.
The move: <agenda, memo, conversation, or change in cadence>, with the opening lines.
What to stop: <behavior>.
```

## Tensions in the doctrine

- **Candor vs caution upward.** Scott says executives are starved of accurate feedback and a subordinate who can give it becomes valuable. Pfeffer finds that making superiors feel good about themselves works and treats blunt truth-telling upward as a career risk. Sequence and test: start with something small, private, and specific, then watch the reaction.
- **Loyalty vs your own path.** Campbell prizes loyalty; McCord says careers belong to the employee, and a bad manager situation should be time-boxed and exited without drama. The user can be loyal in conduct and still clear about their own interests.
- **Know yourself first vs act first.** Hughes Johnson recommends working-with-me documents (and asking for the manager's). Ibarra prefers learning through action. For a new manager relationship, the documents are cheap and pay off quickly; do both.
