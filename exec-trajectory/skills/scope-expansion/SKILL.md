# Scope Expansion

## Purpose

Scope is what senior leaders allocate when they trust someone: ambiguity, people, money, strategic importance, organizational risk. Growing it legitimately is the most direct path to the next level, because it creates the evidence a promotion case needs.

Growing it illegitimately (grabbing, empire-building, taking over working things, stepping on a boss or peer) is the fastest way to lose the trust that makes scope possible. This skill finds the legitimate version and designs how to claim it.

## Use when

- The user wants more scope or is stuck in a role with no next-level opportunities.
- An unowned problem keeps coming up in executive forums.
- A reorg, departure, or new company priority creates an opening.
- Someone offers the user something new to own.
- `attribution-analysis` finds an opportunity gap.

## Don't use when

- The user is failing at their current scope. Fix the core first; taking on more while the base is shaky reads as poor judgment.

## Standards

Everything this skill produces follows the three exec-core standards. If exec-core isn't loaded, apply them as stated here:
- **Evidence:** separate what happened from inference; label any claim about another person's view or motive as a hypothesis with confidence and a falsifier; never state it as fact.
- **Posture:** when the user's behavior is part of the problem, fix the behavior before the wording.
- **Prose:** answer first, recommendation over options, owners and dates, no filler.

## Core doctrine

1. **Agenda setters get scope** [Watkins]. Organizational leaders decide which problems the org works on, including white space no function owns. Ask: what are the three issues the org should tackle in the next 12 months that nobody owns?
2. **Scope is granted in increments to people who close small asks completely, then ask explicitly** [Rabois; Pfeffer]. Leaders find barrels by expanding someone's scope until it breaks and by watching who others go to. In large companies the increments often come through planning cycles, so a sponsor may need to create the next one. People comply with explicit asks more often than they expect.
3. **Create resources from neglected, valued work** [Pfeffer]. The dashboard executives actually read, the cross-org forum nobody runs, the quarterly landscape memo for leadership: things others come to depend on.
4. **Own capability, not one more feature** [Ng]. For an AI PM: evaluation infrastructure, a model gateway, data standards, AI governance, adoption programs. "What would every AI team here need that no single team will build?"
5. **The job is a platform for leadership: be a bridge, not a hub** [Ibarra]. Scope that connects groups (research and go-to-market, legal and product, platform and business units) builds both leadership evidence and strategic networks.
6. **Choose where to work** [Pfeffer]. Units on the company's critical path gain power; showcase side projects don't. Check where budget, headcount, and board airtime are moving.
7. **Early wins must matter to the boss and advance the real agenda** [Watkins]. The easiest wins rarely qualify.
8. **Ambition for the company, not for yourself** [Horowitz; Lencioni; Maister]. Scope sought primarily for the user's benefit reads that way to peers, and ego-driven org proposals are recognized as such. Visible self-orientation lowers trust.
9. **Avoid snacking, preening, and chasing ghosts** [Larson]. Low-value easy work, high-visibility low-impact work, and projects built on a fantasy of what the org needs all look like scope and aren't.
10. **Protect the core** [Doshi; Charan]. New scope that degrades current delivery destroys the trust it depends on. Every expansion needs a plan for what gets delegated or dropped.

## Sources of legitimate scope

| Type | What it is | Typical risk |
|---|---|---|
| Vacuum | Important and unowned | Nobody owns it because it's hard or thankless; check why |
| Adjacency | Next to the user's area; capability transfers | Neighbor reads it as encroachment |
| Boss's problem | Something on the manager's plate the user can take | Manager may not want to let go; must be offered, not taken |
| Seam | Handoff between functions that keeps failing | Requires authority across teams the user doesn't have |
| New bet | A company initiative just starting | High uncertainty; may be killed |
| Turnaround | A failing area | High visibility, high risk; great evidence if it works |
| Inherited | Departure or reorg leaves an area open | Short window; politics around who gets it |

## Evidence to gather

- `self/current-role.md`, `models/scope.md`, `opportunities.md`, `models/readiness.md`.
- People files for the manager and skip-level (their priorities and overload).
- Product Brain `knowledge/strategy.md § Tensions`, org and team files, blocked decisions.
- Evidence of recurring executive frustrations, and `reorg` entries.

## Reasoning procedure

1. **Generate candidates** from the Career Brain (`opportunities.md`, evidence of recurring executive frustrations, reorg signals) and the Product Brains (strategy tensions, decisions blocked by missing owners, org/team files).
2. **Evaluate each** on the matrix in `references/opportunity-evaluation.md`: enterprise value, legitimacy, evidence value (which readiness dimension it demonstrates), visibility, risk, cost to current scope, and the user's capability to deliver.
3. **Apply the anti-empire test:** who benefits most if the user owns it? If the honest answer is the user, stop. If a current owner would be diminished, partner with that owner. Would the user be comfortable if every affected peer knew exactly why they want it?
4. **Diagnose the situation type** of the opportunity (Watkins' STARS: start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success). It changes what "early win" means.
5. **Map who can grant it and who can block it** (`organizational-politics`): the decider, the current owner if any, peers affected, and the action-forcing event (planning cycle, reorg announcement, launch gate).
6. **Design the ask.** Not "can I help with X?" but a one-page proposal: the problem in the company's terms, why now, what the user would do in the first 60 days, what they'd need, and what they'd hand off from current scope to make room. Pre-wire with the manager first; never surprise the boss with a scope proposal to their boss.
7. **Plan formalization.** Informal scope evaporates in reorgs. Target a named mention in a plan, OKR, or org announcement within a quarter.
8. **Record** in `opportunities.md` with status and next step.

## Diagnostic questions

- What three problems should the organization solve in the next year that nobody owns?
- What is your manager overloaded with?
- Who owns anything adjacent, and have you asked them what they want?
- What would you hand off to make room?
- Who can grant this, and what's the next forcing event?
- If you owned it, who would benefit most?

## Failure modes

- Land grabs: pursuing scope primarily for headcount or title.
- Taking over something that works, or that has an owner, without the owner's agreement.
- Accepting scope without resources, then failing publicly.
- Dropping the core; the current area slips while the user chases the new thing.
- Staying informal: doing the work for a year without it ever being named as the user's.
- Choosing visible but low-impact scope (preening), or easy but low-value scope (snacking).
- Asking too early, before delivering anything for the person who would grant it.

## Tensions in the doctrine

- **Take initiative vs team-first.** Rabois, Pfeffer, and Slootman favor taking responsibility and asking boldly. Campbell and Lencioni prioritize the team and peer trust; grabbing reads as self-serving. Resolution: take ownership of problems, not of people's territory; ask boldly for unowned things, partner on owned ones.
- **Powerful units vs where you can win.** Pfeffer says go where power is; Watkins and Ibarra emphasize where you can build leadership evidence. A critical-path unit where the user has no real role beats nothing, but loses to a smaller unit where they own an outcome.
- **Break conventions vs stay in lane.** Pfeffer treats "stay in your lane" as a convention to break when it blocks value, and this skill agrees; integrity rules (governance, security, honesty) are never conventions to break.
